Every year, students lose marks or face serious trouble for work they did not mean to copy. Usually the cause is not bad character. It is a lack of clear rules about how to use other people's work. This post gives you those rules in plain words.

## The short answer

Plagiarism means presenting someone else's words, ideas, data or figures as if they were your own. You avoid it by giving credit every time you use another person's work, and by writing your own thoughts in your own words.

- Put exact words in quotation marks and name the source.
- If you paraphrase, change the sentence structure and the words, then still name the source.
- Cite data, tables, charts and figures that you did not produce yourself.
- Follow the referencing style your department asks for.
- Keep notes of where every idea came from while you read, not after you finish writing.

## What counts as plagiarism?

The Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) explains it clearly in its student integrity handbook. Plagiarism is using another person's words, ideas, claims, data or figures without acknowledging where they came from. That covers more than copying a paragraph. It includes the following.

- Copying a sentence from a website, book, article or a classmate's assignment without quotation marks and a source.
- Rewording a passage by changing a few words, while keeping the same structure and ideas, and not citing it.
- Using someone else's idea or argument as if you thought of it.
- Taking a chart, table or numbers from another source without saying where they came from.

MIT also makes a point that surprises many students. Even when you put an idea fully into your own words, you still need to give credit for it. Your words are yours. The idea belongs to whoever had it first.

Note that your own ordinary thinking and widely known facts do not need a citation. For example, you do not need a source to say that Dodoma is the capital of Tanzania. If you are unsure whether something is common knowledge, ask your lecturer or cite it. Citing too much is a much smaller problem than citing too little.

## Should I quote, paraphrase or summarise?

You have three ways to use a source. Each has a job.

| Method | What you do | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quote | Copy the exact words in quotation marks and cite | When the wording itself matters, such as a definition or a law |
| Paraphrase | Explain one specific point fully in your own words and structure, and cite | When you need the detail but not the exact wording |
| Summarise | Give the main idea of a long source in a few sentences, and cite | When you need only the big picture |

Here is a made-up example to show the difference. The passage below does not come from a real book. It is written only for this lesson.

> Example source text (invented): "Small shops that record every sale in a notebook can see which products earn the most and can plan their buying more carefully."

**Copying (wrong):** Small shops that record every sale in a notebook can see which products earn the most and can plan their buying more carefully.

This is plagiarism. The words are the same and no source is given.

**Bad paraphrase (wrong):** Small shops which write each sale in a notebook can notice which goods earn the most and can plan their purchases more carefully.

This only swaps a few words. The structure is the same and there is no citation. It is still plagiarism.

**Good quote (correct):** According to Example Author (2020), "small shops that record every sale in a notebook can see which products earn the most" (p. 12).

**Good paraphrase (correct):** Keeping a sales notebook helps a shopkeeper learn which items bring in the most money, which makes it easier to decide what to stock (Example Author, 2020).

Example Author and the page number are invented. In your own work you would use the real author, year and page of the source you read.

A good habit for paraphrasing: read the passage, close the book, write the idea from memory, then check that you kept the meaning and did not copy the shape of the sentence.

## How do I cite, and why does my department's style matter?

A citation tells the reader where an idea came from so they can find it. Most citation styles have two parts:

1. A short mark in the text, such as an author and year, or a number.
2. A full entry in a reference list at the end, with the author, title, year and where to find it.

Styles such as APA and Harvard are common in universities around the world. They differ in the small details, for example where the year goes or how names are written. Your department decides which one you must use. Do not choose by yourself. Look in your course handbook or assignment brief, or ask your lecturer. Then use the same style for the whole assignment.

Tips that save time:

- Write the source details in your notes the moment you copy or summarise something. A link alone is not enough. Note the author, title, date, and page.
- Build your reference list as you write, not on the last night.
- Cite the source you actually read. If you only read a summary of a book, cite the summary.
- If you use a table or chart from somewhere else, cite it right under the picture.

## What do Turnitin and similarity scores mean?

Turnitin is software that compares a piece of writing with a large collection of other texts and shows which parts match. Tanzanian universities use tools like this. A 2024 study by Moshi Amsi Mislay and Ahadi Mzumbwe Anania of UDSM reports that the University of Dar es Salaam (UDSM) bought Turnitin in 2015 and that it tested 556 theses and dissertations from one academic unit between January 2016 and December 2021. The authors conclude that using Turnitin reduced the trend of plagiarism in those documents. They also note limits. For example, the software does not show plagiarism levels within each chapter.

In 2020, The Citizen newspaper reported that UDSM's vice-chancellor said postgraduate proposals are checked with Turnitin, that a proposal is revoked when plagiarism is found, and that some students have been discontinued for it.

A similarity score is a percentage of matching text. It is not a verdict. A high score can come from properly quoted text and a long reference list, and a low score does not prove that work is honest, because a paraphrase with no citation may not match anything. A person has to read the report and decide.

Do not look for a "safe percentage". The 2024 study mentions a 30 per cent tolerance level at the unit it studied, and its authors say that level seems high and should be reviewed. Each university and department sets its own rules. Ask yours how similarity reports are used. And never try to trick the software. Honest work does not need that, and universities treat deliberate deception as a serious offence.

## What if I am worried I copied by mistake?

It is a heavy feeling to realise you may have used a passage without credit. Take it step by step.

1. Stop and do not panic. If you have not submitted yet, you can still fix it.
2. Go through the assignment and compare each part with your notes and sources.
3. For every passage that is too close to the source, either put it in quotation marks with a citation, or rewrite it from your own understanding and cite it.
4. Add any missing entries to your reference list.
5. If you have already submitted and you are worried, speak to your lecturer or your department. Explaining honestly and early is usually better than waiting to be found.

We cannot tell you what a particular university will decide in your case. Penalties differ and the rules are in your student handbook. Read the academic integrity section now, before you need it.

## Common questions

### Do I need to cite my own earlier work?

Many universities expect you to say so if you reuse something you wrote for another course. Rules differ, so ask your lecturer before you reuse any of it.

### Is working with friends allowed?

Talking about ideas is usually fine and often helps. Handing in the same answers, or copying a friend's file, is not. If the assignment is individual, the words and analysis must be yours.

### Can I use AI tools to write my assignment?

Rules differ by university and by lecturer. Check your course handbook. If you are told to write your own work, submitting text you did not write yourself breaks that rule, and you stay responsible for every sentence you hand in.

### How many sources should I cite?

There is no magic number. Cite every source that you took facts, ideas, data or wording from. Your assignment brief may also set a minimum.

## What to do next

1. Find your department's referencing style in the course handbook and note its name.
2. Start a simple notes file with columns for the idea, the source and the page, and use it for every reading.
3. Practise once: take one paragraph you have read, close the book, paraphrase it from memory, and add the citation.
4. Read the academic integrity part of your university handbook this week.

Learning to use other people's work honestly is a skill, and you can get good at it with practice. It also protects the thing you came to university for: a degree that really belongs to you.
