Dropshipping sounds simple: you sell a product, a supplier sends it, and you never touch a box. For a reader in Tanzania, the picture gets harder once the supplier is abroad. Time, tax, payment and returns all start to matter.

This post follows one order from the first click to the doorstep. It does not say dropshipping is easy, and it does not promise any income. It also does not recommend or rank any supplier.

## The short answer

You can run a dropshipping shop from Tanzania, but the risks sit mostly with you. You are the seller your customer knows. If the parcel is late, damaged or wrong, the customer blames you, not the supplier.

- Shipping from abroad can take weeks, and the customer waits.
- Customs and tax may apply when goods enter Tanzania. Ask before you promise a final price.
- Some payment tools that foreign shops use are not available in Tanzania.
- Some suppliers do not take returns, so you may lose the money.
- Order a sample first and start with one product.

## How one dropshipping order travels

Shopify, the company that makes shop software, describes dropshipping as selling products you never stock. When an order comes in, you pass it to a supplier, who ships it straight to your customer. You set the price, take the payment and answer the customer's questions.

Here is one order, step by step:

1. A customer in Mwanza sees your post and orders a phone stand.
2. The customer pays you, for example by mobile money.
3. You pay the supplier abroad the supplier's price, and give them your customer's address.
4. The supplier packs the item and hands it to a carrier.
5. The parcel travels across borders and goes through customs in Tanzania.
6. It reaches the customer, or it does not, or it arrives damaged.
7. The customer writes to you.

Notice that steps 3 to 6 happen out of your sight. You do not see the item, the box or the label. You are trusting a stranger with your customer's order.

## Shipping time and customs on the way into Tanzania

Shopify says shipping from overseas can be slow. Its own example is orders sent by one Chinese shipping service, which it says take 7 to 15 business days. That is an example from the company, not a promise for Tanzania. Your own orders may be faster or slower. Ask the supplier for a real delivery time to Tanzania, and test it with a sample.

Customers often expect faster delivery than that. If your page says "arrives in 3 days" and the parcel takes three weeks, you will face angry messages and refund requests. Write an honest time on every product page.

### What happens at customs

We opened the Tanzania Revenue Authority (TRA) page on import procedures. As checked in November 2026, it says that imports into Tanzania follow East African Community customs rules. The importer appoints a licensed clearing and forwarding agent. The documents are filed online through the Tanzania Customs Integrated System (TANCIS). Documents listed include the final invoice, a packing list and a transport document such as an airway bill.

That page describes formal imports of goods, usually done through an agent. It does not describe small courier parcels. So we cannot tell you how duty on a small parcel is worked out. Ask TRA, or the courier, before you promise a price to a customer.

TRA's VAT page says that importing taxable goods into Mainland Tanzania is subject to VAT at 18%, with normal customs procedures applying. Whether a particular small parcel pays this, and how much, depends on the goods and the route. Check with TRA.

### The Tanzania Posts Corporation

If a parcel comes by post, the Tanzania Posts Corporation says on its website that parcels weigh from 3 kg to 30 kg. It says international parcels are sealed in front of a postal officer and a customs officer, and that a declaration form helps to estimate customs duty. Ask which service the supplier will use.

Shopify also warns that low-value imports can face duties and taxes, and that wrongly classified goods can cause fees and delays. Its examples are about the United States, so they are not Tanzanian rules. The lesson still holds: the customs bill can change the real cost of a product.

## Getting paid and paying the supplier from Tanzania

You need a way to take money from customers and a way to pay the supplier. Both can be harder than a video tutorial makes it look.

Shopify Payments is the payment tool built into Shopify. Shopify's help page lists 41 supported countries. As checked in November 2026, Tanzania is not on the list. The page says that if your country is not listed, you need a third-party payment provider. If you plan to use a platform, check its help pages for Tanzania before you pay for anything.

For customers in Tanzania, many people pay by mobile money. Be clear about how you receive it. Our post on [trust for new online sellers](/post/business-ideas/e-commerce/earn-customer-trust-new-online-seller-tanzania/) explains why buyers hesitate to pay first.

Paying a supplier abroad is a separate problem. We did not research the rules for sending money out of Tanzania, and we do not give any here. Ask your bank, and check the Bank of Tanzania website before you send money.

### A made-up example with TSh

This example is made up to show how money can disappear. The numbers are not real prices.

| Item | Amount (TSh) |
| --- | --- |
| Customer pays you | 40,000 |
| You pay the supplier | 20,000 |
| Shipping to Tanzania | 8,000 |
| Payment fee | 2,000 |
| What is left | 10,000 |

Here, 40,000 minus 20,000, minus 8,000, minus 2,000 leaves 10,000. That is before your phone data, advertising, and your time.

Now say the customer returns the item and the supplier refuses to take it back. You refund 40,000, so you get nothing from this sale. You have already paid 20,000 to the supplier, 8,000 for shipping and 2,000 in fees. You lose 30,000. One return like this can wipe out the profit from three good sales in this example.

## Returns, complaints and who carries the loss

Shopify lists returns as a risk. It says some suppliers do not take returns and may not refund, so the store can lose money. It also says the store handles customer support, including delays and supplier mistakes.

Before you sell, ask the supplier in writing:

- Do you accept returns? Who pays the return shipping?
- What happens if the item arrives broken or wrong?
- How long will you take to refund me?
- Can you give me a tracking number for every order?

If the answers are vague, treat that as an answer. Keep the messages.

Write your own return rule on your shop page, and make sure you can honour it even if the supplier does not. Rights under Tanzanian consumer law were not part of what we checked here. Look at the Fair Competition Commission website for current rules.

### Do not sell fakes

Never sell counterfeit goods or fake branded items. It harms customers, and most selling platforms ban it. If a product has a famous brand name at a very low price, think carefully, and walk away if you doubt it is genuine.

### Check who you are dealing with

The Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority (TCRA) has warned about online fraud. For large purchases from foreign online merchants, it advises checking whether the merchant has an agent in the country and buying through that agent where possible. This advice is for buyers, but as a dropshipper you are also a buyer from your supplier. A supplier with no clear business name, address or contact is a warning sign. Our post on [fake followers and "guaranteed sales" offers](/post/business-ideas/marketing/fake-followers-and-guaranteed-sales-scams/) lists other offers to avoid.

## Safer first steps: samples, one supplier, honest delivery times

Shopify says to order samples before listing products, and that you can sometimes ask for the sample fee to be taken off later orders. A sample shows you the real quality, the packaging and the real delivery time to Tanzania.

A careful start could look like this:

1. Choose one small, light item that is not fragile, not branded and not regulated.
2. Order one sample to your own address. Note the dates and any customs charge.
3. Check the quality honestly. Would you be happy if you had paid for it?
4. Write product pages that state the real delivery time and the full price, with no surprise costs at the door.
5. Try a few orders from friends or family who know it is a test, and learn from the problems.
6. Only then think about advertising.

Keep your own spending small, and do not borrow money for stock or advertising. For other starting ideas, see our list of [low-cost ways to start an online business in Tanzania](/post/business-ideas/e-commerce/low-cost-online-business-ideas-tanzania/).

## Common questions

### Is dropshipping legal in Tanzania?

We did not find an official Tanzanian page that names dropshipping. The goods you import and the money you earn still fall under customs and tax rules. Ask TRA and BRELA how your business should be registered. See our post on [registering a small business](/post/business-ideas/startups/register-small-business-tanzania-brela-tin-licence/).

### Do I need a lot of money to start?

You do not need to buy stock, but you will still pay for samples, a way to take payments, and your time. Costs and risks depend on the product, so we do not give a figure.

### Can I make a steady income from it?

We cannot say. Results vary a great deal, and many shops sell little. Treat it as a test and plan for the possibility that it earns nothing.

### What if the parcel never arrives?

Ask the supplier for tracking before you start. Tell the customer early, and offer a refund if the parcel is lost. Keep every message in case there is a dispute.

## What to do next

1. Write down what you would sell, and who would buy it in Tanzania.
2. Ask a supplier the four return questions above, in writing.
3. Ask TRA or a courier what duty and VAT could apply to a small parcel of that item.
4. Order one sample and record the real time and cost.

A slow, careful test costs little, and what you learn from it is worth more than a fast start that ends in refunds.
