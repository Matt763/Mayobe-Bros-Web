You open Facebook and see a post you never wrote. Or your password suddenly stops working. Your stomach drops, and you want to fix it in one minute. Take a breath. This is a common problem, and there is a clear order of steps that helps. This post gives you that order.

## The short answer

If you think someone else is using your Facebook account, act in this order. Speed matters, but order matters more.

- Use a phone or computer you have logged in from before, on your usual home or work network.
- Follow the recovery steps that Meta links from its Safety Center page, and use the form for a compromised account.
- Once you are in, check "Where You're Logged In" and log out every device you do not know.
- Change your password to a new, long and unique one, and turn on two-factor authentication.
- Warn your friends that nobody should send money or codes to "you" until you say you are back.

We cannot promise that every account can be recovered. What you can do is follow the official route and avoid the traps around it.

## Signs your account may have been hacked

Not every strange thing means a hack. Sometimes a friend tags you in a post, or you forget a password. But these signs are worth taking seriously:

- Posts, messages or friend requests you did not make.
- A password that stops working although you typed it correctly.
- Emails or alerts about a login from a place or device you do not know.
- Changes to your name, birthday, email address or phone number that you did not make.
- Friends telling you that you sent them a strange link or asked them for money.

Meta's Safety Center page says that if your account is hacked or compromised, you should "secure it immediately". It links to recovery steps and to a form for reporting a compromised Facebook account. As checked in November 2026, that is the starting point we recommend.

## Do these steps first

If you can still log in, work through this list slowly. Do not close the page halfway.

1. **Use a safe device.** Use a phone or computer you trust and have used for Facebook before. Do not use a shared computer at a cyber cafe if you can avoid it.
2. **Open the Meta Safety Center page** (the link is in the sources below) and follow its link for hacked accounts. Type the address yourself or use the link in this post. Do not tap a link in a message or email that says "Facebook Security".
3. **Run Security Checkup.** Meta's page recommends it as the place to review and add security to your account. You must be logged in to use it.
4. **Open "Security and Login" in your settings and find "Where You're Logged In".** This lists devices that have recently used your account. Log out any device you do not recognise.
5. **Change your password.** Use a long one that you have never used anywhere else. The next section and our [password post](/post/technology-solutions/cyber-security/strong-passwords-and-password-manager/) explain how.
6. **Turn on two-factor authentication.** Meta says it adds an extra layer of protection when someone tries to get into your account from a new computer, phone or tablet.
7. **Turn on login alerts.** Meta says you can be alerted when someone tries to log in from a device it does not recognise. The alert shows the device and where it is.
8. **Check your email.** If Facebook sent you messages about a change to your account, read them carefully. Do not tap links in them. Open Facebook yourself and look at the settings there.
9. **Remove things you did not do.** Delete posts you did not write and apologise briefly to anyone who got a strange message.

Menu names change from time to time and may look different in Swahili or on an older phone. We describe settings by name, as checked in November 2026. If a name is slightly different on your screen, look for the closest match.

## If you cannot get in

This is the scariest case. You type your password and it says no. Your email may have been changed too.

Do not try ten random passwords in a row. Do not install a "recovery app". Instead:

- Start from a device you have used before for Facebook, if you can. It is the safest place to begin.
- Use the recovery steps and the compromised-account form linked from the Meta Safety Center page.
- Check the email account that was linked to Facebook. If you cannot get into that either, recover your email first, using that email company's own help page.
- Be patient. Recovery can take time, and not every case ends the way we would like.

One more warning, and it is our own editorial advice. After a hack, people on social media will offer to "get your account back" for a fee. Do not pay them. A stranger who needs your password, a code or your money is often the next scammer. The only route that works is the one Meta itself provides, and it is free.

## What to tell your friends and family

A hacked account is often used to trick the people who trust you. A message from "you" says, "I am stuck, please send TSh 50,000 on mobile money, I will pay you back." Your friend sees your name and your photo, and may want to help.

So tell people quickly, by phone call or WhatsApp, or from another account if you have one:

- "My Facebook was hacked. Do not send any money or codes to anyone in my name."
- "If I ask for money, call me on my phone first."
- "Do not tap any link that came from my account."

If someone already sent money, tell them to contact their mobile money operator at once. We have a separate post on [how to check an AI voice or video scam](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/ai-voice-and-video-scams-how-to-check/), and the same call-back habit works here.

## Lock it down so it does not happen again

Most hacks begin with a stolen or reused password, or with a trick that gets you to type your password into a fake page. These habits make the next attempt much harder.

| Habit | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| A different password for every important account | One leak cannot open the others |
| Two-factor authentication on Facebook | A stolen password alone is not enough |
| Login alerts on | You hear about strange logins early |
| Check "Where You're Logged In" now and then | You can remove old or unknown devices |
| Open Facebook from the app or by typing the address | Fake login pages are a common trap |
| Never share a code you receive by SMS | Codes are meant only for you |

Meta's own page also says to use a different password for each account, not to share passwords, and not to store them where others can find them. Meta suggests at least eight characters; we suggest much longer, because longer passwords are harder to guess. See our [post on strong passwords and password managers](/post/technology-solutions/cyber-security/strong-passwords-and-password-manager/).

If you also use WhatsApp, protect it too. Our post on [two-step verification for WhatsApp](/post/technology-solutions/cyber-security/protect-whatsapp-account-two-step-verification/) shows how.

## Common questions

### Should I report a hacked account to the police?

TCRA's FAQ advises reporting fraud to a police station, where you can get an RB number and an investigator. We are not lawyers, so ask the police how they treat your case. If money was lost, a police report can help. Keep screenshots of the strange posts and messages.

### Can I just delete my account and start again?

You can, but first try to secure and recover it. Deleting does not stop a scammer from using your name, and a fake new account can still be made. If a fake account pretends to be you, Meta's Safety Center says to report it.

### Will two-factor authentication stop everything?

No. It makes a hack much harder, but you can still be tricked into giving away a code. Never share a code with anyone, even someone who sounds friendly or official.

### My friend's account sent me a strange link. What should I do?

Do not open it. Tell your friend by phone that their account may be hacked. You can also report the message inside Facebook.

## What to do next

1. Today, check "Where You're Logged In" and log out every device you do not know.
2. Change your Facebook password to a long, new one and turn on two-factor authentication and login alerts.
3. Tell three close friends or relatives how to recognise a message that is not really from you.
4. Make a note of your recovery email and phone number, and keep them up to date.

A hacked account feels personal, but it does not mean you were careless or foolish. You acted, and that is what counts.
