If your child has a phone, or borrows yours, you have probably wondered what is on that screen. That worry comes from love. You do not have to be a technology expert to help, and you do not need to be perfect. You only need to stay close to your child and know a few simple steps.

## The short answer

The best protection is a child who feels safe telling you when something goes wrong. Controls on the phone help, but they are only the second layer. If you need help, Tanzania has free and confidential support.

- Talk early and often, and stay calm when your child tells you something hard.
- Set up the phone together, with simple controls such as screen time and app approval.
- Remember that no tool can see everything. Talking works better than watching.
- For advice or a child at risk, call the National Child Helpline on 116 or go to the nearest police station.
- If you find sexual images of a child online, do not forward them. Report them using the Internet Watch Foundation portal for Tanzania.

## Start with a conversation, not a ban

A ban can feel safe, but children often find other ways online, and then they hide it. If they know that telling you will not end in anger, they tell you sooner. That is the real safety net.

Choose a quiet moment, not a moment of anger. A few sentences you can use as they are:

- "I'm not checking up on you. I want us to work out the rules together."
- "If you ever see something that frightens or confuses you, you can tell me. You will not be in trouble."
- "If someone you only know online asks you to keep a secret, or asks for photos, come and tell me straight away."
- "Which apps do you like? Show me how it works."

Then listen more than you speak. Ask what they enjoy, who they chat with and what they have seen. You will learn more from ten minutes of curiosity than from an hour of warnings.

Some rules help most when the whole family follows them. For example, phones are charged outside the bedroom at night, and everyone, including adults, puts phones away at meals. Say why you want the rules, and agree to review them as your child grows.

## Set up the phone: one simple example

Settings differ by phone, so here is one example from a maker's own help page. Google Family Link is a free parental control tool for Google accounts. According to Google's help page, as checked in November 2026, a parent can:

- set daily screen limits and bedtimes, and see how much time a child spends in certain apps
- approve or block app downloads and purchases
- restrict mature content on Google Play
- see the location of the child's Android device

Family Link also has limits that you should know about. Google says it can supervise Android devices, Chromebooks and some Fitbit devices, but not iPhones, iPads or computers other than Chromebooks. Google also says it may not be available in all countries, and that a child account is for children under 13 or the applicable age in your country. We do not state a Tanzanian age limit, because we did not find one on that page. Check Google's page for what applies where you live. iPhones have their own family settings, and your phone's manual or the maker's help page will show them.

Two honest points about any control tool:

1. It cannot replace trust. A clever child can find a way around a setting, and a child who feels watched may stop talking to you.
2. Be open about it. Set it up together and explain what you can and cannot see. We do not recommend any app that works in secret.

## Where to get help in Tanzania

| What you need | Where to go |
| --- | --- |
| Advice about a child, worry about abuse, parenting questions | National Child Helpline, 116. C-Sema says it is free on all mobile networks in Mainland Tanzania and Zanzibar, and confidential |
| A child at risk right now | The nearest police station, or call 116 |
| Sexual images or videos of someone under 18 | Report them on the Internet Watch Foundation portal for Tanzania (Kiswahili and English). You can report without giving your name |
| Scam calls or messages that target your family | Send the scam number to TCRA by SMS to 15040 and follow the automated reply |
| General child online protection information | The Child Online Protection page of Tz-CERT, Tanzania's national computer emergency response team |

We list no opening hours for 116, because the sources we read did not agree. If the line is busy, try again or go to the police station.

Tz-CERT's page has sheets for parents and for children, in Swahili. We did not summarise them here, so please open the page and read them yourself.

## What should I do if I find something harmful?

First, breathe. Your child needs you calm, not frightened.

- **If your child is the one who found it:** thank them for telling you. Say clearly that they did the right thing and are not in trouble.
- **Do not forward, copy or share the material**, even to warn others or to "show someone". Sharing makes the harm spread. If it is sexual material involving a child, use the Internet Watch Foundation portal to report it, and you can do so without giving your name.
- **If a child may be at risk**, call 116 or go to a police station.
- **If someone is bothering your child on an app**, take a screenshot of the profile name and messages, then block and report the person in the app. Keep the screenshots in case the police need them.
- **Do not confront the other person yourself.** Leave that to the police.

We are not doctors or counsellors. If your child seems very upset or afraid after something online, the helpline on 116 can link you to local services.

## Common questions

### At what age should my child get a phone?

There is no single right age, and we found no official Tanzanian rule to quote. Think about your child's maturity, how they would use it, and whether you can talk through the rules together first.

### Is the helpline only for abuse?

No. C-Sema says the National Child Helpline covers child abuse and parenting questions, and it links callers to local services.

### Can I read my child's messages in secret?

We do not recommend secret monitoring. It can damage trust, and a child who stops talking to you is harder to protect. Open rules that you set together work better.

### What if I made a mistake and my child saw something upsetting?

Nobody gets this right every time. Listen, comfort them, and talk about what they saw. Then adjust your settings together.

## What to do next

1. Choose one quiet moment this week and use one of the sample sentences above.
2. Sit with your child and set up one control, such as screen time or app approval. Explain each step as you go.
3. Save 116 in your phone, and show your child where the number is.
4. Open the Tz-CERT child online protection page and read the sheets for parents.

You will not get every moment right, and that is fine. A child who knows you will listen is already much safer.
