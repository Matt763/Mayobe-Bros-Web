Nelson Mandela is quoted everywhere: on posters, in school essays and in social media posts. Many of those lines are real. Some are cut short, and a few were never his. This post lists quotes that we checked against the text of a speech, a letter or a statement, with the date and the occasion for each.

Where we could not confirm the exact words, we say so and leave the line out.

## Key points

- Every quote in the main list comes from a published text with a date and an occasion, mostly the South African government's collection of Mandela quotes and the speeches themselves.
- The most famous "Mandela" passage, "Our deepest fear is not that we are inadequate", is not his. The Nelson Mandela Foundation says so.
- Quotes about education are often shared with different wording. We explain why we do not quote one line as exact.
- A quote means more when you know who said it, when, and what was happening.

## Who he was, in a few dates

These dates come from the Nelson Mandela Foundation's biography.

- **18 July 1918:** born in Mvezo, in the Eastern Cape.
- **1944:** joined the African National Congress (ANC), helping to form its Youth League.
- **5 December 1956:** arrested in the Treason Trial, which ended with his acquittal on 29 March 1961.
- **11 January 1962:** left South Africa in secret and travelled around Africa to gain support for the armed struggle. He was arrested on 5 August 1962 after he returned.
- **9 October 1963:** joined ten others on trial for sabotage in the Rivonia Trial.
- **12 June 1964:** sentenced to life in prison. He was held on Robben Island, later at Pollsmoor Prison and Victor Verster Prison.
- **11 February 1990:** released from Victor Verster Prison.
- **1993:** shared the Nobel Peace Prize with President F W de Klerk.
- **27 April 1994:** voted for the first time in his life.
- **10 May 1994:** inaugurated as South Africa's first democratically elected president.
- **1999:** stepped down after one term.
- **5 December 2013:** died at his home in Johannesburg.

Apartheid was the system of laws by which South Africa's white minority government separated people by race and denied most rights to Black South Africans. Many of the quotes below only make sense against that background.

## Quotes we checked

### 1. "An ideal for which I am prepared to die" (20 April 1964)

At the Rivonia Trial, Mandela made a statement from the dock at the opening of the defence case, in the Pretoria Supreme Court. The court transcript, kept by South African History Online, ends:

> "During my lifetime I have dedicated myself to this struggle of the African people. I have fought against white domination, and I have fought against black domination. I have cherished the ideal of a democratic and free society in which all persons live together in harmony and with equal opportunities. It is an ideal which I hope to live for and to achieve. But if needs be, it is an ideal for which I am prepared to die."

The Foundation notes that he said this while facing the death penalty. People often shorten it to the last sentence. The full passage shows that he hoped to live for the ideal first.

### 2. "Your freedom and mine cannot be separated" (10 February 1985)

In 1985 the government offered Mandela release on conditions. He refused. His reply was a letter, read to a crowd at Jabulani Stadium in Soweto by his daughter Zindzi on 10 February 1985. It says:

> "Too many have suffered for the love of freedom. I too cannot sell my birthright, nor am I prepared to sell the birthright of my people to be free. Your freedom and mine cannot be separated."

The Foundation says that throughout his imprisonment he rejected at least three conditional offers of release.

### 3. "A humble servant of you, the people" (11 February 1990)

On the day he left prison, he spoke in Cape Town:

> "I stand before you not as a prophet but as a humble servant of you, the people. Your tireless and heroic sacrifices have made it possible for me to be here today."

The date is the day of his release, a Sunday. The Foundation records that he was released that day from the gates of Victor Verster Prison.

### 4. Oppression harms the oppressor too (2 July 1990)

In an address to the Parliament of Ireland on 2 July 1990, he said:

> "...we understood that the oppression dehumanises the oppressor as it hurts the oppressed. We understood that to emulate the barbarity of the tyrant would also transform us into savages."

He was explaining why the liberation movement did not copy the methods of the government it opposed.

### 5. "Common humanity" (10 December 1993)

At the Nobel Peace Prize ceremony in Norway, he spoke of a future shaped by "the common humanity that bonds both black and white into one human race". The full sentence, as printed in the government's collection, speaks of "joyful peace which will triumph" because of that common humanity.

### 6. "Never, never and never again" (10 May 1994)

At his inauguration at the Union Buildings in Pretoria, Mandela said, in the text published by the South African government:

> "Never, never and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another and suffer the indignity of being the skunk of the world."

In the same speech he spoke of "a rainbow nation at peace with itself and the world", and ended with "Let freedom reign" and "God bless Africa!"

### 7. Africa's part in the struggle (13 June 1994)

Speaking to the Organisation of African Unity meeting of heads of state and government on 13 June 1994, he said:

> "Africa shed her blood and surrendered the lives of her children so that all her children could be free."

The government's collection gives that date and occasion. It does not name the town, so we do not.

### 8. "People come and go" (14 February 1995)

At the inauguration of the Constitutional Court, he said:

> "People come and go. Customs, fashions and preferences change. Yet the web of fundamental rights and justice which a nation proclaims must not be broken."

## A famous quote he never said

The passage that starts "Our deepest fear is not that we are inadequate. Our deepest fear is that we are powerful beyond measure" is often credited to Mandela, and many people believe he said it at his 1994 inauguration.

The Nelson Mandela Foundation published a correction on 9 November 2007. It says the words are by Marianne Williamson, in her book *A Return to Love*. It also says Mandela did not use them in any of his three public addresses at the time of his inauguration. Those were the speech in Cape Town on 9 May 1994, the speech at the Union Buildings on 10 May 1994, and the oath of office. A Foundation archivist said that as far as she knew he never used the quote in any of his speeches, out of about 1,000 catalogued at that time.

If you see this passage with Mandela's name on it, you can tell people the Foundation says it is not his.

## A quote with changing wording: education

You will see "Education is the most powerful weapon which you can use to change the world" on many pages. An archive listing places a Mandela speech at the launch of the Mindset Network on 16 July 2003, titled "Lighting your way to a better future", and many pages link the line to it. Some sites give the line as "weapon we can use" and others as "weapon which you can use". We could not open the speech text itself, so we cannot say which wording is exact.

What we can say is that Mandela spoke about education often, and that the idea is real. For an exact quote in an essay, find the speech text and check it yourself, then cite the source.

## What the quotes can mean for a student today

You do not need to agree with every line to learn from them. Some practical points:

- **Check before you quote.** Many shared quotes are cut, changed or wrongly credited. Look for the speech, the date and the occasion.
- **Read the full passage.** The Rivonia words mean more with the sentences before them.
- **Notice the setting.** A letter read from a crowd, a speech in court and an inauguration are very different places.
- **Cite your source.** In an essay, name the speech, date and where you read the text. Teachers notice the difference.

## Common questions

### Did Mandela really say "Our deepest fear..."?

No. The Nelson Mandela Foundation says the passage is from Marianne Williamson's book *A Return to Love* and that he did not use it in his inauguration speeches.

### Where can I read his speeches?

The Nelson Mandela Foundation keeps a speeches archive, and the South African government publishes many texts. South African History Online holds the Rivonia court transcript.

### How long was he in prison?

The Foundation's dates show that he was arrested on 5 August 1962, sentenced to life on 12 June 1964 and released on 11 February 1990. You can work out the length from those dates.

### Was he linked to Tanzania?

The Foundation says he travelled around Africa in 1962 to gain support, but its biography does not list the countries, so we do not claim any here. Check a dated source before you write about it.

## What to do next

1. Pick one quote from this list and read the whole speech it comes from.
2. Before you share any quote, search for the speech text, not only for another page repeating it.
3. If you write about Mandela for school, use the Foundation or a government text as your source and give the date.

A short quote is a door. The speech behind it is the room.
