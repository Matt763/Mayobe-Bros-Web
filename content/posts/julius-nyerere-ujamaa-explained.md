Almost every Tanzanian knows the name Mwalimu, "the teacher". Fewer people can say quickly who he was, what Ujamaa meant, or why the policy is remembered with both pride and pain. This post gives the plain facts, in order, and lets you decide what you think.

## The short answer

Julius Nyerere was the leader of the independence movement in Tanganyika and later the first president of the country. Ujamaa, a Swahili word for "familyhood", was his idea of how a poor, newly independent country could develop in a fair way. The Arusha Declaration of 1967 turned it into national policy.

- Nyerere was born on 13 April 1922 in Butiama, near Musoma, and trained as a teacher.
- He led TANU from 1954, became prime minister in 1960 and saw Tanganyika reach independence on 9 December 1961.
- Ujamaa meant shared work, shared property and mutual care, with a strong role for the state.
- Schools and health care grew under the policy, but the rural villages and the economy suffered, and the policy was dropped in the late 1970s.
- In his farewell speech on 5 November 1985 he accepted that his Ujamaa policies had shortcomings.

## Who was Julius Nyerere? From Butiama to Edinburgh

Nyerere was born in the village of Butiama, near the town of Musoma. His father, Nyerere Burito, was a chief of the Zanaki people. According to the EBSCO Research Starter on his rise, Catholic missionaries (the Maryknoll fathers) invited him to start school when he was twelve. After two years he moved to St Mary's, a secondary school in Tabora, and then to Makerere College in Uganda.

At Makerere, in 1943, he helped set up the Tanganyika African Welfare Association. He then taught at his old school in Tabora for four years.

In 1949 he travelled to the University of Edinburgh in Scotland. He studied there until 1952 and became the first Tanganyikan to earn a master's degree, in history and economics. That was a first for his country.

He did not go into government. He went back to the classroom, teaching at Pugu St Francis Secondary School near Dar es Salaam.

## Why do people call him Mwalimu?

"Mwalimu" is the Kiswahili word for teacher. The EBSCO page says he was known by this word affectionately, because teaching was his job before politics.

The title stayed with him as president. One of his best-known policy papers, "Education for Self-Reliance", was about schools.

## From TANU to the presidency

Politics found him anyway. The Tanganyika African National Union (TANU) was formed on 7 July 1954, with Nyerere as president. Tanzanians still mark that date as Saba Saba Day (the "seventh of the seventh").

He made two trips to the United Nations Trusteeship Council in New York, in March 1955 and December 1956. TANU won what the EBSCO page calls an "overwhelming victory" in the Legislative Council elections of September 1958. In August 1960 he was elected prime minister of the new government.

Tanganyika became independent on 9 December 1961. A year later it became a republic and Nyerere became president in 1962. In 1964, after the revolution in Zanzibar, the two countries united as Tanzania. One source dates his presidency from 1962 and another gives 1964 to 1985, which is the period of the united country. Both dates are used because both events were real.

In 1977 TANU joined Zanzibar's Afro-Shirazi Party to form Chama Cha Mapinduzi (CCM). Nyerere argued that a strong, open single party suited Tanzania better than the multiparty systems favoured in the West. Tanzania returned to a multiparty system in 1992, after he had left the presidency.

## What does Ujamaa mean?

Ujamaa comes from the Swahili word for "family" or "extended family". The Konrad Adenauer Foundation, in an article published in 2023, says Nyerere first defined it in a 1962 essay called "Ujamaa: the Basis for African Socialism". The EBSCO page on the Arusha Declaration lists three ideas at its heart:

1. People recognise that they depend on each other.
2. Property is owned in common.
3. Everyone is expected to work.

Nyerere pictured a Tanzania where the care of the extended family, which many people already knew from their own villages, was widened to cover the whole nation.

## The Arusha Declaration of 1967

The Declaration was written by Nyerere for TANU. An English version on the Marxists Internet Archive carries the date 5 February 1967. Its last part, the Arusha Resolution, was adopted by the party's National Executive Committee at a meeting in Arusha in January 1967.

It opens its main policy statement plainly: "The policy of TANU is to build a socialist state." Other parts set out a policy of self-reliance, meaning that Tanzania should depend on its own people and effort more than on foreign money. One line from the text sums it up: "The development of a country is brought about by people, not by money."

The Declaration also set a leadership code for TANU and government leaders. For example, it said that no leader should hold shares in any company, receive two or more salaries, or own houses that he rents to others. The idea was that people in power should not become a new privileged class.

Action came quickly. According to the Julius Nyerere website, all private commercial banks were nationalised on 6 February 1967, and more steps followed on the next four days. In March 1967 came "Education for Self-Reliance". It aimed to help most children finish primary school and then enter farming, with Kiswahili used in rural schools and a farm at each school.

One detail surprises many readers. The EBSCO page notes that the word "ujamaa" does not appear in the Declaration itself. Nyerere wrote about it in more detail in "Socialism and Rural Development" in September 1967.

## Ujamaa villages: what happened

The plan for the countryside was to gather people into villages where they would farm together and share a school, a clinic and clean water. The EBSCO page describes the aim as "ideal rural communities", farming collectively.

Here the historians need careful reading. Priya Lal, writing in the Journal of African History in 2010, notes that villagisation between 1967 and 1972 was voluntary. After 1973, under what was called Operation Vijiji, resettlement became compulsory, and the goal of building true Ujamaa gave way to the more immediate aim of moving people into concentrated settlements. So the answer to "were people forced?" depends on the years you mean. We could not find a reliable page in our research that gives one agreed number of people moved, so we do not give one.

What did the villages become? EBSCO says they later turned into instruments of state control over farming and that many peasants found ways to leave and farm their own plots. It adds that collectivisation ended around 1977 and 1978. The Konrad Adenauer Foundation also says Ujamaa ended in the late 1970s because of economic failure.

## The economy and the gains

The EBSCO article is blunt about the results. It says the goal of self-reliance "was never realized", that the economy was "in shambles" by the end of the decade, that exports fell sharply and that the country struggled to import basic goods. It also says Tanzania fought a war with Idi Amin's Uganda in 1978 and 1979, which was costly. And it says that, when Tanzania could not meet its debts, it gave up socialist policies at the insistence of the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund. The page does not name a source for that last claim, so treat it as one account.

The same article says Ujamaa raised the country's health and education standards, even while calling it "more utopian than utilitarian". Both things can be true at once: a policy can build schools and clinics and still fail to make a country richer.

We try to keep our own opinion out of it. If you want to judge, ask two questions. What did the policy aim to do for ordinary people? And what did ordinary people say about how it felt in their own lives? The second question is best answered by older relatives and by village histories.

## Stepping down in 1985

Nyerere resigned as president on 5 November 1985. In his farewell speech that day, according to EBSCO, he acknowledged the shortcomings of his Ujamaa policies. The page gives no more detail, so we do not put words in his mouth.

The single-party system he had defended ended in 1992, when Tanzania returned to multiparty politics.

## Common questions

### Was Nyerere the president of Tanganyika or of Tanzania?

Both. He led Tanganyika to independence in 1961, became president when it turned into a republic in 1962, and stayed president after the union with Zanzibar in 1964. He left office in 1985.

### What does Ujamaa mean in English?

"Familyhood". The idea was that a nation should treat its members the way a good extended family treats its own.

### Were people forced into Ujamaa villages?

Historians distinguish two periods. Lal notes that from 1967 to 1972 villagisation was voluntary and that after 1973 it became compulsory. Sources do not agree on how many people were moved, so numbers should be treated with care.

### Did Ujamaa fail?

The sources we read say it failed to reach its economic goals and was dropped in the late 1970s. They also say it raised health and education standards. Nyerere himself said, in 1985, that the policy had shortcomings.

## What to do next

1. Read the Arusha Declaration yourself. It is short, and reading the original is better than reading what people say about it.
2. Ask someone older in your family what they remember of village life in the 1970s. Their memory is a real source too. Write it down.
3. Read about how German rule shaped the land Nyerere inherited, in our post on [the Maji Maji War](/post/world-histories/maji-maji-war-why-it-started/).
