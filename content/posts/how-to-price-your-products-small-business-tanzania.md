You open early. You carry, weigh, count, smile and bargain until evening. Customers came all day, yet at the end of the month there is almost nothing left in your hand. That is a heavy feeling, and it is more common than people admit. Very often the problem is not your effort. It is the price.

This post shows a calm way to set a price that covers what the item really costs you, leaves something for your own pay, and still makes sense to the customer. All the numbers in the examples are made up for learning. They are not real market prices.

## The short answer

Your price should be the full cost of one item plus the profit you need. It should not be copied from the neighbour, because the neighbour may be paying different costs, or may be losing money without knowing it.

- Count every cost, including transport, packaging, spoiled goods and the rent of your spot.
- Divide the total by the number of items you can really sell, not the number you bought.
- Add a mark-up, then check what share of the final price is profit. These are two different numbers.
- Keep tax in mind. Tax is paid on sales, so it comes out of your profit if the price does not allow for it.
- Look at the market after you know your own cost, then test your price.

## Which costs should I count?

Most people count what they paid the supplier and stop there. The costs that quietly eat your money are the other ones. Write them in three groups.

**Direct costs** belong to the item itself: what you paid for the goods, the sack or the container, and the transport to bring them to your shop or stall.

**Shared costs** belong to the whole business: rent, a market fee, electricity, airtime for customer calls, a worker's pay. You divide them across the items you sell in a month.

**Hidden costs** are the ones you forget: goods that spill, spoil or are damaged, items you give as a gift or on credit and never get paid for, and the small charges you pay when you send or take out money by phone. Charges differ by network and change over time, so check your own network's current tariff and add what it takes from you.

One more cost is yourself. If you work at the stall every day and your price pays you nothing, you are working for free. Decide on a fair pay for your time, and write it in the list as a cost.

## How do I find the cost of one item?

Here is a made-up example. Say you buy a sack of rice of 52 kg and sell it in small bags by the kilogram. These numbers are invented to show the method.

| Cost for one sack | Amount (TSh) |
| --- | --- |
| The sack from the supplier | 140,000 |
| Transport to your shop | 5,000 |
| Small bags for packing | 2,000 |
| Share of shop rent for this sack | 3,000 |
| Your own pay for the work on this sack | 10,000 |
| **Total cost** | **160,000** |

Now count what you can sell. Of the 52 kg, say 2 kg is lost in spills and weighing mistakes. So you can sell 50 kg.

Cost of one kilogram: TSh 160,000 divided by 50 = **TSh 3,200**.

If you had divided by 52 kg by mistake, you would have got about TSh 3,077 and sold every kilogram too cheaply. That loss of TSh 123 on each kilogram looks small. Over 50 kg it is more than TSh 6,000 on one sack, and you may buy many sacks.

## What is the difference between mark-up and margin?

This mix-up costs traders real money, so go slowly here.

**Mark-up** is the amount you add on top of your cost, shown as a share of the cost.

**Margin** is the part of the selling price that is profit, shown as a share of the price.

They are not the same number. Using the cost of TSh 3,200 per kilogram:

- You add a mark-up of 25%. 25% of 3,200 is 800. The price is 3,200 + 800 = **TSh 4,000**.
- Your profit is TSh 800. As a share of the price, 800 divided by 4,000 is 0.20. Your margin is **20%**.

So a 25% mark-up gives only a 20% margin. Many sellers add 25% and believe 25% of every sale is profit. It is not.

If you really want 25% of the price to be profit, divide your cost by 0.75 (that is 100% minus 25%, written as a decimal):

TSh 3,200 divided by 0.75 = TSh 4,266.67. Round up to **TSh 4,270**.

Check it: profit is 4,270 minus 3,200 = 1,070. And 1,070 divided by 4,270 is about 0.25. It works.

Which one should you use? Either, as long as you know which one you are talking about. If you keep a record of your sales (see our post on [simple business records and a cash book](/post/business-ideas/startups/simple-business-records-small-traders-cash-book-tanzania/)), you will see your real profit as a share of your sales, and you can compare it with the margin you planned.

## Where does tax fit into the price?

Tax is not a surprise bill at the end if you plan for it. This section tells you what TRA states on its own pages, as checked in November 2026. Rules and amounts can change, so confirm on the TRA website before you decide.

**Income tax for small traders.** TRA says an individual trader whose yearly turnover does not pass TSh 200 million can fall under the presumptive tax system, where tax depends on turnover (your total sales), not on profit. Under TSh 4 million of turnover a year the tax is nil. For turnover above TSh 11 million up to TSh 200 million, the tax is 4% of turnover. There are lower bands in between, and the amount is smaller when your records are complete. For a full explanation, read our post on [presumptive tax for small business](/post/business-ideas/startups/small-business-tax-tanzania-presumptive-tax-explained/).

Back to the made-up rice example. Selling 50 kg at TSh 4,000 brings TSh 200,000 for the sack. If the 4% band applied, tax would be 4% of 200,000, which is TSh 8,000. Your profit of TSh 40,000 (200,000 minus 160,000) becomes TSh 32,000. Per kilogram, the tax is TSh 160 and your profit is TSh 640 after tax. If your price ignored this, the tax would eat 20% of your profit.

**VAT.** TRA gives the standard VAT rate as 18%. It says VAT registration is compulsory when taxable turnover reaches TSh 200 million within twelve months, or TSh 100 million within six months. TRA also says VAT is charged by persons registered for VAT only. So a very small trader below those levels does not add VAT to the price. If your business grows near these levels, talk to TRA or a qualified tax adviser. This post is not tax advice.

## How do I check my price against the market?

Only now should you look at what others charge, because only now do you know your own floor.

1. Visit two or three sellers of the same item and write down their prices and the size or quality.
2. If your cost is higher than their price, do not just match them. Ask why. Maybe you buy in smaller amounts, or you carry the goods farther, or you waste more.
3. If their price is much higher than yours, you may be leaving money behind.
4. Be careful with prices that look unrealistic. Even online sellers are told to research similar items first. The Jiji seller help page says an unsuitable price can lead to an advert being declined and advises sellers to research prices of the same item.

If the market will not pay your price, you have three honest choices: lower your costs, sell something with a better margin, or sell in a different place or pack size. Cutting the price below your cost, hoping to make it up in volume, only makes the loss bigger.

## When should I lower or raise a price?

- Lower a price on goods that spoil soon, if the choice is a smaller profit or no sale at all. Do it knowingly, and record it.
- Raise a price when your supplier raises theirs. Waiting a month "to keep customers happy" means you pay the difference.
- Test one change at a time. Change one price, watch your sales for a week or two, and write down what happened.

## Common questions

### Should I put my price lower than my neighbour to win customers?

Only if your costs allow it. A lower price with no profit is a quick way to close the shop. Many customers also care about trust, clean goods, fair weight and being easy to find, not only the lowest price. Our post on [knowing your customers](/post/business-ideas/marketing/know-your-customers-small-business/) shows how to ask them what they value.

### Do I add my own pay to the price?

Yes. Your time is a real cost. If you leave it out, your business looks like it makes money when it does not pay you.

### Is it wrong to charge the same price for goods bought at different prices?

No, many sellers do. But if your supplier price changes a lot, recalculate your cost per item each time you restock.

### Do I have to add VAT?

TRA says VAT is charged by registered persons only, and registration is compulsory at the turnover levels described above. If you are unsure whether it applies to you, ask TRA.

## What to do next

1. Pick your three best-selling items. Write every cost for each one, in the three groups above.
2. Work out the cost of one item, then the price with a mark-up, and check the margin.
3. Read the TRA pages in the sources below and note which tax band fits your yearly sales.
4. Start a simple cash book so you can see if your prices really leave profit.

Pricing is a skill, and every careful number you write down makes the next month a little easier.
