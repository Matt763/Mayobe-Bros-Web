Your first internship can feel like a locked door. You have the certificate, you have the hope, and you do not know where to knock. This post gives you a plan: where to look, how to ask, and how to tell a real offer from a fake one.

## The short answer

A real internship is a fixed period of entry-level work that teaches you the job. You find it through your college, through people you know, and by asking organisations directly. A real one has clear terms, a named supervisor and no fee from you.

- Start with your college or university placement or career office.
- Tell family, lecturers and friends exactly what kind of work you want to try.
- Write directly to organisations, even when they have not advertised.
- Never pay anyone to give you an internship.
- Agree the basics in writing before day one.
- Set two or three learning goals in your first week.

## What an internship is and what it is not

An internship is a short period of work experience. Prospects, the UK graduate careers service, describes it as lasting from a week to 12 months. It lets you test a job and build skills, and employers often hire from their interns.

In Tanzania the word you will also hear is *utarajali*, which is the Swahili term used for internship training. If your course includes a placement, ask your department how it works.

An internship is not a favour you buy, and it is not free labour with no learning. If you only carry boxes and nobody explains anything, it is not doing its job. A fair internship gives you tasks, a person who guides you and feedback.

## Where to look for an internship

There is no single list for the whole country, so use several routes at the same time.

1. **Your college or university.** Ask the placement or career office, your head of department and your lecturers. They often know organisations that take students.
2. **Your own network.** Tell relatives, neighbours, church or mosque friends and former classmates what you are looking for. Be specific: "I want to learn bookkeeping in a small firm" is easier to help with than "I need any job".
3. **Professional bodies and associations.** Many fields have a professional body. Check its website or office for notices about student placements.
4. **Social media and job sites.** Follow organisations you like and read their posts. Treat every advert with care, using the checklist below.
5. **Direct requests.** Write to an organisation that does the work you want to learn. Prospects calls this a speculative application.
6. **Government pages.** The Prime Minister's Office, Labour, Employment and Relations publishes information at kazi.go.tz, and its site links to the Kazi Portal at jobs.kazi.go.tz. As checked in November 2026, we could not find details on that page about who can apply for government internship programmes, so check the portal yourself for what is open and who qualifies.

The same office has a video on internship training in Swahili. Here it is, if you want to see it.

## How to ask an organisation directly

Prospects advises phoning first to learn who should receive your application, and to get a contact name. In Tanzania a visit in person to a small office can work too, if you dress neatly and keep it short.

Then send a short message or letter. Keep it to four parts:

- Who you are, and what you study or have finished.
- Why you chose this organisation (one real reason).
- What you can offer, such as basic computer skills, a good attendance record or a language.
- What you ask for: a few weeks or months of placement, and a time when you could start.

Attach a one-page CV. If you need help with it, see our guide to [writing a CV for your first job](/post/jobs-and-internships/how-to-write-a-cv-for-your-first-job-tanzania/).

Many messages will get no reply. That is normal and it says little about you. Send ten, not two. Follow up once after a week or two, politely.

## How to tell a real offer from a fake one

Most organisations are honest. A few people use the hope of young job seekers to take money. Check every offer against these points.

| Check | A real offer | A warning sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Money | You pay nothing to apply or to start | They ask for a "registration", "processing" or "uniform" fee |
| Contact | A named person, an official email or office you can visit | Only a chat app number, or a free email address pretending to be a big company |
| Terms | They tell you the duties, hours, length and place | Everything is vague, or they rush you to decide today |
| Supervisor | You will know who guides you | Nobody can say who you will work with |
| Organisation | You can find the office and people who know it | You cannot find the office, or the company seems new and has no trace |
| Pressure | You can ask questions and take a day to think | They threaten that the place will go to someone else |

If an offer sounds too good, slow down. Call the organisation on a number you found yourself, not the number in the message. Ask your college office or a lecturer to look at it. Read more in our post on [fake job offers and the warning signs](/post/jobs-and-internships/fake-job-offers-tanzania-warning-signs-never-pay/).

Prospects also suggests checking whether the internship is paid, who will supervise you, and whether the employer hires from its interns. Pay for interns differs a lot between organisations and sectors, so ask openly and do not assume. Prospects gives UK rules about the minimum wage, which do not apply in Tanzania, so we do not repeat them here.

## What to agree in writing

You do not need a long legal paper for a short placement. You do need a short written note, such as an email or a letter, that both sides keep. It should say:

- Your start and end dates, and the days and hours you will work.
- The place of work.
- The main tasks, or the department you will join.
- Who your supervisor is.
- Whether there is any pay, allowance or help with transport or meals. If the answer is none, it is better to know now.
- Whether they can give you a letter or reference when you finish.

Ask politely: "Could you confirm these points by email so I can plan?" A real organisation will not mind.

## Make the most of it: set learning goals

The University of Washington career centre advises writing two or three specific goals before you start, and agreeing them in writing with your supervisor in the first week. Meet your supervisor every week or two, and again at the halfway point to adjust the goals.

Say you are placed in a small accounting office. A weak goal is "learn accounting". A better goal is "learn to record sales and expenses in the firm's books by the end of month one". You can check that one.

Keep a short daily note of what you did and what you learned. At the end you will have real examples for your CV and for interviews. Thank your supervisor in person and ask whether they would act as your referee.

## Common questions

### Does an internship have to be paid?

It depends on the organisation, the sector and the agreement. We did not find a Tanzanian rule that we could confirm and quote here, so do not assume. Ask before you accept, and get the answer in writing.

### Should I pay a fee to get an internship?

No. If someone asks you for money to give you a place, treat it as a warning sign and walk away. Tell your college office.

### Can I apply if no one has advertised?

Yes. Writing to an organisation that does the work you want is a normal way to find a placement, and it is what Prospects calls a speculative application.

### Will an internship lead to a job?

Sometimes, but nobody can promise it. Ask the organisation whether it hires from its interns and how often. Even without a job offer, you leave with skills, a reference and examples.

## What to do next

1. Write down the three kinds of organisation where you would like to learn, and one real name for each.
2. Visit your college placement or career office this week and ask what they know.
3. Send three short messages to organisations that do the work you want.
4. Before you accept any offer, check it against the table above and ask for the terms in writing.

You are allowed to start small. One honest placement, with a person who teaches you, can open the next door.
