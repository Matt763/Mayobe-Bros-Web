A past paper is an old exam paper from an earlier year. Students in Tanzania call it a "past paper", and teachers use it to show how NECTA sets questions. Used well, it is one of the best revision tools you have. Used badly, it becomes a way to memorise answers and feel ready without being ready. This guide gives a method for PSLE, FTNA (Form Two), CSEE (Form Four) and ACSEE (Form Six) candidates, and a short note for teachers and study groups.

## Key points

- Do a past paper to learn how questions are asked, not to guess next year's questions.
- Start with an untimed attempt, then do timed mocks closer to the exam.
- Mark your work honestly and write down every mistake in one notebook.
- Check the paper's layout against NECTA's own format booklet for your subject.
- Do not trust "guaranteed answers" or leaked papers. They are cheating, and many are wrong.

## What is a past paper, and what is it not?

A past paper is a real or school-set exam from an earlier year, with the same kind of sections and marks as the exam you will sit. NECTA is the National Examinations Council of Tanzania, and it sets PSLE, FTNA, CSEE and ACSEE.

A past paper is not a promise. Exams change from year to year, and syllabuses are revised. NECTA says its format booklets exist so examination officers can set papers and teachers can set school tests, and that the formats must not replace the syllabus. So use past papers together with your syllabus and notes, not in place of them.

## Why does practising with past papers help?

The reasons are plain, and you can test them yourself.

- **You see the shape of the exam.** You learn how many sections there are, how many questions to answer and how marks are shared.
- **You find your gaps.** Reading notes feels easy. Answering a question without notes shows you what you really know.
- **You learn the words examiners use.** Words like "list", "explain", "compare" and "discuss" ask for different answers.
- **You practise time.** Many students know the work but run out of time.

Our guide to [study techniques that help you remember more](/post/educational/study-tips/10-proven-techniques-to-study-effectively-for-students/) explains why testing yourself beats rereading. We do not make claims here about exact score gains, because we cannot promise any.

## What does NECTA itself publish?

We checked NECTA's website on 24 November 2026. Here is what we could confirm.

- **Examination formats.** NECTA publishes approved format booklets, for example for the CSEE (2022 edition), ACSEE (2019 edition) and FTNA (2022 edition). For each subject they give the objectives, the exam rubric (how many papers, how long, how marks are shared), the topics, and a table showing the weight of each topic. As an example, the CSEE booklet says Basic Mathematics is one three-hour paper with 14 questions: ten short-answer questions of 6 marks and four structured questions of 10 marks, 100 marks in all. The ACSEE booklet says Advanced Mathematics has two papers of three hours each. Before you practise, read your subject's format. It tells you what a real paper looks like.
- **Item response analysis reports.** These explain how candidates answered each question and where marks were lost. NECTA has them for CSEE and ACSEE (CIRA), FTNA (SIRA, in Swahili for some subjects) and for primary exams (PIRA for SFNA, and a report for PSLE). We opened reports for several of these levels. Our post on [NECTA examiners' reports](/post/educational/study-tips/use-necta-examiners-reports-to-revise/) shows how to read one.
- **Past papers and marking schemes.** We did not find an official NECTA page listing past exam papers or official marking schemes. The Publications page we opened showed no list of them. Papers and answers found elsewhere are made by other people, and we could not confirm that they match NECTA's own. Treat answer keys from unofficial sites as drafts to check with your teacher.

This is why the examiners' reports matter so much. They are the official voice on what a good answer looks like.

## A step-by-step method

### Step 1: Finish the topic first

Do not start with a full paper when you have not studied half the syllabus. Pick questions on the topics you have already covered.

### Step 2: Do one paper untimed

Sit somewhere quiet. Answer without notes and without a phone. Take your time and write full answers. The aim is to learn, not to score.

### Step 3: Mark it honestly

Use your textbook, your class notes and your teacher. Where possible, compare with the examiners' report for that subject and year. Give yourself marks the way an examiner would. For essay answers, ask: did I answer what the question asked?

### Step 4: Keep a mistakes log

This is the most useful habit. Use one notebook and draw a table.

| Question | What I did wrong | The right idea | When I will retry |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Example: Maths Q4 | Forgot to show steps | Method marks need working | Saturday |

Sort your mistakes into three kinds: I did not know it, I knew it but made a careless error, I ran out of time. Each kind has a different cure. Gaps need study, careless errors need slower reading and checking, and time problems need timed practice.

### Step 5: Fix the gaps, then retry

Go back to your notes on the weak topics. A few days later, try a similar question without looking. If you get it right, tick it. If not, it stays on the log.

### Step 6: Add timed mocks

When you have covered most of the syllabus, do papers under real conditions. Use the time written in your subject's format, with only the tools allowed (for example, a non-programmable calculator where the format says so). Practise starting with the questions you can do and leaving the hardest for last.

### Step 7: Review the pattern

After four or five papers, read your log. Which topics keep returning? Those are your priority for the last weeks.

## How many papers, and when?

There is no magic number, and we found no official guidance on it. A sensible plan is below. This is our suggestion, not a NECTA rule.

- **Months before the exam:** one untimed paper per subject every one or two weeks, plus topic questions.
- **Last two months:** timed mocks, about one per subject per week for your weakest subjects.
- **Last week:** do not start new papers for the first time. Read your mistakes log, rest and sleep.

Quality beats quantity. One paper marked well and reviewed is worth more than five papers rushed.

## What not to do

- **Do not memorise answers.** If you learn the answer to a 2018 question, you learn only that question. Learn the idea behind it.
- **Do not trust leaked papers or "sure" tips.** Cheating can lead to your result being cancelled, and anyone selling a paper may be scamming you. Use legal, school-approved material.
- **Do not skip marking.** A paper that is not marked teaches little.
- **Do not only do your favourite subjects.** Weak subjects give the biggest gains.
- **Do not practise only when tired.** Do papers when your mind is fresh.

## How can teachers and groups use them?

- A teacher can set a paper as a class test, mark it with the class and discuss common mistakes.
- A study group of three or four can each take one question, answer it alone, then explain the answer to the others. Explaining aloud shows what you do not yet understand.
- Groups can share a single mistakes log for common errors.
- Teachers can use the format booklet's topic weights to decide how much class time each topic needs, and the item reports to see where learners often lose marks.

## Common questions

### Can I pass just by doing past papers?

No. Past papers show you what to work on. You still need to learn the content from your syllabus, books and teachers.

### Do the same questions come back?

Do not count on it. Topics repeat, because the syllabus is the same, but the questions change.

### Where can I find official answers?

We did not find an official NECTA archive of marking schemes. Ask your teacher, and use the examiners' reports on NECTA's website for how marks were won and lost.

### Is it okay to use a past paper from another level?

Use your own level. A Form Four paper is not a good test for PSLE, and the formats differ.

## What to do next

1. Download the format booklet for your exam from NECTA and read the part for your hardest subject.
2. Choose one topic and do three questions on it, untimed.
3. Start a mistakes log today.
4. Ask a classmate to be your revision partner.

Small, steady practice builds confidence, and you can begin tonight.
