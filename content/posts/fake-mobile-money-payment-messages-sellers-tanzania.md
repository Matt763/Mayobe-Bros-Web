You packed the order with care. The buyer says "I have paid" and shows you a message. Then you wait, and the money never comes. Every seller fears that moment, and it hurts because the work was real. This post gives you a calm routine that makes it much harder for it to happen to you.

## The short answer

Never hand over goods because of a message or a screenshot. Hand them over only when you have seen the money in your own mobile money wallet or bank app.

- A message is only text. Anyone can type or edit one, so it proves nothing by itself.
- Open your own wallet, or dial your network's balance menu yourself, and check that the money is there.
- Do not rush because the buyer is in a hurry. Pressure is a warning sign.
- Never send money back to a caller or buyer because they ask you to.
- If something goes wrong, tell your network, report the scam message, and report to the police.

## Why a message is not payment

When a payment is real, the money is in your account. The message is only a notice about it. A notice can be copied, changed or sent by someone pretending to be the network. It can also be a screenshot, and a screenshot is only a picture.

So the check never changes: look at your own balance, in your own wallet, on your own phone. Do not tap a link inside a message to "confirm". Open the app or the menu yourself.

Jiji, the classifieds site, gives the same kind of warning in its safety tips. It says to watch for fake payment services and requests for fees, and to avoid anything that looks too good to be true. That page is written mainly for buyers, but the lesson works for sellers too: if you cannot verify a payment yourself, do not treat it as received.

## The tricks sellers meet

We could not find an official Tanzanian page that lists the exact scripts scammers use, so we will not claim to know which one is most common. What we can say is that these pressure points appear again and again when people talk about selling online. Treat each as a reason to slow down.

- **Pressure.** "I am late, please send the item now, the money is coming." A real buyer can wait two minutes while you check.
- **The screenshot.** The buyer sends a picture of a payment. It may be old, edited or from someone else's phone.
- **The message from "the network".** A text arrives that looks official but comes from an ordinary number or contains a link. Your network does not need you to release goods for it.
- **The "send it back" request.** Someone says they paid by mistake and asks you to return the money. If the money is not in your wallet, there is nothing to return, and you would be sending your own.
- **A deal that is too good.** A very large order from a stranger, with a request to pay extra fees or use an unusual transfer service, is a reason to stop.

Are these a big problem? Tanzania's regulator, TCRA, runs a public campaign against phone fraud called Sitapeliki. The Daily News reported in July 2025 that TCRA data showed mobile money fraud cases fell from 17,152 in March to 13,837 in June 2025. That is fewer, but still many. The same article advises people to change their PIN often and to check the recipient's details before sending money.

## A five-step check before you hand over goods

Use the same steps for every sale, even when the buyer is a friend of a friend. A routine feels rude only the first time.

1. **Say your rule early.** Write it in your chat or on your shop page: "I release goods after the money shows in my wallet." Honest buyers understand.
2. **Give the right number.** If you have a merchant or business number, give that. Check each digit before you send it.
3. **Check your own wallet.** Open your app or dial the network's menu yourself. Look at the balance and the transaction list. Do not use a link from the buyer.
4. **Match the amount and the name.** The full amount must be there, not less. If the name shown is odd, ask why.
5. **Only then hand over the goods.** If you are not sure, wait. A real payment will still be there in an hour.

## If someone asks you to send money

Never send money to a person you do not know. TCRA says this plainly in its public advice. It also says not to follow instructions from unknown callers, not to click links from people you do not know, and not to reply to prize or emergency stories.

Say a stranger calls and says they sent you money by mistake. They ask you to send it back at once. Do not do it from the phone call. Check your wallet first. If the money is really there, ask your network on 100 how to handle it. If it is not there, you have just been saved from a loss.

## If you have been tricked

It is not your fault that a scammer lied. Act quickly and calmly, in this order.

1. **Call your network on 100.** TCRA tells people to contact their service provider by dialling 100. Say what happened and give the time, number and amount. Yas says that for a wrong transaction you should call customer care on 100. Yas also says its self-reversal tool works only within 30 minutes of the transaction, so speed matters. We only opened Yas's page, so check with your own network what it can do.
2. **Report the scam message.** TCRA says to text the word "Fraud" to 15040. Its Swahili FAQ names the word "UTAPELI" for the same number, and Yas says "FRAUD". The word may change, so follow the instructions in the reply you get. As checked in November 2026, TCRA says reported numbers are passed to law enforcement agencies.
3. **Report to the police.** TCRA's FAQ says to report fraud at a police station, where you can get an RB number (the police report number) and an investigator.
4. **Keep proof.** Save messages, call times, numbers, receipts and your wallet statement. Do not delete the chat.
5. **Use the complaint route if you get no answer.** For complaints that are not crime, TCRA's FAQ says to complain to your provider first. If there is no reply within 30 days, or you are not happy with it, you can take it to TCRA with proof of your complaint. The same FAQ says mobile money services are supervised by the Bank of Tanzania.

TCRA's toll-free line is 0800 008272. Phone numbers can change, so check the TCRA website before you call.

## Safer ways to sell

- **Pay after inspection.** Jiji advises meeting in a public place or using payment on delivery, so the buyer inspects and pays when satisfied. For you as a seller, this means money changes hands while both people are present, and you can check your wallet on the spot.
- **Do not ship on a promise.** Jiji says it recommends no prepayments. If you must ship to another town, wait for confirmed money first.
- **Keep your own records.** Write each sale in a notebook: date, item, amount, the buyer's number. Our post on [simple business records](/post/business-ideas/startups/simple-business-records-small-traders-cash-book-tanzania/) shows an easy way.
- **Be honest in your own ads.** Clear listings bring fewer arguments. See our guide on how to [earn customer trust as a new online seller](/post/business-ideas/e-commerce/earn-customer-trust-new-online-seller-tanzania/).

## Protect your own SIM and PIN

Your wallet is only as safe as your PIN and your SIM.

- Never tell anyone your PIN. Jiji's safety page says the same about codes and passwords. No honest network worker, buyer or "agent" needs it.
- Change your PIN from time to time, as the Daily News article advises.
- TCRA says you can dial *106# and follow the steps to see the SIM cards registered in your name. If you see a number you do not know, ask your provider to remove it.
- Do not lend your phone with the wallet open.

## Common questions

### Can I trust a payment screenshot?

No. A screenshot is only a picture and can be old or edited. Trust only the balance and the transaction list in your own wallet or app.

### The buyer says the money is "pending". What should I do?

Wait. Tell the buyer politely that you release the goods after the money shows in your wallet. A real payment does not need you to hurry.

### Should I ever send money back?

Not on a caller's request. First confirm in your own wallet that the money arrived. If it did and you think it was a mistake, ask your network on 100 what to do.

### Is reporting worth it?

Yes. TCRA says reported numbers are passed on for investigation, and your report may help other sellers. It also gives your network and the police a record.

## What to do next

1. Write your payment rule in one sentence and put it in your shop chat or description.
2. Practise finding the transaction list in your wallet, so you can do it in seconds.
3. Save 100, 15040 and the TCRA line in your phone.
4. Share this routine with anyone who helps you sell.

Careful sellers are not unfriendly, they are the ones who are still in business next year. One small check keeps your hard work safe.
