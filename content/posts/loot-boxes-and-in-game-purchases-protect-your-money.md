You look at your phone and see a charge you did not expect. Your stomach drops. Then you remember your child was playing a game earlier. If this has happened to you, please do not be too hard on yourself, and please do not blame the child. These systems are built to make buying easy.

This guide explains how in-game buying works, how to add a check before any purchase on Google Play, and what you can try if a charge was a mistake. Everything below was checked on official pages in November 2026.

## The short answer

In-game purchases are things you buy with real money inside a game. A loot box is a purchase where you do not know what you will get until you open it. The safest step is to make Google Play ask for verification every time, and to agree a family rule about spending.

- Look for the labels "In-Game Purchases" and "Includes Random Items" before you install.
- Virtual coins and gems make real spending hard to track.
- On Google Play, open Payments and subscriptions, then Purchase verification, and choose the strictest option.
- Report a charge you did not make within 120 days, and do not expect a refund to be certain.
- The FTC, a United States body, gives advice here. We use it as consumer advice, not as Tanzanian law.

## What are in-game purchases and loot boxes?

The ESRB, a games rating body, explains a loot box as a locked chest of random virtual items. You can sometimes earn one by playing, or buy one with real money or with in-game currency. Usually you cannot see what is inside first. A similar idea is called gacha, named after capsule vending machines in Japan.

The ESRB lists the common kinds of in-game purchase:

- **In-game currency:** coins or gems that you buy with real money.
- **Pay-to-continue:** buying lives so you can keep playing without waiting.
- **Pay-to-win:** items or power-ups that speed up progress.
- **Cosmetics:** things that change how a character looks.
- **Expansions and season passes:** more content, added over weeks or months.
- **Playable characters:** new characters sold one by one or in packs.
- **Loot boxes:** random rewards, as described above.

Many games are free to install and make their money this way. Some people feel these purchases add fun, and some feel they push players to spend too much. Both views were raised at an FTC workshop. We do not take a side here. We only want you to know how it works.

## Why does the money go so fast?

Spending often feels small at each step, and the small steps pile up. Two things make it harder to see.

**Virtual currency hides the price.** The FTC says that when real money turns into gems or coins, children may find it hard to track what they spend. A bundle of coins feels like a game point, not like TSh.

**Random rewards hide the odds.** The FTC also says children may not understand their chances of getting the item they want. If a child wants one special item, ten or twenty tries can feel normal.

In a later alert, the FTC warns that adverts in children's games can also push spending, and that clicking some ads may trigger a payment without making that clear. Some games that say they are ad-free still show adverts.

Say you have a prepaid card with TSh 20,000 on it, and the card is saved on the phone. If each pack costs TSh 2,000, ten taps use the whole amount. This is only an example. We did not find prices for any particular game, and prices differ by game and by region.

This is not a sign that your child is careless. Games are designed to make the next tap easy.

## How do you spot purchases and random items before you install?

You can learn a lot in two minutes on the game page.

1. **Read the labels.** The ESRB uses "In-Game Purchases" and "In-Game Purchases (Includes Random Items)". It notes that older games may not show these labels, so search online for older or mobile games.
2. **Check the odds.** Google's Payments policy says games that sell random virtual items must clearly disclose the odds before the purchase and near the point of buying. The ESRB says Nintendo, Microsoft, Sony, Apple and Google require publishers to publish drop rates. These show the chance of each rarity level, not always every item.
3. **Read reviews.** The FTC suggests reading reviews and ratings, watching videos of the game being played, and looking at the game with your child.
4. **Check the age rating.** Our guide to [game age ratings and parental controls](/post/video-games-updates/game-age-ratings-esrb-pegi-and-parental-controls/) explains the labels.

If a game seems to be built around random paid items, you can simply choose another game.

## How do you turn on purchase verification on Google Play?

Google Play can ask for verification before it takes money. Google's help page gives these steps:

1. Open the Google Play app and tap your profile picture at the top right.
2. Tap Payments and subscriptions, then Purchase verification.
3. Choose Verification frequency (in some regions it is called Verification requirement).
4. Pick an option and confirm.

The options are:

| Option | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Always | You verify every purchase made through Google Play billing, including in-app purchases. This is the default. |
| Every 30 minutes | After one check, you can buy for 30 minutes without checking again (mobile only). |
| Never | Google Play does not ask for verification. |

For a phone that a child uses, choose Always. Google also says that for an app or game designed for ages 12 and under, verification is always required for every purchase through Play billing, even if your settings say otherwise.

Keep your own password, PIN or fingerprint private. Google's refund page says refunds are usually not given if you shared your account or payment details, or if you did not protect your account with authentication. Treat that as a strong reason to keep a lock on your phone and your Google account.

We could not confirm whether Google Play accepts mobile money or airtime billing in Tanzania, so this post does not cover them. Check the payment methods on your own Google Play account.

## What can you do if a charge was a mistake?

First, breathe. Then work through these steps.

1. **Check who made the charge.** Look at your Google Play order history and bank or card alerts.
2. **Decide which case it is.** Google separates two cases. If you did not buy it and nobody you know did, report it as unauthorised within 120 days. If a friend or family member bought something by mistake on your account, ask for a refund instead of reporting it as unauthorised.
3. **Ask for a refund.** Use Google's help article "Request a refund on Google Play". Google also says that contacting the app developer directly is often the fastest route, because most apps come from third-party developers.
4. **Be ready for a no.** Whether you get money back depends on what you bought, when and how you paid, and where you live. Google and the developer decide. We cannot promise any result.
5. **Change the settings now.** Turn on purchase verification and consider a Play content restriction, so this does not repeat.

If you believe someone took money from your bank card or mobile money wallet without your consent, contact your bank or mobile money provider too, using the number printed on your card or in your official app.

## Make a family rule that does not rely on tools

Tools help, but the ESRB says families should agree house rules together before switching on controls, and that parents should follow the same rules. It compares spending limits to an allowance.

Here is a plain way to try it:

- Decide together how much, if anything, can be spent in a month.
- Agree that a child always asks before pressing a buy button.
- Tell your child that if a mistake happens, you want to hear about it quickly and you will not be angry. A quick report gives you the best chance to ask for a refund.
- Look at the game together once a week for a few minutes.

A child who feels safe telling you about a mistake is your best protection.

## Common questions

### Are loot boxes the same as gambling?

We cannot give a legal answer. People disagree, and rules differ by country. We did not open any Tanzanian source on this, so we make no claim about Tanzanian law. This post does not promote loot boxes or gambling-style games.

### Will Google give me my money back?

Maybe, maybe not. Google's page says eligibility varies. Report an unauthorised charge within 120 days, and contact the developer too.

### Is a game that is free to install really free?

Not always. The install may be free, while coins, lives, characters or random items cost real money. The "In-Game Purchases" label tells you the game sells things.

### Does verification stop all charges?

It stops purchases through Google Play billing unless the check is passed. It does not replace talking with your child, and it does not cover other ways of paying that a game might offer outside Play.

## What to do next

1. Open Google Play now and set Purchase verification to Always on every phone your child uses.
2. Look at the order history for the last few weeks, so you know where you stand.
3. Pick one family rule about spending and say it out loud tonight.
4. Read the ESRB blog and the FTC alerts listed below for more advice.

You noticed the problem, and that is already the first step to fixing it.
