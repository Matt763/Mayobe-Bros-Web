Fertiliser is one of the biggest costs for a Tanzanian farmer, and the planting season is when prices and supply matter most. The government has kept its fertiliser subsidy (*ruzuku ya mbolea*) for the 2026/27 season, and the Tanzania Fertiliser Regulatory Authority (TFRA) is warning traders who overcharge. This post explains how the system works, what the official "indicative price" means, and what to do if someone asks you for more. The facts were checked in November 2026.

## The short answer

- The 2026/27 budget measures include continuing to settle fertiliser subsidy claims, so that supplies are adequate this season.
- Subsidised fertiliser is sold through a digital system that TFRA runs. Agents must sell at the government's indicative prices.
- TFRA published its guide for running the 2026/27 subsidy on 15 October 2026. Ask your village or ward agriculture officer for the rules that apply to you.
- A trader who sells subsidised fertiliser above the indicative price, or outside the system, can lose the licence and face legal action.
- You can report overcharging to TFRA on 0800110154 or 0800110153 (the numbers listed on its website).

## How the subsidy works

The subsidy lowers the price a farmer pays for fertiliser such as DAP, urea and CAN. The government pays part of the cost, and the shop sells to the farmer at a lower, fixed price.

According to TFRA's Lake Zone manager, quoted by Daily News on 9 July 2026, the digital subsidy system was set up in 2022. Its aim is to get subsidised fertiliser to farmers on time, at reasonable prices and in a way that can be checked. Registered agents must record sales in that system and sell at the government's indicative price.

Daily News also reported a figure that shows the size of the help. A regional official said the subsidy lowers fertiliser prices by an average of Sh63,000 to Sh65,000 per bag, while a 50 kg bag of DAP costs about Sh158,000 at the market price. That is a report of what officials said, not a price list. The price at your local shop depends on the product, the pack size and the season, so check the current list before you buy.

## What an "indicative price" is

An indicative price (*bei elekezi*) is the price the government expects a farmer to pay at the shop. TFRA publishes these lists on its website for each product, for wholesale and for retail packs of 5, 10, 25 and 50 kg. On the page for 2026 prices, the most recent entries we saw were dated 29 and 30 September 2026.

The list is the thing to compare with the price you are asked to pay. Prices also differ by region because transport costs differ, so look at the list for your product and your area.

## Why the rules are being enforced now

Several things happened in 2026 that put fertiliser in the news.

**World prices rose.** The Citizen reported on 4 June 2026 that global urea prices rose from US$509 per tonne in February to US$658 in May, about 29%. Higher fuel costs and supply problems were named as causes.

**The government said supply is enough.** At that time, Agriculture Minister Daniel Chongolo told farmers not to panic and said fertiliser was available. The Citizen quoted TFRA's Director-General saying importers were expected to bring in about 300,000 tonnes between July and September. In July, the minister said 248,617 tonnes of various fertilisers were in stock, with another 313,800 tonnes expected from July to September.

**Distribution started early.** On 4 October, Daily News reported the minister saying fertiliser was reaching sales centres across the country. The national target for food crops was 150,000 tonnes, and 33,524 tonnes of DAP had been received and distributed so far. In Katavi alone, the plan was 5,000 tonnes: 2,000 of DAP, 2,500 of urea and 500 of CAN.

**Overcharging was found.** In Simiyu, TFRA and the police acted on farmers' reports in July. Daily News reported that one trader who was not a registered agent was arrested for selling urea at Sh150,000 when the guide price was Sh86,250, and another product at Sh120,000 when the guide price was Sh98,856. A registered agent in the same area was charged with breaking his subsidy agreement after officials found fertiliser in his store that was not recorded in the system. The article says TFRA promised strict legal action but does not list fines.

Daily News also reported that the Vice-President told traders in Ruvuma's Madaba district that government indicative prices must be observed, after farmers complained of rising prices.

## How to buy safely

These steps follow what the authorities say they are enforcing.

1. **Buy from a registered agent.** TFRA keeps a list of licensed dealers and registered fertilisers at fis.tfra.go.tz, which it links from its announcement page.
2. **Ask for the indicative price.** Know the price for your product and pack size before you go. TFRA publishes the lists under "Publications".
3. **Expect the sale to go through the system.** TFRA says agents must sell through the digital system. A seller who says "no records, cash only" is a warning sign.
4. **Ask for a receipt.** It is your proof if you need to complain.
5. **Check the bag.** Look for an intact seal and a clear product name. Minister Chongolo has warned about fake and substandard inputs, and members of parliament in Songwe said fake seeds and fertiliser are still common.

If you are not sure how you qualify for the subsidy or how to register, go to your village or ward agriculture extension officer. We could not read the full 2026/27 guide, so we cannot list its exact registration steps or limits. Do not pay anyone for "registration"; ask the officer or TFRA first.

## Common questions

### Is the subsidy ending?

No. Daily News reported on 18 June 2026 that the Finance Minister's 2026/27 budget measures include continuing to settle fertiliser subsidy claims so supplies are adequate. The Agriculture Minister has also said subsidies will continue.

### What should I do if a shop asks for more than the indicative price?

Do not argue at the counter. Note the shop name, the product, the price and the date, and keep the receipt if you have one. Then report it. TFRA lists the toll-free numbers 0800110154 and 0800110153, the phone +255 22 2861939, and the email info@tfra.go.tz. It also has a complaints page on its website. Your ward or district agriculture office can also help.

### Why do prices differ between towns?

Transport costs are part of the price, and TFRA's indicative lists cover different products and pack sizes. A price that is higher than the list for your product is the problem, not a price that is simply different from another town's.

### Is it only for maize farmers?

The reports we read mention food crops, with a national target of 150,000 tonnes of fertiliser for food crops. Ask your extension officer what applies to your crop.

## What to do next

1. Find your product and pack size on TFRA's indicative price list before you buy.
2. Speak to your village or ward agriculture officer about registration for 2026/27.
3. Buy only from a registered agent, and keep the receipt.
4. Report overcharging to TFRA with the date, shop and price.
