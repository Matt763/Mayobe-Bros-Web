Many of us remember the first time we shared a screen with a brother, a cousin or a friend. One person held the controller while the others shouted advice. That small joy has a long history, and it began with people who simply liked to tinker.

This post tells that story in short, using museum sources. It also answers a question people search all the time: what was the first video game?

## The short answer: there is no single first video game

The answer depends on what you call a video game. If a game needs a screen, the answer is different from one that needs a computer or a home console. Museums list several early candidates, and they do not all agree on every year.

- 1940: Nimatron, a machine that played the game Nim.
- 1950: Bertie the Brain, a machine that played tic-tac-toe.
- 1958: Tennis for Two, shown on an oscilloscope.
- The early 1960s: Spacewar!, on a computer at MIT.
- 1972: the Magnavox Odyssey, the first home game system, and Pong in arcades.
- 1983 to 1989: Nintendo's home and handheld consoles.
- 1994: the first PlayStation, launched in Japan.

## What came before screens? Machines that played games (1940 to 1958)

In 1940, Westinghouse built a machine called Nimatron for the World's Fair. It was an electro-mechanical machine that played Nim, a simple game of taking turns to remove objects. The Strong National Museum of Play lists it as one early answer to the "first game" question.

In 1950, a custom-built machine called Bertie the Brain played tic-tac-toe against visitors at the Canadian National Exposition.

In 1958, scientists at Brookhaven National Laboratory made a tennis game for an oscilloscope, a screen that normally shows electric signals. It was called Tennis for Two, and it used custom controllers. It was shown at a public open house, and the Strong museum says an improved version followed the next year.

Think about that for a moment. Ordinary visitors to a science open day could play a simple tennis game made by scientists.

## Why do the dates differ between sources?

Sources count different things. One may date a game from the day it was first shown. Another may use the day it was finished, or the day people could buy it. Tennis for Two is a good example. The Strong museum says it was shown at a public open house in 1958, and that an improved version followed the next year. Both dates tell a true part of the story.

The same care applies to the word "first". A machine that plays a game, a game on a screen, a game on a shared computer, a game in a bar and a game in your living room are all different firsts. When you read a claim that something was "the first video game", ask which of these it means.

## Spacewar! and the campus years: the early 1960s

Next came computers at universities. The Strong museum says Spacewar! was made in 1963 by MIT students led by Steve Russell. Two players flew dueling spaceships on a digital computer, and the game spread to other computer centres.

The Computer History Museum says Spacewar! "came on the scene in 1961". The two museums give different years, so the safe way to say it is the early 1960s. The Computer History Museum also says it helped spark interest in programming on college campuses.

Games and computing grew up side by side.

## Arcades and the first home consoles (1971 to 1972)

Around 1971, Nolan Bushnell and Nutting Associates released Computer Space, an arcade game where two ships try to destroy each other in outer space. The Strong museum says its controls were clumsy and it did not sell well.

In 1972, the Magnavox Odyssey arrived. The Strong museum calls it the first home video game system, an invention of Ralph Baer. It used analogue circuits. The Computer History Museum says Baer had built a prototype called the Brown Box in 1966.

Also in 1972, Atari's Pong brought video games to the public in a big way. The Computer History Museum says Allan Alcorn built the first prototype for Nolan Bushnell, and it was put in a bar in Sunnyvale, California, in 1972. It was so popular that it broke after it got jammed with coins.

We did not find a source that settles who borrowed ideas from whom between the Odyssey and Pong, so we leave that question alone.

The Computer History Museum calls the Atari 2600 "the most successful console of the 1970s and early 1980s". It names Pitfall! and Adventure among the games made for it.

## Nintendo and the home console boom (1983 to 1989)

Nintendo's own history page gives these years:

- **1983:** the Family Computer System launched. Outside Japan it is known as the Nintendo Entertainment System.
- **1985:** Super Mario Bros. went on sale for that system.
- **1989:** the Game Boy handheld launched.

That page lists years only, not exact dates. The Game Boy was a handheld, so the game came with the player. Home consoles and handhelds are two different ways of playing, and Nintendo's page shows both in this short span of years.

## PlayStation and the next step (1994 onward)

Sony's PlayStation site says its story starts when the original PlayStation launched in Japan in 1994. The racing series Gran Turismo began on that console in 1997, and the PlayStation 5 launched in 2020.

We have not source-checked other later parts of the story, such as phones, other console makers or sales numbers, so we leave them out. This post stops where our sources stop. For readers who want to continue, the museum pages in the list below are a good next step, and the history of later years is a fine topic for a future article.

## Can you play old games today?

Some old games come back as official re-releases and collections from the companies that own them. If you want to try one, use the official store of the console or a trusted app store. Do not download copies from unknown sites. Those are often illegal, and they can carry harmful software.

If you pick a game for a young player, check its age label first. Our guide to [game age ratings and parental controls](/post/video-games-updates/game-age-ratings-esrb-pegi-and-parental-controls/) explains how.

## Why does this history matter?

It shows that games came from curiosity. Scientists, students and engineers tried things for the pleasure of seeing them work. A simple idea, such as two paddles and a ball, grew into a world industry over a few decades. Every big change started with someone who made something small and let other people try it.

## Common questions

### What was the first video game?

There is no single answer. The Strong National Museum of Play lists Nimatron (1940), Bertie the Brain (1950), Tennis for Two (1958), Spacewar! (early 1960s), the Odyssey and Pong (1972) as candidates. Your answer depends on your definition.

### Was Pong the first video game?

Pong was not the first game. The Strong museum says it became a widespread hit and brought video games to the public for the first time. Earlier games existed in labs and universities.

### Which was the first home console?

The Strong museum names the Magnavox Odyssey of 1972 as the first home video game system.

### When did PlayStation start?

Sony says the original PlayStation launched in Japan in 1994.
