You work long days. You open the shop early, you greet people, and you hope they come back. Many small owners never get time to sit down and ask one plain question: who is actually buying from me?

You do not need money or a research company to find out. A notebook, a pen and a few honest conversations are enough to start. This post shows a simple way to do it.

## The short answer

Start with the people who already buy from you. Write down who they are, ask a few of them five simple questions, check what other sellers charge, and then write one sentence that describes your best customer. That sentence helps you decide what to stock, what to say and where to spend your money.

- Look at who walks in or messages you now, before you guess about strangers.
- Ask ten real customers the same five questions.
- Look at what other sellers offer and charge near you.
- Write one sentence about your customer and keep it where you can see it.
- Do this again every few months, because people change.

## Why guessing about customers costs money

Say you sell school bags. You believe your buyers are university students, so you pay for a big banner near a campus. Then you notice that most of your sales come from parents who shop in the evening on their way home. The banner did not reach them. The money is gone.

Nobody is clever enough to guess every time. Government small business guides in the United States and Australia both start with the same advice: learn about your market and your customers before you spend on promotion. The US Small Business Administration says research helps you find customers and lowers risk. Those guides are written for other countries, so we use them only for the method. All the examples here are in Tanzanian shillings and are made up to show the idea.

Good research does not have to be expensive. The SBA page describes two kinds. One is reading data other people have already collected, such as population or income figures. It saves time but may not describe your own street. The other is asking customers directly, which gives a closer picture but takes more time. For a small shop, asking directly is usually the better start, because the people in front of you are the ones who pay.

## Who is already buying from you? Four simple ways to group customers

Business Queensland, an Australian government service, lists four common ways to group customers. You can use the same four ideas with a notebook. You do not need long forms.

1. **Who they are.** Age, work, whether they are students, parents, drivers or office workers. If you sell to other businesses, think about the kind of business.
2. **What they care about.** Some people want the cheapest price. Some want good quality. Some want a shop they can trust to be open when it says it will.
3. **How they buy.** How often do they come? Do they buy one item or many? Do they pay cash or by mobile money? Do they ask on WhatsApp first?
4. **Where they live or work.** Are they on your street, in the next neighbourhood, or in another town?

For one week, make a small table in your notebook with a row for each customer you remember. Do not write full names if you do not need them. Write what you notice, for example "mother, buys uniforms in January, lives nearby, always bargains".

After a week, look for patterns. Which kind of customer comes most often? Which kind spends most? Which kind is easy to reach? Business Queensland suggests comparing groups by size, how much money they bring, how easy they are to reach and how steady they are over time. You do not need numbers you cannot get. A rough feeling from your own notes is a fine place to start.

## Five questions to ask ten customers

Pick ten customers. Choose some regulars and some who bought only once. Ask each person the same five questions, in a friendly voice, when they are not in a hurry. Say you want to serve them better.

1. **How did you first hear about my shop?** This tells you which of your efforts people notice, such as a sign, a friend or a phone message.
2. **What made you buy today?** You may hear price, closeness, trust or habit.
3. **What do you wish I sold or did differently?** This can show a gap you can fill.
4. **Where else do you buy this?** It shows who your real competitors are, from the customer's side.
5. **What would make you tell a friend about us?** Listen for the small things, like a fair price, a kind greeting or fast service.

Write down the answers on the same day, in the customer's own words as closely as you can. Do not argue with an answer. If someone says you are too expensive, the useful thing is that they said it, not whether you agree.

Business Queensland also suggests following up after a big sale to ask if the customer is happy. Another idea on that page is to ask a friend to contact your business and tell you honestly how it felt. That friend can show you things you no longer notice, such as a slow reply or an unclear price.

A few cautions:

- Never promise a gift for answering. You want honest words, not polite ones.
- If someone looks tired or rushed, thank them and move on.
- Ten answers will not prove anything for the whole city. They are a first look. Treat them as clues.

## Look at what others sell and what people pay

You are never the only choice. Your customer also sees the shop next door, the stall at the market and the seller on social media.

Business Queensland says your prices should compare well with other businesses, and it suggests three ways to check: look online, read flyers, or make a phone enquiry. In many places in Tanzania you can do the same thing by walking along the street, looking at price tags and asking a seller politely about a product.

Write down three or four sellers near you. For each one note:

- What they sell that you also sell
- Their price for two or three common items
- What they do better than you
- What they do not offer

The same page gives a useful warning. If your price is much higher than others, customers need to see a clear reason, such as better quality or a guarantee you really keep. If it is much lower, you might be giving away profit and hiding the value you offer.

Prices change. The numbers you write today will be old in a few months, so put the date next to them.

Here is an example with made-up numbers. Say you sell cooking oil in small bottles. Three sellers near you charge TSh 2,000, TSh 2,200 and TSh 2,500 for the same size, and you charge TSh 2,300. You are in the middle. If your oil is the same as theirs, the price alone will not make you stand out. You may decide to stand out in another way, such as always having stock, or opening an hour earlier.

## Turn your answers into one sentence about your customer

Now put it together. Read your notes from the week and your ten conversations. Then complete this sentence:

"My best customer is a ___ who lives or works ___, buys ___ because ___, and finds me through ___."

An example, made up for this post: "My best customer is a parent who lives within ten minutes' walk, buys school items in January and July because she wants fair prices and quick service, and finds me through other parents."

This one sentence is more useful than it looks. It tells you:

- What to keep in stock before school terms
- Where to put a sign or notice
- What to say in a phone message
- What to skip, because it does not help that customer

If you find two different kinds of customer, you may write two sentences. Try to keep it to two. Trying to please everyone usually means pleasing nobody well.

Research does not guarantee sales. It lowers the chance that you spend money in the wrong place. What happens after that depends on your products, your prices, your service and many things you do not control.

## Common questions

### Do I need a survey form or an app?

No. For a small shop, a notebook and a friendly chat work well. Forms can help later if you have hundreds of customers, but they are not needed to start.

### What if customers do not want to answer?

Keep it short, ask at a calm moment, and thank them even if they say no. Two or three honest answers are still worth more than a guess.

### What if the answers upset me?

That is normal. A hard answer stings, but it is also free advice. Write it down, wait a day, and then ask what small change you could make.

### How often should I do this?

Once at the start, then again every few months or when something changes, such as a new competitor, a new product or a new season.

## What to do next

1. Today, start a customer notebook. Write down who bought and what you notice for one week.
2. This week, ask ten customers the five questions and write their answers the same day.
3. Walk past three nearby sellers and note their prices and what they offer. Write the date.
4. Finish the "my best customer" sentence and put it somewhere you will see it every morning.

You already have what the big companies pay for: people who trust you enough to come back. Listening to them is a good first step.
