For many of us, WhatsApp holds family chats, school groups, customers and money talk. If someone else gets into your account, they can pretend to be you and ask your contacts for money. The good news is that three habits stop most takeovers, and they take about ten minutes to learn.

WhatsApp changes its menus often. This post describes settings by name and not by exact taps, as checked in November 2026. For the current steps on your phone, use the WhatsApp Help Center.

## The short answer

Your WhatsApp account is tied to your phone number and a 6-digit code. Anyone who gets that code can move your account to their phone. So never share it. Then switch on two-step verification, so the code alone is not enough, and look at your linked devices from time to time.

- Never share the 6-digit registration code. WhatsApp says never to share it, "not even friends or family."
- Turn on two-step verification and keep its password or PIN private.
- Check the linked devices list and remove any you do not know.
- If your account is taken, get it back first. Reporting it first may lead to a ban, according to WhatsApp.
- Be suspicious of anyone who asks you to pay, send money or share a code to "keep" or "unlock" your account.

## Why is the 6-digit code the key to your account?

When you set up WhatsApp on a phone, it sends a 6-digit code by SMS or phone call to confirm that the number is yours. This is called the registration code. Whoever enters it can register that number on their own phone.

That is why scammers try to get it from you. They do not need to break into anything. They only need you to hand it over.

Here is a clearly marked example of how it can go. Say you receive a message from a friend's account: "Sorry, I typed your number by mistake and a code came to you. Please send it back." Moments later your own phone buzzes with a WhatsApp code you did not ask for. The message looks harmless and the friend seems real. But the code was sent because the scammer tried to register your number. If you send it, they own your account. The friend's account may already have been taken over.

What to do instead:

- Do not reply with the code. Not to a friend, a relative, a "support agent" or anyone else.
- If you get a code you did not ask for, ignore it. Do not click links around it.
- Call your friend on a normal phone call to ask what happened.

## What is two-step verification?

Two-step verification is an optional setting that adds a second layer of security. WhatsApp says it helps protect your account by asking for more than the SMS code. Even if someone learns your 6-digit code, they still cannot register your number without the second step.

When you switch it on, WhatsApp's help page says you can choose an additional verification factor, such as a phone number, a password or an email address. The page also describes a passkey: it links your account to your phone's own security, such as your fingerprint, face or screen lock, and can let you sign in faster when two-step verification is on.

WhatsApp has been changing how two-step verification works, and we could not confirm every detail of the change on its help pages in November 2026. You may see a 6-digit PIN, a password, an email option or a passkey, depending on your app version. The exact names differ by phone and version. That is why we describe the idea and not the taps.

## How do I turn on two-step verification?

The steps differ by phone, so follow this order of ideas and use the names you see in your app:

1. Open WhatsApp and go to Settings.
2. Open the Account area and look for the setting called Two-step verification (it may also appear under Privacy or a "Passkeys" option, depending on your version).
3. Choose to turn it on and follow the screens. You may be asked to create a password or PIN, depending on your version.
4. Add an email address if the app offers it. This helps you reset the PIN if you forget it. Use an email account that only you can open.
5. Save your password in a safe place. A trusted password manager or a paper note kept at home are both better than the notes app on a shared phone.
6. If your phone offers a passkey, consider setting one up, so you can sign in with your fingerprint, face or screen lock.

Tips that apply to any version:

- Choose something that is not your birth year, your phone number or "123456".
- Never tell the password to anyone. A WhatsApp page says to set a secret PIN and never share it with anyone to stop someone stealing your account. No real WhatsApp helper will ask for it.
- Do not tap a link in a message that says "verify your two-step verification." Go into the app yourself.

Check the WhatsApp Help Center for the up-to-date steps on your phone.

## How do I check my linked devices?

WhatsApp lets you use your account on other devices, such as a laptop or tablet. These show up in a list called Linked devices. If someone else has linked their device to your account, they may be able to see your messages as they arrive.

Every few weeks:

1. Open WhatsApp Settings and find Linked devices.
2. Look at each device on the list.
3. Remove any you do not know or no longer use.

Do the same after you borrow a computer or have your phone repaired.

Never scan a QR code someone sends you in a chat. A QR code scanned to link a device gives that device access to your account.

## What are the signs that someone has taken over your account?

- You are suddenly signed out and WhatsApp asks you to register your number again.
- You get a 6-digit code you did not ask for, again and again.
- Friends say they received strange messages from you, or requests for money.
- Messages appear as sent that you did not send.
- You see a device in the Linked devices list that is not yours.
- You cannot sign in, and the app says the number is registered on another phone.

Not every sign means a takeover. A new phone or a bad network can also cause some of these. If you see two or more, act as if your account is at risk.

## How do I get my account back?

WhatsApp's help page on recovering a compromised account gives this order. Follow it before you do anything else.

1. Open WhatsApp and choose the option to log back in, then continue. If asked, enter your full phone number, with the country code, such as +255 for Tanzania.
2. Enter the 6-digit code that WhatsApp sends you by SMS or phone call. Only you should see this code. Do not share it with anyone while you do this.
3. If two-step verification is on and you do not know the password or PIN, WhatsApp says to wait 7 days and try again.
4. Once you are in, WhatsApp says re-registering logs out everyone else, including all linked devices. Even so, open the Linked devices list and remove any device you do not recognise.
5. Turn on two-step verification now, if it was off, and choose a new password.
6. Tell family and friends in another way, such as by calling them, that someone may have pretended to be you. Ask them not to send money or codes.

WhatsApp also warns that if you report your account as compromised, you could lose access to your account or data. That is why it says to try recovering the account first.

If your SIM card was lost or you no longer receive messages on your number, contact your mobile network and ask about replacing the SIM. Do this only at an official shop of your network, with your own ID.

## How do I spot other WhatsApp scams?

Account takeover is one kind of trap. Others include fake job offers, romance, investment and impersonation scams. The warning signs are common to all of them: pressure to act fast, a request for money or codes, and a story that cannot be checked. If you want to see how job scams work, read about [fake job offers in Tanzania](/post/jobs-and-internships/fake-job-offers-tanzania-warning-signs-never-pay/).

If someone, even a contact, asks for money through WhatsApp, call them on a saved number first. For voice and video tricks, see [AI voice and video scams: how to check before you send money](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/ai-voice-and-video-scams-how-to-check/).

## Common questions

### Will WhatsApp ever ask me for my 6-digit code?

No. WhatsApp's own guidance says you should never share your registration code with anyone, including friends and family.

### I got a code I did not ask for. What should I do?

Do not share it, and do not tap any link that came with it. Someone may be trying to register your number. Turn on two-step verification now, if it is off, and check your linked devices.

### I forgot my two-step verification PIN or password. What now?

Use the reset option in the app, which may involve the email address you added. If you cannot reset it, WhatsApp says to wait 7 days before trying again.

### Does re-registering delete my chats?

WhatsApp says past conversations stay on your phone, and the person trying to take over cannot read them. Still, keep backups according to the options in your app, and check the Help Center for the latest details.

### Should I report the hacker to WhatsApp?

Try to recover your account first. WhatsApp says reporting a compromised account may lead to losing access to it. You can still report the matter to the police if you lost money. In Tanzania, you can report scam numbers to TCRA by SMS to 15040 and follow the automatic reply.

## What to do next

1. Open WhatsApp now and check whether two-step verification is on.
2. Look at your Linked devices list and remove any device you do not know.
3. Tell your family and friends the rule: nobody ever sends a 6-digit code to anyone.
4. Save the WhatsApp Help Center address, so you can check current steps if the menus change.

A few minutes today can protect your chats, your contacts and your money. You are doing something good for the people who trust you.
