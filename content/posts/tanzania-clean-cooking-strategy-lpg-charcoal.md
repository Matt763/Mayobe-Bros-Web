Most Tanzanian households still cook on firewood or charcoal. The government has a ten-year plan to change that, and in 2026 it has been in the news again: a national conference in Arusha, new figures on how many people have switched, and a call for billions of shillings to pay for it. This post explains what the National Clean Cooking Strategy says, what has changed for households so far, and how to compare cooking fuels for your own kitchen. The facts were checked in November 2026.

In Swahili the topic is often called *nishati safi ya kupikia*, which means clean energy for cooking.

## The short answer

- The National Clean Cooking Strategy (2024 to 2034) aims for 80% of people to use clean cooking energy by 2034.
- Officials say access has risen from 6.9% in 2021 to 28.6%. Sources give that figure for slightly different dates (2025 or March 2026), so treat it as "about 29%".
- The state is helping with half the price of 452,445 LPG starter packs and most of the price of 200,000 improved cookstoves, and has removed some taxes on clean cooking products.
- Firewood and charcoal are banned in large institutions. There is no ban for ordinary homes in the sources we read.
- The plan is short of money. Officials say it needs nearly Sh4.6 trillion and cannot rely on the government budget alone.

## What the strategy is

The National Clean Cooking Strategy covers 2024 to 2034. According to a press release from the United Nations Capital Development Fund (UNCDF) after the National Clean Cooking Conference in Arusha on 10 and 11 June 2026, the government is using policy changes, financing, community work and private companies. The tools named include LPG subsidies, lower electricity tariffs and tax waivers on clean cooking equipment.

The Citizen reported on 29 July 2026 that the ten-year plan needs nearly Sh4.6 trillion, or about US$1.8 billion, with most of it going to infrastructure such as LPG terminals and biogas plants. Less than a third of the US$800 million pledged under the Tanzania Energy Compact had been raised, a gap of more than US$560 million. The Ministry of Energy's Director of Clean Cooking, Nolasco Roman Mlay, said the target cannot be reached through public funding alone.

## What "clean" means

The World Health Organization (WHO) treats these as clean at the point of use: solar, electricity, biogas, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), natural gas, alcohol fuels and biomass stoves that meet its emission targets. It discourages kerosene and advises against unprocessed coal. Firewood and ordinary charcoal fires and stoves are not on the clean list.

The reason is health. The WHO says household air pollution was linked to about 2.9 million premature deaths worldwide in 2021, including more than 309,000 children under five. It also warns that poorly ventilated homes can have indoor fine-particle levels 100 times higher than acceptable. The Citizen's report quotes officials saying more than 33,000 premature deaths a year in Tanzania are linked to indoor air pollution.

## What has changed for households

The main changes reported in 2026 are these.

**Subsidised LPG starter packs.** Officials told The Citizen that 452,445 LPG starter packs carry a 50% subsidy. We could not find a public, step-by-step guide for an ordinary household to apply, so ask the Ministry of Energy or your local government office how packs are given out in your area.

**Improved cookstoves.** An 85% subsidy covers 200,000 improved cookstoves, according to the same report.

**Tax changes.** Some taxes on clean cooking products have been removed. The reports do not list which products, so check at the shop or with the seller.

**Institutions.** Firewood and charcoal are banned in large institutions such as big schools and hospitals, so their kitchens must use cleaner fuel. UNCDF says the World Food Programme is bringing clean cooking technology into 36 schools, and UNCDF's CookFund has supported about 100 institutions.

**Private effort.** Oryx Gas and the Doris Mollel Foundation have distributed about 24,000 clean cooking solutions, according to The Citizen. UNCDF says its CookFund has helped more than 413,000 households (about 1.94 million people) and that the European Union has committed 30 million euros.

None of this means charcoal is illegal for your home. Charcoal is still how most households cook. The aim is to make gas, electricity and better stoves easier to buy and cheaper to use.

## How to compare fuels for your own kitchen

The best fuel is the one you can afford to use every day. A cheap starter pack does not help if the refill is too costly. Here is a simple way to compare, using made-up numbers to show the method.

*Example only:* Say your family buys one sack of charcoal each week and one 6 kg gas refill costs the same as two sacks. If a refill lasts you three weeks, then three weeks of charcoal costs three sacks, while one refill costs two sacks. In this made-up case gas is cheaper. If a refill lasted only one week, charcoal would be cheaper.

To do this with real prices:

1. Write down what you spend on charcoal or firewood in a normal month.
2. Find the current price of the refill at a licensed dealer, and ask how long one lasts for a family of your size and the foods you cook.
3. Add the one-time cost of the stove and regulator.
4. Compare the monthly cost, and then think about smoke, time spent collecting wood and your health.

We have not given a gas price here. Fuel and gas prices change, and we could not confirm a current official price for a cooking-gas cylinder. For petrol and diesel, see [how the EWURA price caps work](/post/tanzania-news/tanzania-fuel-security-measures-navigating-current-challenges/).

## Safety tips

- Buy gas equipment from licensed dealers, not from a stranger.
- Cook in a place with fresh air. Poor ventilation is the main danger of smoke.
- Keep children away from fires, stoves and fuel. The WHO notes that kerosene swallowed by accident is a leading cause of poisoning in young children, and that cooking energy use is linked to many severe burns.
- If a cylinder smells of gas or looks damaged, stop using it and take it back to the seller.

## Common questions

### Is charcoal banned in Tanzania?

Not for ordinary homes, based on what we found. The reports describe a ban on firewood and charcoal in large institutions. The national target is to move people to clean energy by 2034, mostly through subsidies, tax changes and building supply.

### How many people use clean cooking now?

Officials say roughly three in ten people. The Citizen dates the latest figure to 2025, and UNCDF to March 2026. The starting points differ too: UNCDF counts from 2022 and The Citizen from 2021. Treat it as an estimate.

### Does gas make a small cook-shop or restaurant cheaper to run?

It depends on prices and on how long a cylinder lasts for you. Use the comparison method above with your real costs, because we have no verified figures on savings for businesses.

### Where does the money come from?

The government, development partners and private companies. The European Union has committed 30 million euros, and the plan still has a large funding gap.

## What to do next

1. Work out your monthly cooking-fuel cost with the steps above.
2. Ask your local government or the Ministry of Energy how the subsidised LPG packs and improved stoves are given out where you live.
3. Buy only from licensed dealers and check any claimed discount with the seller.
4. If you run a school, clinic or hostel, find out if the institution rules apply to you.
