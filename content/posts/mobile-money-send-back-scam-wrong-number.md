Your phone buzzes. A message says money has arrived. A minute later, a call or another text says, "Sorry, that was my mistake, please send it back. I am in trouble." You want to be kind. Most of us would. That kindness is exactly what this trick uses, so first, know this: if you fell for it, or almost did, you are not foolish.

## The short answer

Do not send anything back because of a message alone. Messages can be faked, and a real sender can get their money back through their own operator. First check your own balance through your operator's official channel, not through the text. If the money is not really in your balance, there is nothing to return.

- Never give your PIN to anyone, for any reason.
- Do not act on a text that only looks like it came from customer service.
- Do not rush. Scammers need you to hurry.
- If a real sender made a mistake, they can ask their own operator for a reversal.
- Report the number and keep the messages.

## How the send-back trick works

TCRA's Consumer Consultative Council (TCRA-CCC) described this trick in Tanzanian press in 2017 (The Citizen, 30 January 2017, updated in March 2021). It goes like this:

1. A text arrives that says money was sent to your account by mistake. Nothing may have been sent at all. The text only looks like a real mobile money alert.
2. A second text follows. It is made to look like it comes from the network's customer service. It asks you to return the money.
3. If you send your own money, it is gone. The person at the other end is a scammer, not someone who made a mistake.

The council urged users not to respond or act on such messages, even when the number looks familiar. Its advice was to call that number from a different phone to confirm who is really writing.

The trick is old, and TCRA has run public campaigns against mobile money fraud in later years too. For example, TCRA's page of 4 August 2022 asks people to check both the message and the number that asks for money before any transaction. We do not claim here that the trick is more or less common today than it used to be, because we could not confirm current numbers for this particular scam.

## Checks to make before you send anything

Stop and run through this short list. It takes two minutes and can save you a week's wages.

1. **Check your balance the official way.** Use your operator's own menu, app or customer service, not a link or number given in the message. If the money is not in your balance, the message was fake.
2. **Look at the sender.** A real alert comes from your operator's usual sender name. A message from an ordinary phone number that talks like a bank is a warning sign.
3. **Never share your PIN.** No operator, agent or "customer service" needs it to take money back.
4. **Call the person from a different phone** if you know them, as the council advised. If you do not know them, do not send money.
5. **Tell the sender to use their own operator.** If someone really sent money by mistake, ask them to contact their operator and ask for a reversal. We do not print operator codes in this post, because we could not check them on official pages. Ask your operator for the current process.
6. **Sleep on it if you can.** A real mistake can wait an hour. A scam cannot.

The TCRA campaign page gives similar advice in plain words: never send money through your mobile phone to someone you do not know, and examine the message and the number before completing a transaction.

## What real operators do, and what scammers do

This table shows patterns. It is a guide, not a guarantee.

| A real operator or sender | A scammer |
| --- | --- |
| Lets you check your balance in the official menu or app | Tells you to trust the text and not check |
| Never asks for your PIN | Asks for your PIN "to cancel the mistake" |
| Handles reversals through its own process | Asks you to send the money to a personal number |
| Lets you take your time | Says it is urgent and someone is in trouble |
| Does not call to announce prizes or bonuses | Calls to say you have won something |

On that last point, The Citizen reported in 2017 that Vodacom Tanzania said it does not call customers to tell them about M-Pesa bonus payments, and never asks customers for their M-Pesa password before paying promotional winnings. That is one operator's statement from 2017, but the habit is a good one to keep with any operator.

## Fake prize calls and fake customer-service messages

The same trick has cousins. In the 2017 report, callers told people they had won money in a promotion or an M-Pesa bonus. TCRA's 2022 campaign says that if someone calls to say you won a lottery you never entered, you should not respond.

Hang up. A real prize does not need you to pay first, share a PIN or send "a small fee". You cannot win a promotion you never joined.

TCRA also says not to follow instructions from unknown callers whose identity you cannot confirm. If a caller claims to be from your operator, end the call and ring your operator through the number you already trust. TCRA's campaign page says you can reach your service provider by dialling 100.

## If you already sent money

First, be kind to yourself. Scams like this are designed by people who do this daily. Your feeling of shame is normal, and it is the thing the scammer counts on to keep you quiet. Do not let it stop you from acting.

Act as soon as you can:

1. **Contact your operator.** TCRA says to send your complaint to your service provider first, in writing. Tell them what happened and give the time, the amount and the number you sent to.
2. **Go to a police station.** TCRA's FAQ advises reporting fraud there to get an RB number and an investigator. Take the messages and your transaction record.
3. **Report the number by SMS to 15040.** TCRA's FAQ says to send the word UTAPELI followed by the number. Its 2022 campaign page says to send "Fraud". The pages differ, so follow the automated reply you get and check tcra.go.tz for the current wording.
4. **Escalate if needed.** If your operator does not answer within 30 days, or you are not satisfied with the reply, TCRA says you can complain to it. Its toll-free line is 0800008272.
5. **Keep the evidence.** Do not delete the texts or call logs.

We cannot promise that you will get your money back. We can say that reporting helps: TCRA's campaign page says reported numbers are investigated by law enforcement.

TCRA's FAQ also says you can dial *106# to confirm that your SIM registration is complete. If you think someone is misusing your identity, follow what the screen shows.

## Common questions

### What if someone really did send me money by mistake?

Check your balance in the official channel first. If the money is there, ask the sender to contact their own operator for a reversal. We have not checked what Tanzanian law says about keeping money sent in error, so we give no legal view. Ask your operator or the police if you are unsure.

### The message used my name. Is it real?

Not necessarily. Scammers can learn names from public posts or from other leaks. A name does not prove a message is real.

### Can I call the number that texted me?

If you do, use a different phone, as the council advised, and never give a PIN. A better option is to check your balance first and say nothing until you know.

### Does a message from a number I know count as proof?

No. TCRA-CCC said not to act even when the number looks familiar. Phone numbers can be copied or a friend's phone can be misused. Confirm by calling the person on another line.

## What to do next

1. Choose one rule today: "I check my balance in the official menu before I send anything back."
2. Tell your family, especially older relatives, that nobody ever needs a PIN.
3. Save your operator's customer-care contact from its official page, so you can reach it fast.
4. If you were caught, report it, and do not carry the shame alone.

One careful pause can protect your hard-earned money, and a short pause is something anyone can learn.
