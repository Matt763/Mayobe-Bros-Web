A chatbot feels private. It is just you, a screen and a friendly reply. But what you type is sent to a company's servers, and some of it may be read by people or used to improve the product. Once it is sent, you cannot take it back.

This post goes deeper than the short data tips in our list of [10 AI tools](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/top-10-ai-tools-for-productivity/). It uses the companies' own words, as checked in November 2026.

## The short answer

Think of the chat box as a public noticeboard. If you would not pin it up at the market, do not type it.

- Never type passwords, PINs or one-time codes, including mobile-money PINs.
- Think twice before sharing ID numbers, phone numbers, addresses and other people's details.
- Keep exam papers, work secrets and client information out unless your school or employer allows it.
- Use a placeholder such as "[NAME]" instead of the real detail.
- If you already pasted something, act quickly: change the password, delete the chat and tell the person or employer concerned.

## What happens to the words you type?

Different companies handle this differently, and settings change. We only describe what we could read on the official pages. We could not open OpenAI's help page for ChatGPT, so we say nothing here about ChatGPT's settings. Check them in the app yourself.

### Google Gemini

Google's privacy page for Gemini Apps says a subset of chats is reviewed to improve its services. Reviewers, including trained service providers, check whether answers were low-quality, inaccurate or harmful. Google says chats are disconnected from your account before service providers see them, and that reviewed chats are kept for up to three years.

Google then gives this plain advice: do not enter confidential information that you would not want a reviewer to see.

The page also says:

- Turning off the Keep Activity setting stops your future chats from being reviewed to improve Google services.
- Deleting your activity does not delete chats that were already reviewed.
- Temporary chats are not used to train Google's AI models, though Google says it still keeps them for 72 hours.

### Anthropic's Claude

Anthropic's privacy page says chats and coding sessions may be used to improve its models in three cases: when you choose to allow it, when a conversation is flagged for safety review, or when you join a programme and opt in. It says incognito chats are not used to improve Claude, even if the model-improvement setting is on.

We saw different numbers for how long Anthropic keeps chats on other websites, so we do not give any figure. Check the company's own page for the current rules.

### The lesson

The details differ, but the pattern is the same. A chat can be kept, reviewed or used. A setting may help, but a setting is not a promise that nothing will leak. The safest information is the information you never typed.

## What should I keep out of the chat box?

| Never share | Think twice | Usually fine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passwords, PINs, one-time codes (OTP) | Your full name, phone number, home address | General questions about a topic |
| Mobile-money or bank PINs and card numbers | NIDA, TIN or student numbers | A paragraph you wrote, with names removed |
| Exam papers before the exam, and answers you are not allowed to share | Other people's names, photos or messages | Practice questions you made yourself |
| Company secrets, client lists, contracts | Medical details about you or family | Public information, such as a news article |
| Anyone's private data that you do not have the right to share | Work documents (ask your employer first) | Made-up examples |

If your school, office or client has rules, those rules win. The University of Dar es Salaam's AI guidelines, for example, warn that AI tools may pass on or expose sensitive or confidential information, and say people must follow the university's rules on confidentiality. That is one university's rule, and it applies to its own staff and students. But the idea is wise for everyone.

### Other people's information

Your friend's story, a customer's complaint or a patient's details are not yours to share. Tanzania has a Personal Data Protection Act, and the Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC) is the regulator. Its website says organisations that collect or process personal data must register, and that people can lodge a complaint with the Commission. We are not giving legal advice. If you handle other people's data for work, ask your employer or a qualified adviser what the law means for you.

## What are safer ways to ask the same question?

Swap the real detail for a placeholder, and keep the question.

**Before:** "My customer Mr Juma Hassan, phone 07xx xxx xxx, from Kinondoni, has not paid TSh 450,000 for the sofa. Write him a message."

**After:** "A customer has not paid TSh 450,000 for a sofa that was delivered two weeks ago. Write a polite but firm reminder. Use [CUSTOMER NAME] and [DATE] as placeholders."

Both are made-up examples. The second gets the same help and leaks no one's name or number. You then add the real details yourself, outside the chatbot.

More habits:

- Describe the situation in general words ("a student at a university", "a small shop").
- Round or change numbers that identify you, when exact figures do not matter.
- Cut a document down to the part you need help with.
- Remove names, numbers and addresses before you paste text.
- Use a temporary or incognito chat if your app offers one, knowing from the above that it is not a perfect shield.
- Look at the privacy settings of the app you use, and read them again from time to time.

## What if I already pasted something by mistake?

Do not panic. Take these steps in order:

1. **Stop.** Do not add more details to the same chat.
2. **Change any password or PIN you shared.** Do this first. Use the official app or website of the service. For mobile money, contact your provider's official line.
3. **Delete the chat** in the app, and look for the activity or history settings. As Google's page says, deleting does not remove chats that were already reviewed, so do not rely on deletion alone.
4. **Tell the right person.** If it was your employer's, client's or school's information, tell them quickly. A short honest report is much better than hiding it.
5. **Watch for odd activity.** Look at your bank, mobile-money and email alerts for the next few weeks.
6. **Learn from it.** Add a placeholder habit for next time.

If the data involved other people and you think it was serious, you can ask the PDPC how to proceed. Its website has a complaint form and a helpdesk.

## Common questions

### Is it safe if I turn off chat history?

Safer, not safe. Google says turning off Keep Activity stops future chats from being reviewed to improve its services, but chats already reviewed stay. Settings can also change. Keep sensitive details out anyway.

### Can the chatbot company see my chats?

Companies describe cases where chats are reviewed, such as improving the service or safety checks. Read the privacy page of the tool you use.

### Can I upload my CV?

A CV has your name, phone and address. Remove those, or use a placeholder, before asking for help. Then put them back yourself.

### Can I paste my assignment?

Only your own work, and only if your lecturer allows AI. Read [using AI to study without cheating](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/using-ai-to-study-without-cheating/) for more.

## What to do next

1. Open the privacy settings of the AI app you use most. Read what it says about your chats.
2. Write yourself a note: "No passwords, PINs, ID numbers or other people's details."
3. Practise using placeholders for the next three questions you ask.
4. Learn to ask clearly in [how to write a good AI prompt](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/how-to-write-a-good-ai-prompt/), and check answers with [how to check an AI answer for mistakes](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/how-to-check-ai-answers-for-mistakes/).

AI can be a good helper, and you can keep it as one. A few seconds of care before you press send protects you and the people who trust you with their information.
