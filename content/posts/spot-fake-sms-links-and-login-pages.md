A message pings on your phone. Your account is "blocked", a prize is waiting, or a parcel cannot be delivered. For a second your stomach drops, and that is exactly what the sender wants. This guide shows you how to pause, check, and stay in control.

## The short answer

Do not use the link in a message you did not expect. Go to the website or app yourself, by typing the address or opening the official app, and check there. That one habit beats most scam messages, whatever the message says.

- Scam messages push you to act fast, so slow down.
- A real company does not need your password or PIN by SMS.
- Good spelling no longer proves a message is real.
- Report scam numbers to TCRA by SMS to 15040 and follow the automated reply.
- If you already tapped or typed a password, act now. The checklist below shows how.

## Why do scam messages work?

They aim at feelings, not at your phone. Fear ("your account will be closed"), greed ("you won TSh 5,000,000") and kindness ("I am in trouble, please help") make people act before they think. TCRA, the communications regulator, says people should not respond to prizes they never entered and should check the message and the number before any transaction.

Many people used to look for spelling mistakes. The US cyber agency CISA now warns that artificial intelligence can write flawless messages. So good grammar proves nothing. Look at what the message asks you to do instead.

## Six signs of a scam message

| Sign | What it looks like (invented examples) |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Urgency or threats | "Your account will be closed in 1 hour. Act now." |
| 2. A request for personal or financial details | "Reply with your PIN or password to confirm." |
| 3. A strange or shortened link | A link that is shortened, or that has an odd spelling of a known name |
| 4. A sender you do not know, or who copies someone you know | A new number saying "Hi, it's me, I changed my number" |
| 5. A prize you never entered | "You have won! Send TSh 10,000 to collect." |
| 6. A request to move away from the official place | "Do not call the bank. Chat with us on this number." |

CISA lists urgent or threatening language, requests for personal or financial information, and untrusted shortened links or wrong web addresses among the common signs. The extra ones in the table come from our own common sense. One sign alone does not always mean a scam, but two or three together should stop you.

Here is an invented example, written for this article. It is not a real message and uses no real company or number:

> "Dear customer, your mobile wallet is blocked. Confirm your PIN at the link below within 1 hour or lose your money: exampl3-wallet-check.test"

It uses a threat (sign 1), asks for a PIN (sign 2), and gives a strange link (sign 3). Three signs in two lines.

Look closely at web addresses. An address can look familiar and still be wrong, for example an extra letter or a number in place of a letter. CISA's own example is a spelling like "amazan" in place of a known name. A fake login page can also look exactly like the real one, so the page itself is not proof.

## The safe habit: do not use the link

Whenever a message asks you to log in, pay or "verify", do this instead:

1. Do not tap the link, open the attachment, or call the number in the message.
2. Open the official app, or type the website address yourself.
3. If you have to contact the company, use a phone number you already have from a bill, a card or the official site. CISA gives the same advice: contact the company or person in a way you find yourself.
4. For your mobile network, TCRA says genuine help from service providers comes through short code 100. Be careful with anyone who contacts you from a personal number saying they work for the network.
5. If it is a friend or relative asking for money or a code, call them on the number you already have, to check it is really them.

If there is a real problem with your account, it will also show when you log in the safe way. If nothing shows, the message was probably fake.

Never share one-time codes sent to your phone, with anyone. ## If you already tapped or typed a password

Do not panic and do not blame yourself. Fast action can limit the damage.

1. **Stop.** Do not enter anything more, and do not reply to the message.
2. **Change the password** of that account right away, using the official app or site that you opened yourself. If you used the same password anywhere else, change it there too.
3. **Turn on two-step verification** on that account, if it offers it.
4. **If you gave a mobile money PIN or sent money,** change your PIN, then contact your provider on short code 100 and go to a police station. TCRA says police give you an RB number when your report is registered.
5. **If you downloaded an app from the link,** do not open it. Uninstall it, and ask someone you trust for help if you are not sure how.
6. **Tell your contacts** if your WhatsApp or social media account may have been taken over, so they ignore odd messages from you.
7. **Report the scam number** by SMS to 15040. TCRA's 2022 page says to send the word "Fraud", and its FAQ says "UTAPELI" followed by the scam number. Follow the automated reply you receive.

TCRA's 2022 page also says you can dial *106 to see which numbers are registered with your national ID, and ask your provider to remove any you do not recognise.

## Common questions

### Can a message be real even if it has my name in it?

Yes, and a scam can also have your name. Names are easy to find. Judge the message by what it asks you to do.

### What about messages from a number that looks like my bank or network?

Do not trust the sender name alone. Open the official app or call a number you already have.

### Is it safe to open the link just to look?

It is better not to. Tapping can lead to a fake login page or a download. Delete the message instead.

### Should I forward the scam message to friends as a warning?

Do not forward the link. Tell them about it in your own words, and report the number to 15040.

## What to do next

1. Choose one thing today: turn on two-step verification on your most important account, usually your email.
2. Save short code 100 and 15040 in your phone notes so you can find them in a hurry.
3. Next time a message hurries you, wait five minutes, then check the safe way.

You do not have to know every trick. If you stay calm, slow things down and go to the real site yourself, you are already ahead of most scams.
