You work hard, you are tired, and an assignment is due. An AI assistant can write a whole page in seconds, and the temptation is real. Many students feel it. This post is here to help you use AI in a way you can be proud of, one that makes you stronger and does not put your place at risk.

## The short answer

Use AI like a tutor, not like a ghost writer. Before you use it for any graded work, find out what your lecturer and your university allow.

- Ask your lecturer first, in writing if you can. Rules differ between courses and institutions.
- Use AI to understand, practise and improve your own work, not to replace it.
- Never use it in a closed-book test or exam. Where the rules ban it, a ban means a ban.
- If you used AI in work you hand in, say so honestly.
- Do not trust AI facts without checking them.

## What may your university say about AI?

Every institution sets its own rules, and they change. We did not find one rule from the Tanzania Commission for Universities (TCU) that covers all universities, so do not assume there is one. Tanzania's Ministry of Education, Science and Technology launched National Guidelines for Artificial Intelligence in Education, according to The Citizen newspaper in May 2025. The same report says each institution should write its own AI framework. We read the news report, not the guidelines themselves.

So the rule that counts for you is the one from your school, college or university, and then from your lecturer for each course.

### One example: the University of Dar es Salaam

As one example, the University of Dar es Salaam (UDSM) has a document called Guidelines on Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence. It is published on the UDSM website and dated February 2026. The copy we opened has blank approval details on its information page, so please confirm the final version with the university. It applies to UDSM only. It is not a rule for every Tanzanian university.

As we read that document, it says:

- Students should use AI ethically in open-book assignments, such as individual or group assignments, presentations, lab work, project work and quizzes.
- The use of AI in closed-book assignments and examinations is prohibited.
- Using AI to generate reports or presentation materials from practical-year projects is prohibited, and the document lists lab reports and reports from practical training or teaching practice.
- Any use of AI in a dissertation or thesis should be carefully documented and disclosed.
- For writing courses, it supports guided use, such as an outline that students then practise revising, or ideas that students then judge for themselves.
- It discourages staff from relying on automatic AI detection programs because they are unreliable and cannot give clear proof.

If you study somewhere else, your rules may be stricter, looser or just different. Read them. If you cannot find them, ask.

## How can I use AI to learn?

The test is simple. After you use AI, do you understand the topic better, and could you explain it without the AI? If yes, you probably used it well. Here are uses that build understanding:

- **Explain an idea in simpler words.** "Explain inflation to a Form Four student, with an example about the price of maize." Then explain it back in your own words.
- **Quiz yourself.** "Ask me five questions on cell division, one at a time, and tell me if I am right after each." This is active practice, which is better than just re-reading.
- **Check your own outline.** Write your own plan first. Then ask, "What is missing from this outline?" Decide for yourself what to keep.
- **Find the gap in your thinking.** "Here is my answer. What question might a lecturer ask about it?"
- **Get ideas, then judge them.** Brainstorming is fine when you do the choosing and the work.
- **Practise a language.** "Correct my English and tell me what the mistakes were, so I can learn."

For more on asking well, see [how to write a good AI prompt](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/how-to-write-a-good-ai-prompt/).

## Which uses cross the line?

These uses replace your learning, and many will break the rules:

- Pasting a question and handing in the answer as yours.
- Having AI write a whole essay, report or dissertation chapter.
- Using AI during a closed-book test or exam, or on a phone under the desk.
- Using AI to write lab or practical-year reports where your institution bans it.
- Copying AI-written sources or quotes without checking that they exist.
- Using AI to disguise copied work. We will not help with this. UDSM's own guidelines say detection tools are unreliable, but that does not make cheating safe. Lecturers also know your voice, and a viva or a follow-up question can show quickly who did the thinking.

There is also the cost to you. If a machine does the thinking now, you arrive at the exam, the interview and the job without the skill. The Citizen report quotes education experts who worry that students may rely on AI too much and lose independent thinking. That worry is about you.

AI can also be wrong. It may invent a book, a law or a reference that sounds real. Read [how to check an AI answer for mistakes](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/how-to-check-ai-answers-for-mistakes/). For plagiarism rules in general, read [how to avoid plagiarism in university assignments](/post/educational/study-tips/how-to-avoid-plagiarism-university-assignments/).

## How do I say honestly that I used AI?

When your lecturer allows AI, tell them how you used it. A short note is enough. Here are sample sentences. Change them to fit your own work and to match your institution's format:

> "I used an AI assistant to explain the topic and to check my outline. I wrote the text myself and checked all facts against my course notes and textbooks."

> "I used an AI tool to suggest corrections to my English. I accepted or rejected each suggestion."

If your lecturer wants a particular form, use it. UDSM's document includes a declaration form for scientific writing as an appendix, which shows that some places want a formal statement.

## A short checklist to ask your lecturer

Ask these before the first assignment, and keep the reply:

1. May I use AI for this course? For which tasks?
2. Is it allowed for brainstorming, outlines, language checks or explanations?
3. Is it banned in tests, exams, practicals or reports?
4. Which tools may I use?
5. How must I record or declare my use?
6. What will happen if I use it where it is not allowed?

## What about privacy?

Do not paste personal details, exam papers or other people's work into an AI tool. UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education also stresses data privacy and says users' data is at risk where national rules are weak. We cover this in [what you should never paste into an AI chatbot](/post/technology-solutions/ai-tools/what-not-to-paste-into-ai-chatbots/).

## Common questions

### Is it cheating to use AI to explain something I do not understand?

Usually no, if your institution allows AI and you then do the work yourself. Explanation is how a tutor helps. Confirm with your lecturer, and check the explanation against your notes.

### Can I use AI to correct my English?

Many lecturers allow it, but some do not. Ask first. Learn from the changes, and keep the ideas your own.

### Will AI detectors catch me?

Do not plan around detectors. UDSM's guidelines call them unreliable, so they can also wrongly accuse honest students. Keep drafts and notes so you can show how you worked. The better plan is to do honest work.

### What if my university has no AI rules yet?

Ask your lecturer in writing and follow the answer. If the answer is unclear, do not use AI for graded work until you know.

## What to do next

1. Find your institution's AI or academic integrity rules on its website, and ask your lecturer where they are not clear.
2. Choose one study use from the list above and try it this week.
3. Keep your own notes and drafts.
4. Check any fact the AI gave you.

Learning is slow and sometimes lonely, but every page you understand yourself is yours to keep. AI can walk beside you. You still have to do the walking.
