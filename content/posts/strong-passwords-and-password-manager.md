Most of us have too many passwords and too little time. So we pick something short, add a number, and use it again and again. That is human, and it is fixable. In one afternoon you can protect the accounts that matter most, and you do not need to be a computer expert to do it.

## The short answer

A strong password is long, hard to guess, and used on only one account. A passphrase made of several unrelated words is a good way to get there, because it is easier to remember than a jumble of symbols. A password manager can remember the rest for you, so you only have to remember one strong passphrase.

- Make passwords long. CISA, the US cyber-security agency, says at least 16 characters.
- Use a random string, or a passphrase of 4 to 7 unrelated words.
- Use a different password for every account.
- Use a password manager. CISA says some are free, such as the ones built into web browsers.
- Turn on a second step (multifactor authentication), especially for email, social media and money accounts.

## What makes a password strong now

For years we were told to use symbols, capital letters and numbers, and to change the password every few months. The advice has moved on.

CISA says a strong password follows three tips: make it long (at least 16 characters), make it random, and make it unique. For random, it offers two ways. One is a random string of mixed letters, numbers and symbols. The other is a memorable phrase of 4 to 7 unrelated words, which it calls a passphrase. CISA notes that you can put spaces between the words if you like.

What about the standard that websites are asked to follow? The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) publishes rules in a document called SP 800-63B-4. It is written for service providers, not for ordinary readers, so here is only what it asks websites to do:

- Accept passwords of at least 15 characters when the password is used alone (as the only login step).
- Let people make passwords as long as they want and use any characters they like, including spaces, to help memory.
- Not force strange rules such as "one capital, one symbol".
- Not force people to change passwords on a timetable, unless there is evidence that the password has been stolen.
- Allow password managers and autofill, and allow pasting into the password box.

Note that CISA says 16 and NIST says 15. They are different documents for different readers, and both point to the same idea: length matters. We suggest that you aim for 16 or more.

### How to build a passphrase you can remember

Pick four or five words that have no link to each other, and do not use a famous sentence, a song line or a quote. Do not use your name, birth date, pet's name or phone number, because CISA warns that this kind of personal detail is not safe.

Here is a made-up example, only to show the shape: *river chair seven tomato blue*. Do not use that exact one, since anyone who reads this post knows it. Make your own.

A few tips:

- Choose words from different parts of your life, without a story that connects them.
- Say them out loud several times. Imagine a silly picture if it helps.
- Type it from memory for a few days, and it will stick.
- Never write the passphrase in a note on your phone, in a chat or in an email to yourself.

## Why using one password everywhere is the real danger

A short password is a problem. The same password on every account is a bigger one.

Think of it this way. If you use the same key for your house, your shop and your bicycle lock, then anyone who copies the key can open all three. A password works the same way. If a website you used years ago is hacked, the thieves may try the same email and password on Facebook, Gmail and your mobile money app.

So one weak website can open your important accounts. That is why CISA says to use a different strong password for every account.

This table compares habits:

| Weak habit | Better habit |
| --- | --- |
| Short password with a birthday | Long passphrase of unrelated words |
| One password on every account | A different password for each account |
| Passwords in a paper list in your pocket | A password manager with one strong passphrase |
| Sharing a code you got by SMS | Never share a code with anyone |
| No second step | Two-step verification on important accounts |

Please do not type your real passwords into online "password strength checkers", because you cannot be sure who is reading them. If you want to test one, type a similar made-up password instead.

## How a password manager helps

A password manager is a program that makes, stores and fills in your passwords. CISA says that when you use one, you only need to remember one strong password, the one for the manager itself. It also says managers can warn you about weak or reused passwords.

NIST's document says that password managers have been shown to help people choose stronger passwords, especially managers with a password generator.

CISA says some password managers are free, such as the built-in ones in web browsers, and some cost money. It suggests reading reviews from a trusted source before choosing one. We name no paid product and do not rank any as the best.

As one example, as checked in November 2026, Google's help page says that Google Password Manager:

- creates strong, unique passwords and passkeys,
- saves them to your Google Account and fills them in on sites and apps,
- can alert you if your saved passwords appear publicly online,
- stores your passwords with encryption.

Google also recommends adding recovery information and turning on 2-Step Verification to protect your saved sign-in details.

There is one risk to understand. If you lose access to the account that holds your manager, you lose access to your passwords. Keep your recovery phone number and email up to date. Protect that one account with a long passphrase and a second step.

## An afternoon plan for your five most important accounts

You do not need to change fifty passwords today. Start with five. Set aside one hour or two, and sit somewhere quiet.

The five accounts that matter most for many people are:

1. Your main email account.
2. Mobile money or banking.
3. WhatsApp.
4. Facebook.
5. Your Google or Apple account.

Follow these steps:

1. **Pick your manager.** You can choose the one built into your phone's account or browser. Set it up and protect that account with a new passphrase that you can remember.
2. **Add recovery details.** Add a recovery phone number and email, and check that you can still use both.
3. **Do email first.** Email can reset the others, so give it a new, long, unique password and turn on a second step.
4. **Do the money account next.** Change the password in the way your provider describes. Never share your PIN or codes with anyone.
5. **Do Facebook and your Google or Apple account.** Let the manager suggest a strong password and save it.
6. **Do WhatsApp last.** WhatsApp does not use a classic password, so it needs its own steps. Our post on [two-step verification for WhatsApp](/post/technology-solutions/cyber-security/protect-whatsapp-account-two-step-verification/) shows how.
7. **Keep backup codes safe.** Google says you can print or download a set of backup codes for when you lose your phone. Keep them somewhere safe and never give them to anyone.
8. **Look at the second step.** Google says text and call codes can be vulnerable to phone number-based hacks. Where you can, choose a passkey or a prompt in an app. See also our post on [SIM swap fraud](/post/technology-solutions/cyber-security/sim-swap-fraud-what-to-do/).

Menu names and set-up steps can change, so we have not given exact taps. Look for words like "Security", "Password" or "2-Step Verification" in each account's settings.

Other days, do a few more accounts. Each one you fix closes a door.

## Common questions

### Is it safe to save passwords in my phone or browser?

CISA lists built-in browser managers as one free option. They are much safer than reusing one password. Protect your phone with a screen lock, and protect the account that stores them.

### Do I need to change my password every three months?

NIST's document says websites should not force regular changes without a reason. Change a password when you think it was stolen, or when a site tells you it was leaked.

### Should I write passwords on paper?

CISA says that rather than writing passwords down, you should use a password manager. If you do keep a paper list as a temporary step, keep it at home in a safe place, never in your wallet, and never label it "passwords".

### What is a passkey?

Google describes a passkey as a simpler and safer alternative to a password. It says that passkeys and hardware security keys protect your Google Account from phishing attacks, which are fake pages that try to steal your login.

## What to do next

1. Today, pick one account, your email, and give it a new passphrase of four or five unrelated words.
2. This week, set up a password manager and move your five key accounts into it.
3. Turn on two-step verification for email and Facebook.
4. Next month, add five more accounts.

Every strong password is a small act of care for yourself and the people who depend on you. Start with one.
