Your phone is already a good enough camera to start selling. What makes a photo work is not the price of the phone. It is light, a calm background and an honest view of the item. Many people sell from a small room with one window, and that is enough.

This guide shows a simple set-up you can try today with things you already own.

## The short answer

Put the item near a window, on a plain cloth or sheet, wipe your lens, and take one clear photo of one item from a few angles.

- Use daylight from a window, not the phone flash.
- Use a plain background, such as a clean white or light cloth.
- Wipe the camera lens with a soft, clean cloth before you start.
- Show one item per photo, filling most of the picture.
- Show real colour, real size and any flaw, so buyers are not surprised.
- Take your own photos. Never use someone else's.

## What do Jiji and Google ask for?

Platforms that sell things online have rules about photos. Reading them tells you what buyers and reviewers expect.

As checked in November 2026, Jiji Tanzania tells sellers to use unique photos that they took themselves, with no contact details and no watermarks. Its tips page says better photos make an ad more attractive and tend to bring more calls. It also lists reasons why photos are declined, including screenshots, several items in one photo, images you did not take yourself, irrelevant images, low quality, photos used in an earlier ad, visible contact details or prices, logos and inscriptions.

Google publishes an image guide for sellers who list products in its shopping tools. Those tools may not be open to every seller in Tanzania, so we use the guide only as a benchmark for quality. It says, as checked in November 2026:

- Images should be at least 500 x 500 pixels, and 1500 x 1500 or more is recommended.
- The product should fill between 75% and 90% of the picture.
- No watermarks, added logos, prices or promotional text on the image.
- Use extra images for other angles and details.
- Show one variant (such as one colour) per image.

Most modern phones take photos much larger than 1500 pixels, so the size rule is usually easy to meet. The framing rule is the one you can control.

## Set up in five minutes

You need a window, a table and a cloth. Follow these steps.

1. **Find the light.** Put a table next to a window in daytime. Soft light from the side makes the item look natural. Direct sun can make harsh shadows and bright spots, so move back a little or hang a thin white cloth over the window.
2. **Switch off the flash.** Flash often makes things look flat and shiny, and it can change colours.
3. **Lay a plain background.** Use a clean white or light sheet, a plain wall, or a large sheet of paper. Remove clutter, wires, cups and other items. A plain background means the buyer looks at your product.
4. **Clean the lens.** Fingerprints make photos look foggy, and this is the easiest fix there is. Wipe the lens with a clean, dry, soft cloth.
5. **Hold the phone steady.** Rest your elbows on the table, or lean the phone on a stack of books. Tap the item on the screen so the camera focuses on it.

If you do not have a stand, a few books and a small piece of tape work fine. Do not buy equipment before you have tried this.

## Which angles answer buyers' questions?

Buyers cannot touch your item, so your photos have to do the touching for them. Think about what they would ask in a message and show it.

- **Front view** of the whole item, with the product filling most of the frame.
- **Back and sides**, so there are no surprises.
- **Close-up of details**, such as a zip, label, stitching, screen or serial area.
- **Size reference.** A ruler or a common object beside it helps. Do not guess in words only.
- **Flaws.** A scratch, a mark or a worn corner should appear in a close-up photo.
- **What is included.** If the box, charger or tags come with it, show them.

Keep to one item per photo and one item per ad. Jiji's rules say "1 ad = 1 item", and separate ads need their own photos.

## Be honest about colour, size and flaws

Say a shop sells a dress and the photo looks brighter than the real cloth. The buyer receives it, feels cheated and wants a refund. The sale is lost, and so may be the buyer's trust.

An honest photo prevents that. It costs you a little more thought, and it saves you arguments.

- Check the colour on your screen against the real item in daylight. If your phone changes colours, say so in the description.
- Write the size in centimetres or the unit people use, and make sure it matches the photo.
- Show the flaw. A buyer who sees a small scratch in the photo cannot say you hid it.
- Photograph the exact item you will hand over. Jiji advises buyers to check that the item they inspected is the one they receive, so your photo should match it.

Honest pictures also support trust over time. Our post on how to [earn customer trust as a new online seller](/post/business-ideas/e-commerce/earn-customer-trust-new-online-seller-tanzania/) goes into more detail.

## Edit lightly and keep your originals

You may adjust brightness a little, or crop the edges so the item fills the frame. Do not change the colour so much that it looks different from the real thing.

- Save the original photos in a folder. If a buyer disputes the item, you can show what you sent.
- Do not add text, prices, phone numbers or your logo over the picture. Jiji declines photos with contact details, prices, logos or inscriptions, and Google's guide also does not allow overlays.
- Save space by keeping a smaller copy for sharing. Keep the large original.

## Never use other people's photos

Do not copy photos from other sellers or from the internet. It is unfair, it breaks Jiji's rules, and the buyer may ask for a photo of the item in your hands. If the item is a branded one, do not edit in a company logo that is not on the real item.

Your own photos also carry your brand. When people see the same clean style again and again, they begin to recognise your shop. Our post on [branding basics](/post/business-ideas/marketing/branding-basics-name-logo-colours/) explains how to keep a simple, steady look.

## Common questions

### Do I need a new phone to take good photos?

No. A clean lens, good light and a plain background matter more than the phone. Start with what you have.

### Should I use the flash?

Usually not. Window light looks more natural. If the room is dark, wait until daytime or move the table closer to a window.

### Can I use a photo from the supplier or the internet?

Not as your own. Jiji asks for unique photos you took yourself. If you sell a branded item, show the real item you hold.

### How many photos should I take?

Enough to answer the buyer's questions, and no more than needed. Front, back, close-up of details and any flaw are a good start. Check the limit for each platform.

## What to do next

1. Choose one item and take it near a window with a plain cloth behind it.
2. Take a front, back, close-up and flaw photo, then compare them with the real item.
3. Read Jiji's photo tips and compare your pictures with the decline reasons.
4. Save your originals in a folder named for the item.

Good photos are a small habit, and every item you photograph carefully is a step toward a shop people trust.
