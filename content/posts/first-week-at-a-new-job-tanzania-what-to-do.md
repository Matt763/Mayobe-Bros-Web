You got the job, or the internship. After all the waiting and applying, someone said yes. That is a real achievement, and you may feel proud and nervous in the same breath. Both feelings are normal on the first morning.

This post gives you a plain plan for the first week. Every workplace is different, so use it as a starting point and follow your employer's own rules first. Much of the advice comes from careers services in the UK and US, so we call it general advice.

## The short answer

In week one, your job is to learn, not to impress. Arrive on time, listen, write things down, and ask clear questions. Agree two or three goals with your supervisor, and make sure you have your main terms in writing.

- Arrive early and be ready to listen.
- Carry a notebook and write down names, tasks and instructions.
- Ask your supervisor what a good first month looks like.
- Agree two or three goals and write them down.
- Ask for your job description, hours and pay terms in writing.
- Ask when you do not understand. It is better than guessing.

## Before day one

A little preparation takes away a lot of fear. A day or two before you start, do these things.

1. **Know the way.** Work out your route, the fare and how long it takes. If you take a daladala or bodaboda, plan for traffic and leave earlier than you think you need to. If you can, do a trial trip before day one.
2. **Confirm the details.** Check the start time, the address, who to ask for and what to bring. Ask politely by phone or message if you are not sure. Bring your ID and any documents the employer asked for.
3. **Choose your clothes.** Pick something clean and suitable for the place. If you do not know the dress rules, ask or choose neat and modest.
4. **Pack simply.** A notebook, two pens, your phone with enough charge, water and a small snack.
5. **Rest.** Go to bed at a normal hour. A calm start is worth more than a late night of worry.

## Day one: arrive, listen, write things down

On the first day, you are mostly a guest who is learning the house.

- **Arrive early,** a little before the start time. Say good morning to people, smile, and give your name clearly.
- **Listen first.** Notice how people speak to each other, how meetings run and what time people take breaks.
- **Write things down.** Names, job titles, passwords you should keep private, where things are, and every task you are given. A notebook shows you take the work seriously, and it means nobody has to explain the same thing three times.
- **Ask who is who.** Ask your supervisor which people you will work with most.
- **Switch your phone to silent** and keep it away during meetings unless you need it for work.

The University of Washington careers office (US) names professionalism as being dependable, prepared and careful with details, and teamwork as listening and owning your responsibilities. Day one is where those habits begin. Nobody expects you to know everything. They do notice whether you are on time and paying attention.

## Days two to five: a simple rhythm

Here is one way to shape the week. Adjust it to fit your workplace.

| Day | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Day 1 | Arrive, meet people, write down instructions |
| Day 2 | Learn the main tasks, repeat back what you understood |
| Day 3 | Do a small task fully and ask for feedback |
| Day 4 | Meet your supervisor to agree goals |
| Day 5 | Review the week, write what you learned, plan week two |

A useful habit is to repeat back an instruction in your own words: "So you would like the list sorted by date, and I should send it to you by 3 pm. Is that right?" It prevents mistakes and shows you care about getting it right.

If you make a mistake, say so early, say what you will do to fix it, and then fix it. Hiding a mistake usually makes it bigger.

## The questions to ask your supervisor

Good questions show that you are keen and thoughtful. Prospects, a UK graduate careers site, lists questions that candidates can ask at an interview, such as "What does a typical day look like?", "How could I impress you in the first three months?" and "Can you tell me more about the team I would be working in?" They work just as well in your first week, because now you can ask them as a new member of the team.

Try these, in your own words:

- What does a typical day or week look like in this role?
- What would you like me to learn in my first month?
- How can I do well in my first three months?
- Who should I ask when you are not available?
- How do you prefer to communicate: in person, by phone call, by WhatsApp or by email?
- What should I do if I do not understand a task?
- How will I get feedback, and how often?
- What are the working hours, and how do I tell you if I will be late or sick?

You do not need to ask them all on one day. Choose three or four for your first meeting and keep the rest in your notebook.

## Set two or three goals in week one

Goals give you something to aim at and give your supervisor a clear way to help you. The University of Washington's guide on learning goals says to meet your supervisor in the first week and get your goals agreed and written down. It then suggests a check-in every week or every two weeks, and a look at progress around the midpoint. It also says that two or three solid goals are enough for a quarter-long internship, and that you should expect to revise your goals after feedback.

That advice is written for internships in the US, but the idea fits any new job.

Good goals are specific. Compare these:

- Weak: "Do well at work."
- Better: "By the end of month one, I can prepare the weekly sales summary without help."
- Better: "By the end of month two, I can answer the most common customer questions without asking."

Write the goals in your notebook, show them to your supervisor and ask, "Is this what you would like me to focus on?" Then book a short regular check-in, even ten minutes a week.

## Get your terms in writing

This part is easy to forget in the excitement, and it protects you.

Tanzania's Employment and Labour Relations Act, 2004 (section 15) says that when an employee starts work, the employer must give them certain particulars in writing. These include:

- your name, age, permanent address and sex,
- the place of recruitment,
- your job description,
- the date you start,
- the form and length of the contract,
- your place of work,
- your hours of work,
- your pay, how it is worked out, and any benefits or payments in kind.

The Act also says that if you do not understand these written particulars, the employer must make sure they are explained to you in a way you understand. If any of the listed matters change, the employer must update the written particulars in consultation with you and tell you in writing.

We read the text of the Act as published on the Tanzanian government labour site (kazi.go.tz), but laws can be amended, and different rules can apply to different kinds of work. This is not legal advice. For your own case, ask your employer's human resources office, your trade union if you have one, or the labour office.

Practical steps:

1. In week one, ask politely: "Please could I have my job description and the written terms of my job?"
2. Read them. If a word is unclear, ask for it to be explained.
3. Keep a copy, and keep your own notes of what was agreed on pay and hours.
4. Never sign something you have not read or do not understand.

For an unpaid internship or attachment, ask for the same clarity in writing: the dates, the hours, what you will do and who will supervise you. A reliable organisation will not mind the question.

## Common first-week mistakes

These are opinions based on common sense, not rules. Many are easy to avoid.

- **Arriving late.** It leaves a first impression that is hard to change.
- **Pretending to understand.** Ask, then write the answer down.
- **Staying silent for days.** Say hello, join conversations at lunch, and offer help where you can.
- **Using your phone too much.** Keep personal calls and scrolling for breaks.
- **Complaining about the old job, your boss or colleagues.** You do not yet know the full story, and words travel.
- **Talking about pay with everyone.** Ask your supervisor or the office responsible, not the whole team.
- **Trying to change everything.** Learn how things are done first. Suggest improvements after you have earned some trust.
- **Forgetting to say thank you.** A simple thank you to the person who trained you goes a long way.

## Common questions

### What if I make a big mistake on day one?

Tell your supervisor quickly and calmly. Say what happened and how you plan to fix it. Most supervisors expect new staff to make errors and care more about honesty and learning.

### Is it too early to ask about pay and hours?

No, if you ask politely and in the right place. Ask your supervisor or the person responsible for staff matters, and ask for the written terms. This is a normal request in a new job.

### What if I do not get written terms?

Ask again, politely, and keep a note of the date. Section 15 of the Employment and Labour Relations Act describes the employer's duty to supply written particulars. If the issue is not resolved, talk to a trade union representative or the labour office. Do not stay silent about it.

### How do I deal with nerves?

Prepare the night before, breathe slowly on the way in, and remember that everyone in the room was new once. Focus on one task at a time. The nerves usually ease within days.

## What to do next

1. Do a trial trip to your workplace, and pack your notebook and pens.
2. Write down your five most useful questions for your supervisor.
3. In week one, ask for your job description and written terms, and agree two or three goals.
4. On Friday, write what you learned, what confused you and what you will ask on Monday.

Starting a new job is a big step, and you are allowed to be proud of it. Take it one day at a time. The week will end, and next Monday will feel a little more like your own.
