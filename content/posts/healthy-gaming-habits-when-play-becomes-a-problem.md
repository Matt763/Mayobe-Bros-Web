If you are a parent or guardian, you may feel a quiet worry when your child plays for hours. You may also feel guilty for feeling it. If you are a young person, you may feel that everyone is judging something you simply enjoy. Both feelings are normal, and neither one makes anyone a bad person.

This post sets out what the World Health Organization (WHO) actually says, what healthy play can look like, and how a family can agree on fair rules. It is general information, not medical advice. For any real concern, please talk to a qualified health professional.

## The short answer

There is no magic number of hours that is "too much". WHO says that gaming disorder affects only a small share of people who play, so most players are not affected. What matters more is whether play is crowding out the rest of life.

- WHO describes gaming disorder by three features: impaired control, gaming taking priority over other activities, and continuing despite harm.
- It would normally be clear for at least 12 months.
- ESRB and a paediatric group both advise family rules made together, not hours dictated from above.
- Sleep, school, food, friends and movement are the things to protect.
- A health professional, not a website, should decide whether something is a problem.

## What the World Health Organization says

WHO's page on gaming disorder explains how it appears in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). We read it in November 2026. It describes gaming disorder as a pattern of gaming behaviour. It lists three features:

1. Impaired control over gaming.
2. Increasing priority given to gaming over other activities, over other activities.
3. Continuing or increasing gaming even when negative consequences appear.

WHO also says the pattern must be severe enough to significantly impair a person's functioning. It adds that this would normally have been clear for at least 12 months.

Then comes the most important sentence for worried families. WHO says gaming disorder affects only a small proportion of people who play digital or video games. Most people who play are not affected.

WHO also gives advice to players themselves. It suggests that people who game should watch how much time they spend on it, especially when it takes over from other daily activities. They should also notice any change in their physical or mental health, or in their social life, that could be linked to gaming.

This page is a description of what WHO says. It is not a test you can use at home. Only a trained professional can say whether someone has a disorder.

## What healthy play looks like day to day

Games can be fun, social and creative. Many young people play to be with friends. Some learn teamwork, patience and quick thinking. Healthy play usually sits comfortably beside everything else in a person's day.

Here are signs that play is fitting in well:

- Homework and school duties get done.
- Meals, sleep and family time still happen.
- The player can stop when asked, perhaps after a short grumble.
- There is still time for friends outside games, and for moving the body.
- The player seems generally well in mood and health.

The ESRB, which rates games in North America, says on its family guide that it gives no fixed number of hours. It suggests asking whether gaming gets in the way of other activities or responsibilities.

WHO's physical activity page says any amount of physical activity is better than none, and that people of every age should limit time spent sitting. It gives no screen-time limit for children, so we do not invent one here. A football game with neighbours, a walk to the shop or helping at home all count as movement.

## Signs to watch, and what they do not prove

If you are worried, it helps to watch the pattern over time, not one bad evening. Using WHO's own words as a guide, you might notice:

- Your child cannot seem to cut down, even when they want to.
- Games keep winning over schoolwork, meals, sleep or friends.
- Play continues even after real trouble, such as falling grades or constant arguments.
- Changes in mood, health or friendships that seem linked to gaming.

None of these signs proves a disorder. A child may play late during a school holiday. A teenager may be upset about something else that has nothing to do with games. Heavy play can sometimes be a way to cope with stress, loneliness or bullying, and the real issue may lie elsewhere.

So please do not label your child, and do not label yourself. Describe what you see, stay curious, and ask for help if the worry stays. Avoid casual words such as "addict". WHO uses careful wording, and a harsh label can make a young person stop talking to you.

## Fair family rules that children accept

Rules work better when children help make them. Here are ideas drawn from the ESRB and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), which publishes a family media plan.

1. **Start with a calm talk.** The ESRB suggests a conversation without judgement that accepts games are part of your child's life. Ask what they enjoy about their favourite game.
2. **Agree on simple rules together.** An example from the ESRB is play after homework is finished, or devices put away at dinner.
3. **Use screen-free zones.** The AAP names the dinner table, homework time and bedtime as times to keep screens away. It says this supports family connection, learning and sleep.
4. **Allow logical stopping points.** Some games only save at certain places, and leaving an online match can let teammates down. The ESRB suggests giving a few extra minutes, such as "five more minutes", to respect your child's hobby and friendships.
5. **Make room for other things.** The AAP suggests reading, outdoor play, family games and hobbies, so that screens do not crowd out other experiences.
6. **Choose content with care.** Check age ratings before a game is installed. See our post on [game age ratings and parental controls](/post/video-games-updates/game-age-ratings-esrb-pegi-and-parental-controls/).
7. **Review the rules.** The ESRB says to revisit rules as children grow. The AAP suggests reviewing at the start of a school year or during holidays.

Parental controls on devices can help too. The ESRB says they can manage who your child plays with, how much they can spend, and when and for how long they play. It calls them a backup to house rules, not a replacement for talking.

Money is part of healthy play as well. If your child buys items in a game, read our post on [loot boxes and in-game purchases](/post/video-games-updates/loot-boxes-and-in-game-purchases-protect-your-money/).

One more thing about rules. Try to follow them yourself. If the family rule is no phones at dinner, that includes adults. Children notice, and they respond to fairness.

## When to ask for help

Consider talking to a doctor, a clinical psychologist or another qualified health professional if:

- the worry has lasted and does not ease with kind, clear family rules;
- sleep, school, health or relationships are clearly suffering;
- your child seems very low, anxious or withdrawn;
- you are unsure what is happening and want a professional opinion.

You do not have to wait for a perfect moment. Asking early is not an overreaction. Start with a local clinic or hospital, or with a school counsellor if your child's school has one. We cannot name a specific service for every area, so please ask a local health worker where to go.

If you are a young person reading this and feel your gaming is hard to control, you are not weak. Tell someone you trust, such as a parent, an older relative or a teacher, and ask them to help you find a health professional.

## Common questions

### How many hours of gaming is too many?
Neither WHO nor the ESRB gives a number on the pages we read. The ESRB suggests asking whether play gets in the way of other activities. If sleep, school, meals or friendships are suffering, it is time for a calm talk.

### Does my child have gaming disorder if they play every day?
Not necessarily. WHO says most people who play games are not affected. Only a professional can judge. Daily play that fits well with school, family and sleep is not the same as the pattern WHO describes.

### Should I take the console or phone away?
Sudden punishment often leads to hiding and arguments. The ESRB recommends talking first, building rules together and allowing sensible stopping points. If you do set a limit, explain why and say when you will review it.

### Are games always bad for children?
No. Games can be a way to enjoy time with friends and to learn. The question is balance and content. Check the age rating and keep other parts of life going.

## What to do next

1. Choose a calm time and ask your child what they like about their games. Listen first.
2. Write two or three family rules together, such as screens off at dinner and at bedtime, and review them in a few weeks.
3. Add something active or social that the whole family can enjoy.
4. If the worry continues, speak with a qualified health professional.

Caring about how your child plays is a form of love. Keep the door open, stay kind, and remember that you are on the same side.
