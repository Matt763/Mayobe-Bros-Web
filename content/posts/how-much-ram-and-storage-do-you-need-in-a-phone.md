You have saved for months, and now every phone box shows big numbers. It is easy to think the biggest number wins. It does not. The best phone for you is the one that fits what you do every day and still works well in three years.

This post explains RAM and storage in plain words, shows you how to check your own use, and gives a simple way to choose when your budget is fixed.

## The short answer

RAM is the phone's short-term working space. Storage is where your photos, apps and files are kept. Most readers feel storage problems sooner than RAM problems, so storage and software updates should usually come first in your decision.

This is our editors' guidance, not an official rule. No maker publishes a number that is "enough" for everyone.

- Check how much storage you use now, and plan for more.
- Ask how many years the phone will get software updates.
- Look at the battery, then at RAM.
- Do not pay extra for a number you will never use.
- Test the phone in the shop with your own apps if you can.

## What RAM does, and what storage does

Think of a desk and a cupboard. RAM is the desk. It holds what you are using right now. Storage is the cupboard. It keeps things for later, even when the phone is off.

### RAM, the desk

When you open an app, the phone loads part of it into RAM so it can run fast. Android's developer pages explain that when you leave an app, the phone does not always close it. It keeps the app in a cache so that switching back is quicker. When memory runs low, the system closes cached apps to make room (Android Developers, as checked in November 2026).

So a phone with less RAM may reload your apps more often. You may open WhatsApp, switch to a video and come back to find the chat has to load again. That is annoying, but it does not harm the phone.

### Storage, the cupboard

Storage holds your apps, photos, videos, voice notes, school PDFs and downloads. When it is nearly full, the phone slows down, cannot take new photos and fails to install updates. This is the problem most people meet first.

Some phones take a memory card, which adds storage. Others do not. Ask before you buy, and remember that a card does not help with every kind of file, because some apps must stay on the phone's own storage.

## How to check how much storage you really use

Before you buy, look at your current phone. It shows what you really need, not what an advertisement says.

### On Android

Open Settings and tap Storage. Tap a category to see what uses the space. Google's help page also describes a Smart Storage setting that can clear photos and videos that are already backed up when storage is almost full (Google Android Help, as checked in November 2026). Some steps on that page need Android 14 or later, and menu names may differ by brand and Android version.

### On iPhone

Open Settings, tap General, then tap the storage entry for your device. Apple's page says you will see recommendations and how much each app uses. You can offload an app, which frees the space the app uses but keeps its documents and data (Apple Support, as checked in November 2026).

### What to look for

Write down three figures: the space used in total, the space used by photos and videos, and the space used by apps and chat media. Photos, videos and WhatsApp media are often the largest part. If you already use most of your storage, a phone with the same amount will feel full within a year.

## A simple way to match a phone to your use

Make a short list of what you do on your phone every day.

- **Mostly WhatsApp, calls, mobile money and light browsing.** You need little RAM, but you still want enough storage for photos and chat media.
- **Many photos and videos.** Choose more storage first. RAM matters less.
- **Heavy games or video editing.** RAM matters more, and so does a good battery. Test the phone before paying.
- **School and study.** PDFs and notes are small. Video lectures are not. See [how to save data when studying online](/post/educational/online-learning/save-mobile-data-when-studying-online/) if your bundle is the real limit.

Our editors suggest this order when you compare phones: storage, update years, battery, then RAM. It is advice, not a law, and you may weigh them differently.

## Software updates: ask how many years the phone will get them

Updates fix security holes and keep apps working. A phone that stops getting them is riskier to use, however much RAM it has. A phone with big numbers but no updates is not a bargain.

Some makers publish a clear promise. For example, Google says Pixel 8 and later phones get 7 years of Android and security updates, counted from the day the phone first went on sale in the US. Pixel Fold and the Pixel 6 and 7 series get 5 years. Pixel 5a and older no longer get updates (Google Pixel Help, as checked in November 2026). This is only an example of what to ask any maker. Other brands may have different promises, so ask the shop and read the maker's own page.

After you buy, check where you are. On many Android phones, open Settings, tap About phone, then tap Android version. You will see the Android version and the Android security update date. Google's page notes that update schedules depend on the device, the maker and sometimes the carrier (Google Android Help, as checked in November 2026).

A phone that is cheap but old may already be near the end of its updates. Ask the date it was first sold.

## A simple way to decide with a TSh budget (hypothetical example)

The numbers below are made up to show the method. They are not real prices.

Say you have TSh 600,000. You find two phones at that price.

- **Phone A:** more RAM, less storage, updates for 2 more years.
- **Phone B:** a little less RAM, twice the storage, updates for 4 more years.

Now ask yourself three things.

1. Do you take many photos and keep many chats? If yes, storage matters most, so B looks better.
2. Do you plan to keep the phone for more than two years? If yes, the longer updates help, so B looks better again.
3. Do you play heavy games? If yes, test both and see which one runs your games well.

You may still choose A, and that is fine. The point is to choose on your own use, not on the biggest number.

Another hypothetical: if your phone is used for 40 GB of photos and apps now, a phone with 64 GB leaves little room, because the system also needs space. Move up a size if you can afford it. Check the maker's page for how much of the stated storage is free for you, as it is always less than the number on the box.

## Common questions

### How much RAM is enough?

No official source gives one answer. It depends on how many apps you use together and how heavy they are. If you mostly chat, call and browse, a phone with modest RAM can be fine. If you play heavy games or keep many apps open, more RAM will help. Test the phone with your own apps.

### Does a memory card fix a full phone?

It helps with photos, videos and music if the phone has a card slot. It does not make the phone's working memory bigger, and not every app can be moved to a card.

### Is it better to buy a new phone or a used one?

Either can work. If you buy used, read [our checklist for buying a used phone](/post/technology-solutions/smartphones/buying-a-used-phone-in-tanzania-what-to-check/) and ask how many years of updates are left.

### Can I free up space without buying a new phone?

Often yes. Check what uses space, delete things you do not need and back up your photos before you delete them. Offloading apps on iPhone and Smart Storage on some Android phones can help.

## What to do next

1. Open your phone's storage page today and write down what uses the space.
2. Decide how long you want to keep your next phone, then ask how many years of updates it will get.
3. Make a short list of two or three phones and test each with your own apps in the shop.
4. Set aside a little money for a case and a charger, since the box may not include them.

A good choice is not the loudest phone in the shop. It is the one that quietly does what you need, year after year.
