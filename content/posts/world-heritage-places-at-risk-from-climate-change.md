Headlines often tell people to "visit before it disappears". The evidence is more careful than that. UNESCO and its partners describe real climate risks at some World Heritage sites, but they also say outcomes depend on what people do. This post sets out what the main sources say about four places, separates what is measured from what is predicted, and shows what a traveller can do. Every statement about a site below is from the UNESCO pages listed at the end, as checked on 24 November 2026.

## Key points

- UNESCO lists climate change among the threats to several World Heritage sites, including Kilimanjaro, Kilwa Kisiwani and the Great Barrier Reef.
- A 2022 UNESCO and IUCN study says glaciers in a third of the 50 World Heritage sites with glaciers are set to disappear by 2050. It says the other two thirds can be saved if warming stays within 1.5°C.
- For Africa, UNESCO says the available data show that glaciers in all World Heritage sites there will very likely be gone by 2050, including Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya. That is a projection, not a certainty.
- Sites can be damaged by many pressures at once, so climate is rarely the only cause.
- Staying away is not the answer. Careful travel can bring money and attention, if it is done responsibly.

## What the evidence can and cannot say

A **measurement** says what has already happened, such as how much ice a glacier lost. A **projection** says what is likely to happen under certain conditions, such as a level of warming. UNESCO's glacier study uses the word "very likely" and ties the result to future emissions. Treat these as strong warnings, not as dates written in stone.

Also remember that "at risk" is not the same as "lost". UNESCO keeps a separate List of World Heritage in Danger. A site can face climate threats without being on it.

## Kilimanjaro: the ice on Africa's highest mountain

Kilimanjaro National Park was listed in 1987. UNESCO's own page for the site says its glaciers "are vulnerable to retreat" and are cited as a feature of particular vulnerability to global climate change. It lists climate change next to fire, invasive species and pollution as stresses on the park.

The 2022 study adds numbers for the wider picture. UNESCO says the 50 World Heritage sites with glaciers hold 18,600 glaciers, covering around 66,000 square kilometres, almost 10% of the Earth's glacierised area. They are losing 58 billion tons of ice every year, according to the study. For Africa, UNESCO's news item says that, according to available data, the glaciers in all World Heritage sites there will very likely be gone by 2050, including those on Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya.

What does this mean for you? The summit will still be a mountain. The ice cap, which is part of what made the mountain famous, is what is under threat. For more on the listed Tanzanian sites, read our explainer on [Tanzania's World Heritage Sites](/post/tanzania-news/tanzania-world-heritage-sites-explained/).

## The Swahili coast: Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara

The ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara were listed in 1981. In its description of the management of the site, UNESCO names "climate change impact due to increased wave action and beach erosion" among the key management issues. The same page also lists other problems: people and animals moving onto the site, an incomplete conservation programme for the monuments, and too little community involvement.

This is an honest example of how heritage works. The sea is one pressure. Maintenance, funding and local involvement are others. The history of the town is told in our post on [Kilwa Kisiwani](/post/world-histories/kilwa-kisiwani-swahili-trade-city/). We did not find a UNESCO statement tying climate change to Stone Town of Zanzibar in the pages we opened, so we make no claim about it here.

## The Great Barrier Reef, Australia

UNESCO's page for the reef says a 2009 Outlook Report found that the long-term challenges are "dominated by climate change over the next few decades". It also says the extent of damage will depend on how much the world's climate changes and on how well the reef can recover.

The condition of the reef changes from year to year. Australia's Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) publishes an annual summary of coral cover, and it has shown both sharp falls after marine heatwaves and later recoveries. We could not open the latest summary this session, so we give no coral cover numbers here. If you want the current picture, read the newest AIMS report directly.

## Venice, Italy

UNESCO describes the lagoon of Venice as having become "vulnerable as a result of irreversible natural and climate changes". Its page on conservation says the rise in the frequency and level of high tides, together with waves from motor boats, is one of the main causes of damage to buildings and urban areas. The page also says this does not at present endanger the integrity of the property, and that tourism-related change in the city is a major concern too.

## What is being done

- **Monitoring and reporting.** UNESCO asks countries to report on the state of conservation of their sites and monitors the threats.
- **Calls for emission cuts.** UNESCO's glacier report says that only a rapid cut in CO2 emissions can save glaciers, and it calls for an international fund for glacier monitoring.
- **Local management.** Management plans for sites such as Venice include monitoring systems for high water, according to UNESCO.

## What travellers can do

These are practical suggestions, not claims from the sources above.

- **Travel fewer, longer trips** when you can, and choose lower-carbon transport where it is practical.
- **Hire local guides and stay in locally owned places.** Money that stays in the area helps pay for care.
- **Follow the site rules.** Stay on paths, carry out your rubbish and do not touch coral.
- **Never buy coral, shells or other natural souvenirs** from a reef or protected place.
- **Do not believe "last chance" marketing.** Look for facts from UNESCO or the managing authority.

## Common questions

### Will Kilimanjaro lose all its ice?
UNESCO says that, according to available data, the glaciers of African World Heritage sites, including Kilimanjaro, will very likely be gone by 2050. It is a projection based on current data and it could change.

### Is the Great Barrier Reef dead?
No. Its condition changes from year to year, and the results published by AIMS show that. UNESCO says that the extent of damage depends on how the climate changes and on the reef's resilience.

### Are these places on the danger list because of climate?
Not necessarily. Being at risk is different from being on the List of World Heritage in Danger. Check UNESCO's page for each site.

### Should I avoid visiting?
That is a personal choice. The sources here do not say that visitors should stay away. They stress cutting emissions and good management. Visit responsibly.

## What to do next

1. Read the UNESCO page of any site you plan to visit.
2. Choose local guides and follow the site rules.
3. Share facts from the source, not "last chance" headlines.

Caring about a place can start with learning what is true about it.
