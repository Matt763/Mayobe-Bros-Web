When you badly need work, a message that says "You have been selected" can feel like an answer to prayer. That hope is exactly what scammers use. This post helps you slow down, check, and protect your money and your documents.

## The short answer

A real employer does not ask you to pay to get a job, to be shortlisted or to attend an interview. If someone asks for money before you start work, treat the offer as fake until an official source proves otherwise. Stop, do not pay, and check with the organisation using contact details you find yourself.

- Never pay for the promise of a job, a "form", a "medical", a "uniform" or a "training".
- Do not trust a job that you did not apply for, especially one that arrives by SMS or WhatsApp.
- Public service jobs are applied for through the official recruitment portal, not through a person.
- Check the offer in another place before you reply.
- If you paid, act quickly and report it.

## Warning signs of a fake job offer

Several official bodies describe the same pattern. The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) says honest employers do not charge applicants, and its advice is simple: do not pay for the promise of a job. The International Labour Organization (ILO) says in its fair recruitment principles that no recruitment fees or related costs should be charged to workers or job seekers. Those principles are not a law, but they show a common global standard.

This is general advice from outside Tanzania, and it matches the local pattern. Watch for these signs:

- **They ask for money.** Fees for registration, shortlisting, interview, training, a uniform or a "security". The FTC lists fees for kits, training and certificates as a warning sign.
- **You never applied.** A surprise offer for a job you did not apply for is a reason to be careful.
- **Big pay for little work.** The FTC says offers of high earnings for little effort are almost certainly scams.
- **Hiring only by chat.** No office, no interview, no written job description, only a chat app or a phone call.
- **A pressure clock.** "Pay today or lose the post."
- **A cheque that needs a refund.** The FTC describes fake cheque scams, where a new "employer" sends a payment and asks you to send part of it back or buy something. The cheque later bounces and you lose your own money.
- **Contacts that do not check out.** Free email addresses for a large organisation, a phone that does not answer, a website that has disappeared.
- **Placement firms that charge up front.** The FTC says honest placement firms are usually paid by the employer, not by you.
- **Requests for ID, bank or mobile money details** before any real hiring step.

One more point: a real organisation can still be pretended. A fake advert may use a real company's name. That is why checking matters. We do not name any company here, because a fake message does not mean the company did anything wrong.

## What real recruitment looks like

Real recruitment leaves a trail you can check.

For jobs in the central public service, the vacancy announcement we read in November 2026 said that all applications must be sent through the official recruitment portal and that applications sent through any other means will not be considered. It also said only shortlisted candidates will be told of the interview date. It did not mention a fee. So a person who says "give me money and I will put your name in" is not following the official route. See our guide to [applying through the Ajira Portal](/post/jobs-and-internships/ajira-portal-how-to-apply-public-service-jobs/).

For private employers, you can usually expect:

- An advert on the employer's own website or official page
- A clear job title, duties and qualifications
- A named contact and a real address
- A written interview invitation, and an interview at a real office or on a known video platform
- A written contract before you start work, with no payment from you

Some organisations have published their own warnings. World Vision Tanzania has one that says it does not charge for recruitment, but we could not open that page when we checked in November 2026, so we do not quote it. If you think an offer is from a big organisation, find its official careers page and look for a warning or a list of real vacancies.

## A five-minute check before you reply

Take these steps before you send a document, a payment or even your phone number.

1. **Pause.** Do not answer straight away. Tell a trusted person, as the FTC advises.
2. **Find the employer yourself.** Search for the organisation's own website or official page. Do not use links or numbers sent in the message.
3. **Look for the same advert there.** If the vacancy is not on the official page, ask the organisation using the contact details you found yourself.
4. **Search the offer.** Type the company name or the person's name with the word "scam" or "complaint". The FTC suggests this check.
5. **Read the message again.** Does it ask for money, ID, bank or mobile money details? If so, stop.
6. **Look at how they want to talk.** Real interviews do not happen only through voice notes.
7. **Do not send money, even a small amount.** A small fee is a test. If you pay once, they will ask again.

If the offer passes all these steps, continue carefully. If you are not sure, you can wait. A real job will still be there tomorrow.

## Calls and messages that pretend to be a ministry

The Prime Minister's Office, Labour, Employment and Relations published a public notice on 15 October 2026. We looked at the notice on its website. It warned about fraudulent calls from people pretending to be staff of that office. The callers were contacting people who had applied for work permits, appeals and exemptions, and demanding money with promises to speed up their applications.

That notice is about work permits, not about job vacancies, so we do not claim it covers other jobs. But the pattern is the same one to look for in any government matter:

- The office said those callers are not its employees.
- It said it does not give decisions about work permits, appeals or exemptions by telephone. It says decisions are published on its official website.
- It asked the public to ignore such calls and to report similar incidents to the office, using the contacts on the notice. We do not print the numbers here. Please take them from the notice on the official site, www.kazi.go.tz.

The lesson is useful for any office. An official body does not need your money to process a case, and it does not give you results by a surprise phone call. If a caller says they are from a ministry, end the call and contact the ministry using the details on its own website.

## What to do if you already paid or shared documents

First, do not feel ashamed. Scammers are practised, and many careful people have been caught. Act in this order:

1. **Stop all payments.** Do not send more money to "release" what you paid.
2. **Contact your mobile money provider or bank straight away.** Tell them what happened. The FTC advises contacting the company you used to send money and asking whether it can be reversed. We cannot say that reversal will be possible in Tanzania, but a quick call is always worth it.
3. **Keep the evidence.** Save messages, receipts, numbers and screenshots. Do not delete the chat.
4. **Report it.** Use the reporting contacts of the organisation being copied, and the police. For a scam that pretends to be a government office, use the contacts on the official notice. The FTC runs its own reporting page, but that is for US cases.
5. **Protect your identity.** If you sent copies of your ID or certificates, watch for strange calls or messages, and change passwords on email and mobile money accounts that you linked to them.
6. **Warn others.** Tell your friends and family group, without naming private people.

We are not lawyers, so for legal advice ask a qualified person or an official office.

## Common questions

### Is it normal to pay for a medical test before I start a job?

A job offer should not depend on a payment to the person offering the job. If a medical check is needed, ask the employer in writing, and verify the request with the organisation using contacts you found yourself.

### Can a real recruiter charge a fee?

The ILO principle says no recruitment fees or related costs should be charged to job seekers. The FTC says honest placement firms are usually paid by the employer. Treat any up-front fee as a red flag.

### I got a job offer on WhatsApp. Is it always fake?

Not always, but a job offer with no interview, no written contract and a request for money is very likely fake. Check it using the five-minute list above.

### Who can I ask if I am not sure?

Ask the organisation using official contact details, a teacher, a careers office at your school, or an adult you trust.

## What to do next

1. Read your messages again and look for any request for money or ID.
2. Do the five-minute check on any offer you are considering.
3. Save the official website of the organisations you want to work for.
4. Share this post with a friend who is job hunting.

Hope is not a weakness. It just needs a little protection, and now you know how to give it some.
