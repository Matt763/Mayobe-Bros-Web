Most university students in Tanzania are not held back by a lack of ability. The usual causes are smaller and more ordinary: fees that arrive late, a weak phone, no quiet place to read, or too much stress and no one to talk to. This post looks at the common barriers and at what students, parents and lecturers can do about each one. It is practical advice, not a research report.

We have kept the claims modest. We use only facts we could trace to a source, and we do not quote statistics or stories we could not confirm.

## Key points

- Most barriers can be reduced with early planning: apply for the loan on time, budget for data, and ask for help before you fall behind.
- Free course materials online can fill gaps, but they do not replace your own university's syllabus and exams.
- Study groups work best when they are small and have a clear plan.
- Stress and low mood affect studying. Using campus counselling or a clinic is sensible, not weak.
- Lecturers and parents help most by being clear, patient and available, not by adding pressure.

## Barrier 1: Money

Fees, accommodation, food, transport and books add up quickly, and families often pay in parts. The first step is to know your full yearly cost before the semester starts.

Write a simple list for the year. Put tuition, hostel or rent, food, transport (for example daladala fares to campus), printing, books and data. Then mark which parts are paid by a loan, a sponsor or your family, and which parts you must cover yourself.

If you need a loan, apply early through the Higher Education Students' Loans Board (HESLB). Application windows are set each year. As checked in November 2026, applications for 2026/2027 were closed, so watch [heslb.go.tz](https://www.heslb.go.tz) for the next opening. Our post on [how to apply for a HESLB student loan](/post/educational/study-tips/students-loan-tanzania-complete-guide/) explains the steps.

Other ideas that cost nothing:

- Ask your university's student affairs or finance office about payment plans, bursaries or part-time campus work. Policies differ by institution, so ask directly.
- Buy second-hand textbooks from older students and share one copy within a study group.
- Use the library for expensive books. Photocopy only the chapters you need.
- Be careful with quick-money offers. Anything that asks for an upfront fee to "guarantee" a scholarship or loan is a warning sign.

## Barrier 2: Data, devices and electricity

Online learning has become common at many universities, but not every student has a laptop or steady internet. You can still study well on a basic setup.

![Two students look at a tablet showing a 3D model of an electric motor while others work on laptops in a library](assets/posts/d11250cb04a0.webp)

Some low-cost habits help:

1. **Download at the library or campus Wi-Fi, study later at home.** Save PDFs, lecture notes and videos while you have a good connection.
2. **Choose a monthly bundle rather than many small ones.** Compare the bundles from Vodacom, Tigo, Airtel and Halotel for your area. Prices and offers change, so check before you buy.
3. **Lower video quality.** Watching at 360p uses much less data than high definition.
4. **Charge your phone and power bank before long study sessions.** Load shedding can interrupt study at the worst time.
5. **Learn the tools your university uses.** Many institutions use a learning platform such as Moodle, a student portal or email. Ask your department what they use and how to log in.

If your university offers computer labs, find out the opening hours and what you need to register.

### Free learning material online

Free course material can help you understand a topic that your lecturer covered quickly. MIT OpenCourseWare, for example, publishes materials from thousands of MIT courses for free, with no account needed. It also has offline options for some courses and lower-bandwidth mirror sites. It does not give credit or certificates, so treat it as extra study help.

Khan Academy and similar sites also offer free lessons in maths and science. Always check that the topic matches your own syllabus. If the lecturer's notes and an online source disagree, ask the lecturer.

## Barrier 3: Studying with little guidance

Many first-year students find that university study is different from secondary school. Nobody reminds you to revise. Assignments are longer, and the reading is heavier.

Here is a simple weekly routine:

- On Sunday evening, write down every lecture, assignment and test for the week.
- Block two or three fixed study times on days when you are usually free.
- After each lecture, spend ten minutes the same day to tidy your notes and write down questions.
- Visit your lecturer's office hours with specific questions, not just "I don't understand".

For methods that make revision more effective, see our post on [10 study techniques that work](/post/educational/study-tips/10-proven-techniques-to-study-effectively-for-students/).

If your writing or English is a worry, many students struggle with this. Ask whether your university has a writing centre or a communication skills course, and read a little in English each day. Reading news or textbook summaries in short sessions helps over time.

## Barrier 4: Working while studying

Some students work part time to pay rent or help family. That is hard, and the answer is not "just try harder". It is better to plan.

- Tell your lecturers early if your work hours clash with classes. Some will allow notes or recordings.
- Pick the few courses or tasks that matter most each week. Do those first.
- Protect your sleep. Tired study is slow study.
- Keep work hours the same each week if you can, so that you can plan study around them.

## Barrier 5: Stress, loneliness and mental health

Starting university can feel lonely, especially if you have moved from another region or you are the first person in your family to attend. Pressure from exams, money and family expectations makes it worse.

Some signs that you may need support: you cannot sleep for several nights, you avoid class, you feel hopeless for more than a couple of weeks, or you have lost interest in things you usually enjoy. None of this is a character flaw.

What you can do:

- Talk to a friend, a relative, a religious leader or a trusted lecturer.
- Find out whether your university has a counselling service or a student clinic.
- See a doctor or nurse if the feeling does not improve. We cannot give medical advice, and a health worker can guide you properly.
- Make small daily habits: regular meals, a walk, and fixed sleep times.

If you ever feel you might harm yourself, go to the nearest hospital or ask someone near you to take you now. Do not wait.

## Study groups and peer help

Learning with friends can help because you hear explanations in your own words and find gaps in what you know. Some tips for a group that works:

1. Keep it to three to six people.
2. Decide the topic before you meet.
3. Take turns explaining a topic aloud. If you can't explain it, you've found what to study.
4. Share notes in a WhatsApp group, but keep the chat on topic.
5. Include quiet members and be kind about mistakes.

A group is not a place to copy assignments. Copying can lead to disciplinary action at most universities, and it does not help you in exams.

## What lecturers and universities can do

These are suggestions, not a checklist from a regulator.

- Share the syllabus, assessment dates and marking criteria in the first week.
- Upload notes in a form that works on phones, such as light PDFs, and avoid very large files.
- Allow questions by email or a class group, and reply within a stated time.
- Point students to counselling, finance and disability support early.
- Make lecture halls, materials and exams accessible for students with disabilities, for example by providing large print or extra time where the institution's policy allows.

## What parents and families can do

- Ask how studies are going, and listen before giving advice.
- Plan fees and upkeep early and talk honestly about what is affordable.
- Avoid comparing your child with others. Pressure rarely helps.
- If your child is struggling, encourage them to speak to a lecturer or counsellor.

## Common questions

### Can I learn a full degree online for free?

No. Free sites can teach you topics, but they don't give you a recognised degree. For a degree you must register with an institution accredited in Tanzania. The Tanzania Commission for Universities (TCU) is the body that regulates universities, so check there if you are unsure about a programme.

### What if I have no laptop?

Many students manage with a smartphone, the library and computer labs. Use a laptop when you must type long assignments, and plan lab time.

### How do I find a good study group?

Ask classmates after lectures. Start with two or three people who are serious but friendly, and test a few meetings before you commit.

### Is it normal to feel behind in first year?

Yes, many students feel this way. Go to your lecturers, make a weekly plan, and ask for help early.

## What to do next

1. Write down your full cost for the year and the source of each payment.
2. Make a weekly timetable with fixed study blocks.
3. Find out where your campus offers counselling, finance help and computer labs.
4. Join or start a small study group this month.
