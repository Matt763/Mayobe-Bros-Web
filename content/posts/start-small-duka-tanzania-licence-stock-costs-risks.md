For many families, a duka is the first business and sometimes the first real hope of steady money. If you are thinking about opening one, you are not alone, and your dream is worth taking seriously. Take it seriously with your eyes open. This post covers the papers you may need, a made-up budget so you can see how the money moves, and the ways a small shop can lose money. We cannot promise profit, and no honest guide can.

## The short answer

Get your papers in order first, then buy stock. Keep enough cash aside so the shop can survive a slow month.

- Get a TIN from TRA within 15 days of starting the business.
- Get a business licence from your council trade office. The council sets its own fee, so ask it.
- If you sell food or cosmetics, ask the Tanzania Bureau of Standards (TBS) about premises registration.
- Do not put all your money into stock. A full shelf is not cash in your hand.
- Keep shop money and home money apart from day one.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Steps are as checked in November 2026 and can change.

## The papers a duka needs

**TIN.** TRA's page says that a person who starts a business should apply for a TIN within 15 days of starting. You attach a letter from your Local Government Authority and a lease agreement or title deed for the shop place. Details are in our post on [how to register a small business](/post/business-ideas/startups/register-small-business-tanzania-brela-tin-licence/).

**Tax clearance and licence.** After the TIN, TRA says there is an assessment interview, and after you meet your tax duties you get a tax clearance. You then use it to get a business licence from the trade office in the district, municipal or city council. BRELA's page says local businesses get a Class B licence from the council. It lists the documents an individual usually needs: NIN, TIN, proof of the business place, tax clearance, and a permit from the regulator when the business needs one.

We could not find council licence fees on any official page we read. They are set locally. Visit your council trade office and ask what the fee is and how to pay it officially.

**TBS premises registration.** TBS says it registers premises used for food and cosmetics businesses. Its page names food and cosmetic shops, supermarkets, restaurants and butcheries. The online application attaches your TIN, building drawings or photos, your business licence, and a map showing where the building is. TBS gives the fee after you choose the type of activity, and an inspector visits after payment. The page does not list amounts. If you plan to sell food, ask TBS whether your duka needs this.

**Medicines.** Do not sell medicines without the right permit. We did not check any detail on this, so ask the Tanzania Medicines and Medical Devices Authority (TMDA) before you stock anything of the kind.

## What you will spend money on: a made-up budget

Say you have TSh 1,000,000. This is a pretend example. Your rent, stock and papers will cost different amounts. Here is how a careful split might look.

| Use of money | TSh |
| --- | --- |
| Shelves, counter and a lock | 150,000 |
| Scale, notebook, bags, small tools | 50,000 |
| First stock | 600,000 |
| Reserve for papers and fees (ask the council and TBS for real amounts) | 100,000 |
| Cash reserve for slow days | 100,000 |
| **Total** | **1,000,000** |

Check the sum: 150,000 plus 50,000 is 200,000. Add 600,000 and you reach 800,000. Add 100,000 and 100,000 more, and you reach 1,000,000.

Notice that stock is only 60% of the money. That is on purpose. If you spent all TSh 1,000,000 on shelves and goods, you would have no money for the first rent bill, a new licence or a bad week.

For a longer look at estimating costs and the number of sales you need, read our post on a [one-page business plan](/post/business-ideas/startups/write-one-page-business-plan-small-business-tanzania/).

## Cash flow: why a full shelf is not cash in hand

Cash flow means the money that moves in and out of your hands. A shop can have lots of stock and still be unable to pay its rent.

Imagine your made-up rent is TSh 50,000 a month, and it is due on the 1st. On the 28th you spend your last cash to restock because the sugar is finished. On the 1st, the shelves look full but you have TSh 5,000 in the drawer. You cannot pay with sugar.

Some habits that help:

- Keep a cash reserve, like the TSh 100,000 above, and treat it as off limits for stock.
- Buy more of the items that sell every week and less of the items that sit.
- Write down the day you buy each item and the day it sells. You will quickly see what is slow.
- Count the cash and the stock at the end of each week.

## Credit, expired stock and other ways to lose money

**Credit to customers.** Giving goods "on credit" feels kind, and in a small community it is hard to say no. Look at what it does. Say ten customers each take goods worth TSh 30,000 and promise to pay later. That is TSh 300,000 of your stock gone, with no cash back. In the made-up budget, that is half of your first stock. If you give credit, write each name, date and amount in a notebook, set a small limit, and agree a date to pay.

**Expired or spoiled goods.** Food, drinks and some cosmetics have dates. Say you buy 20 packs at TSh 1,500 each, which is TSh 30,000, and they pass their date unsold. That money is lost. Check dates when you buy, put older goods in front, and buy small amounts of slow items.

**Slow-moving stock.** An item that sells one piece a month locks up cash. Sell it at a lower price once, learn from it, and do not buy it again.

**Mixing shop and home money.** Taking a little from the drawer for a household need is how many owners lose track. Pay yourself a fixed amount each week and write it down. Everything else stays in the shop.

**Theft and mistakes.** Count change carefully and keep a simple daily record. If you hire a helper, check the stock regularly.

## Receipts, tax and records from day one

Start a notebook on the first day. Write the date, what you bought and from whom, what you sold in total, and your costs. Keep supplier receipts.

On tax, TRA's page says that a resident individual with only business income and turnover up to TSh 200 million can fall under the presumptive tax. For turnover up to TSh 4,000,000 the table shows no tax. Above that, the tax is lower with complete records than with incomplete ones in the middle bands. TRA also says a new taxpayer pays no tax for the first 12 months if they qualify and claim it. We explain the table, with examples, in our post on [small business tax in Tanzania](/post/business-ideas/startups/small-business-tax-tanzania-presumptive-tax-explained/).

TRA also says a seller whose yearly turnover is assessed at TSh 11 million or more must give fiscal receipts using an approved electronic device. Ask TRA when this applies to you.

## Common questions

### How much money do I need to open a duka?
It depends on your place, your goods and your rent. We do not give one number. Use the budget layout above with real prices from your area.

### Should I sell on credit?
It is your choice, but it is one of the fastest ways to run short of cash. If you do, keep a written list, set a limit and ask for payment on a fixed date.

### Do I need a licence for a very small shop?
Ask your council trade office. TRA and BRELA describe a business licence as part of starting a business, and councils set the local rules and fees.

### Can I mix a duka with other goods like airtime or mobile money?
Those services have their own rules and agreements with the companies. We did not check them, so ask the company or agent network directly.

## What to do next

1. Visit your council trade office and ask what papers and fees apply to a duka at your location.
2. Choose your place and ask the owner for a written lease before you pay.
3. Make your own budget on one page, and keep a cash reserve.
4. Start a small stock of items you know will sell, and write down every sale from the first day.

You do not need a perfect shop to begin. A careful, small start gives your hard work the best chance.
