A common line says that in 1884 European leaders sat in Berlin and drew the map of Africa. It is a strong picture, and it is only partly right. The conference did matter, and its exclusion of Africans is a fact. But most of the borders were not drawn there. This post separates what happened from what people say happened.

## The short answer

The Berlin Conference was a meeting of 14 states, held from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885. It set rules for trade and shipping on the Congo and Niger rivers and for how powers should announce new claims on the African coast. It did not draw Africa's borders. Those were fixed later, over decades, by many treaties and agreements.

- No African representatives took part, according to EBSCO and the German Foreign Office archive.
- The General Act of 26 February 1885 is a set of rules. The German archive says it drew no specific borders.
- The "Scramble for Africa" had already begun before the meeting.
- A 2025 study of all 107 bilateral borders found that Europeans settled 62% of them along existing African or other political lines or earlier settlements.
- Even so, colonial rule was imposed on African peoples without their say.

## What was the Berlin Conference?

It was a meeting in Berlin hosted by Otto von Bismarck. The German Foreign Office archive says it ran from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885 and was officially called the West Africa Conference.

The 14 states, listed in the archive, were Germany (the host), the United States, the Ottoman Empire, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, France, the United Kingdom, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Spain and Sweden-Norway. The EBSCO article says the main players were France, Germany, Britain and Portugal, along with King Leopold II's International Association of the Congo, which had no legal status.

The conference was about the Congo basin and the Niger, and about avoiding fights between European powers over claims. It was not set up to draw a map.

## Who was in the room, and who was not

The delegates were European diplomats, plus the United States and the Ottoman Empire. The EBSCO article names no African representative and says the meeting happened "without the benefit of consulting the views of Africa's peoples on their own future". The German archive says the same: no African representatives were consulted.

That fact is not in dispute. Decisions that touched African lands, trade and rivers were discussed with no African leader present.

## What the powers agreed in the General Act

The final document is called the General Act. A copy of the text shows these main points:

1. **Free trade in the Congo basin (Article 1).** Trade of all nations "shall enjoy complete freedom" in the basin. The text includes in the basin Lake Tanganyika and its eastern rivers, which touches present-day Tanzania.
2. **Free navigation on the Congo and the Niger.** The German archive says the Act contained two Acts of Navigation, one for each river.
3. **Slavery (Articles 6 and 9).** The powers promised to help end slavery and the slave trade, and agreed that the territories must not be used as a market or route for slave trading.
4. **Notice of new claims (Article 34).** A power taking land on the African coast, or a protectorate there, must tell the other signatories.
5. **Effective occupation (Article 35).** Powers must set up authority in coastal areas they occupy, enough to protect existing rights and free trade.

Two cautions. First, EBSCO says that humanitarian concerns and the slave trade "played little part in the conference discussions", so the promises on slavery should not be read as the main purpose of the meeting. Second, the exact meaning of "effective occupation" was argued about. EBSCO says Britain and France preferred a loose definition and Germany a stricter one. The German archive describes it as meaning a power had to set up real authority, not just raise a flag.

The recognition of Leopold's Congo Free State by several powers is a separate matter. The German archive says its creation was agreed on the side and is not recorded in the General Act. EBSCO calls the Congo Free State one of the most brutal regimes in Africa. Belgium took the colony from Leopold in 1908.

## The myth: did Berlin draw Africa's borders?

The popular line says yes. Many historians say no. EBSCO states that Africa "was not formally partitioned" at the conference, and that the Scramble was already under way.

A 2025 summary on the AEH Network blog, by Jack Paine, Xiaoyan Qiu and Joan Ricart-Huguet, calls the importance of Berlin a "stubborn myth" among non-specialists, citing the historian P. Nugent. It points out that:

- Half of Africa's international borders did not exist in any form until 1891.
- Half were changed in major ways after 1908, more than two decades after the conference.

That is one research team's finding, published in the American Political Science Review in 2025. Historians still debate how much influence Berlin had. The sources we opened do not settle how much.

## How borders were really made, treaty by treaty

The same research team examined all 107 bilateral borders. They found that Europeans settled 62% of them in line with the locations of African precolonial states, non-European states such as the Ottoman Empire, earlier white settlements, or, less often, the movement areas of nomadic groups.

The typical border was shaped by about 3.5 features, often rivers or lakes, but also mountains and towns. Straight-line borders are less common than people think and are concentrated in thinly populated areas such as the Sahara.

So the borders you see on a map came from many deals, between Britain, France, Germany, Portugal, Belgium and others, made at different times. Both things can be true: the borders were not drawn at one table, and they were still drawn by outsiders.

## What it meant for East Africa

The World History Encyclopedia article on German East Africa gives the East African sequence. In 1884 Carl Peters collected trade agreements with local rulers, which Germany used as the basis of a legal claim. In 1885 the German government declared the territories Peters had covered to be protectorates, ignored the claims of the Sultan of Zanzibar and sent warships to the island. In 1886 Britain and Germany signed a treaty recognising each other's claims in East Africa, and Germany's share was the area that became mainland Tanzania. In 1890 a second Anglo-German agreement swapped Heligoland for British control of Zanzibar. In 1891 the German government took over the colony from the German East Africa Company.

So the German claim on the Tanganyika coast began in the same years as Berlin and used the rules of the era, but the border with British territory was settled by treaties between Britain and Germany. For what happened next, read our post on [the Maji Maji War](/post/world-histories/maji-maji-war-why-it-started/), and for the end of that rule, [how Tanganyika won independence](/post/world-histories/how-tanganyika-won-independence/).

By the early 20th century only Ethiopia, Liberia and Morocco were free of direct European control, according to EBSCO.

## Why the story matters today

Getting the history right does not make colonial rule gentler. The point is accuracy. If we say "a few men drew the map in one afternoon", we hide the long list of treaties, the many European governments involved. If we say Berlin was only a trade meeting, we hide that African peoples were left out of decisions about their own land.

It also matters for how we speak about borders now. Many national borders today began in colonial deals, but they are also the borders of countries with their own people, governments and history.

## Common questions

### Did the Berlin Conference divide Africa?

Not directly. It set rules for claims and for trade on two rivers. EBSCO says Africa was not formally partitioned there, and the borders came from later agreements.

### Were any Africans at the conference?

No African representatives are named by the sources we read, and the German Foreign Office archive says none were consulted.

### Did the conference end slavery?

No. The Act contains promises to suppress the slave trade, but EBSCO says slavery played little part in the conference discussions, and slavery lasted in many places long after 1885.

### Which countries were there?

Fourteen: Germany, the United States, the Ottoman Empire, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, France, the United Kingdom, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Spain and Sweden-Norway.

## What to do next

1. Look at the General Act itself. Articles 1, 6, 9, 34 and 35 are short enough to read in a few minutes.
2. Compare a map of Africa in 1880 with one from 1914 and note what changed and when.
3. Next time you read that "Europeans drew Africa's borders in Berlin", ask which borders, drawn when, by which treaty.

History is easier to understand when the date and the document come first and the slogan comes last.
