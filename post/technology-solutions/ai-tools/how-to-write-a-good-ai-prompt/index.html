How to Write a Good AI Prompt: A Simple 4-Part Method

A simple four-part method for asking an AI assistant clearly, with before-and-after examples for a student, a job seeker and a shop owner.

You open an AI assistant, type a few words, and get an answer that is too long, too general or just not what you wanted. That is a common first experience, and it is not your fault. The tool cannot see your situation. A small change in how you ask can fix most of it.

This post teaches one method you can remember in a few seconds. It works in any assistant, so we do not depend on one app, menu or price.

The short answer

A good request, called a prompt, usually has four parts:

  • Who: the role you want the assistant to play ("act as a careful English teacher").
  • Task: what you want done ("check this paragraph and fix mistakes").
  • Background: the facts it cannot know ("I am writing to a school head, the tone should be polite").
  • Format: the shape of the answer ("five bullet points, simple words").

You do not need all four every time. Google's own guide for writing prompts says the same: it lists these four parts and notes that using a few of them helps. Then read the answer, ask a follow-up, and check any facts before you use them.

Why does a vague request get a vague answer?

An assistant only has your words. If you type "write about farming", it has to guess everything: who will read it, how long it should be, and what you already know. So it picks a safe, middle answer.

Think of asking a bodaboda rider to take you "somewhere near the market". He can start moving, but you may end up in the wrong place. If you say "Kariakoo, the side gate near the bank, and I am in a hurry", he knows what to do. Prompts work the same way. The more useful detail you give, the less the assistant has to guess.

OpenAI's guide to writing prompts makes a similar point. It says to give clear, explicit instructions and to supply information the model would not otherwise have. Google adds that you should write in complete sentences, as you would talk to a colleague, and be specific about what you need.

What are the four parts of a good prompt?

1. Who: give it a role

A role tells the assistant what point of view and level to use. Google's example starts with a line like "You are a program manager". For your own use, try "You are a patient maths tutor for a Form Three student" or "You are a friendly shop assistant writing to a customer".

A role is not magic. It simply sets the tone and the level of detail.

2. Task: say the action you want

Use a clear verb. "Summarise", "explain", "compare", "rewrite", "make a list", "check for mistakes". Avoid "help me with this", which could mean anything.

One task at a time works best. If you need three things, ask for them in order or send three messages.

3. Background: add what it cannot know

This is the part most people skip, and it often matters most. Tell the assistant:

  • who the reader is (a lecturer, a customer, a hiring manager);
  • what you already know or have tried;
  • limits, such as word count, time or budget;
  • the text itself, if you want it improved (but read our warning on private details below).

4. Format: describe the shape of the answer

Ask for what you will actually use: a table, five bullet points, a short email of about 100 words, a step-by-step plan, or "explain it in simple English, no technical words". On a phone, shorter answers are easier to read, so you can say so.

Three before-and-after examples

All three examples below are made-up situations to show the method. They are not real people or real messages.

SituationWeak promptBetter prompt
A Form Six student revising biology"Explain photosynthesis.""You are a patient biology teacher. Explain photosynthesis to a Form Six student in simple English. Use one short example from a farm and end with three questions I can use to test myself."
A job seeker writing an email"Write a job email.""You are a careful editor. I am applying for a sales assistant job at a hardware shop in Mwanza. Here is my draft: [your draft]. Make it polite and clear, under 120 words, and keep my own details."
A shop owner replying to a customer"Reply to this customer.""You are a friendly shopkeeper. A customer says the phone cover she bought does not fit. Write a short, kind reply in simple English offering to exchange it if she brings the receipt. Two or three sentences."

Notice what changed. The better prompts name the reader, give the situation, and say how long the answer should be. None of them is long. Each is a few sentences.

In the job seeker example, the draft is yours to write first. The assistant improves your words. It should not invent your experience, and you should not send anything that is not true about you.

How do I follow up when the first answer is weak?

Google's guide treats prompting as a conversation: if the result is not right, you refine it with another message. You do not need to start again. Try one of these:

  • "Make it shorter. Maximum 80 words."
  • "Use simpler words. I am learning English."
  • "You skipped the cost. Add that."
  • "Give me two other ways to say this."
  • "Which part of your answer are you least sure about?"
  • "Show me the steps one by one, not in a paragraph."
  • "Ask me three questions you need answered before you write this."

The last one is useful when you do not know what background to give. Let the assistant ask you.

OpenAI's guide also suggests showing the assistant an example of the output you want. If you have a good email you wrote last month, paste a short version (without private details) and say "write the new one in this style."

A tip from Google's page

Google's guide says that in the Gemini app you can start a message with "Make this a power prompt:" followed by your draft, and Gemini will suggest a better version. As checked in November 2026, that is how the page describes it. Features like this can change, so treat it as a bonus and not as the method. The four parts work everywhere.

What should I do after I get an answer?

Read it, then check it. Google's guide reminds users to review the output for clarity, relevance and accuracy before using it, and says the final output is yours. Google's help page for Gemini also warns that the tool may give inaccurate information, so you should double-check what it says. That is true of every assistant.

A well-written prompt gives you a better draft. It does not make the facts correct. If the answer has names, numbers, dates, fees or rules, confirm them on an official site. We explain a quick routine in how to check an AI answer for mistakes.

Also think before you paste. Your prompt may include names, phone numbers or documents that are not yours to share. Read what you should never paste into an AI chatbot first.

Common questions

Do I need to write prompts in English?

You can try your own language, but we did not check how well any tool handles Swahili, so we cannot promise good results. If an answer in Swahili looks odd, try again in simple English, or ask for the answer in both languages and compare. Always read the result yourself.

Does a longer prompt always work better?

No. Google's guide says to keep requests brief and specific. Add detail that changes the answer, such as the reader, the length and the purpose. Leave out the rest.

Can the assistant do my homework for me?

It can, but that may break your school's rules and you will learn less. Ask it to explain, quiz you or check your own work. Read using AI to study without cheating and follow your own lecturer's rules.

Which AI tool should I use?

The method works in all of them. For an overview of tools we looked at, see 10 AI tools that can save you time at work and school. Pick one and learn it well before trying others.

What to do next

  1. Take something you asked an assistant recently and rewrite it with the four parts: who, task, background, format.
  2. Send it, then ask one follow-up to improve the answer.
  3. Check every fact in the answer before you use it.
  4. Save your best prompts in a note on your phone so you can reuse them.

Asking clearly is a skill, and like any skill it gets easier with a little practice each week. You already know how to explain what you need to a person. This is the same thing, written down.
