AI Voice and Video Scams: How to Check Before You Send Money

A call that sounds like your child or your boss can be fake. Learn five checks that still work when the voice sounds real, and what to do if you paid.

Your phone rings late in the evening. The voice is crying. It sounds like your daughter, or your brother, and it says "please, I need money now." Your heart drops before your mind can think. That is exactly what the caller wants. This post gives you a few simple habits that protect you even when the voice sounds perfect.

The short answer

You cannot tell a real voice from a copied one by listening alone, so do not try. Check by another route. Hang up, then call the person back on a number you already have. Do not send money because of a voice or a video call alone.

  • Slow down. Real emergencies can survive five minutes of checking.
  • Hang up and call back on a saved number, not the number that called you.
  • Ask something only the real person could answer.
  • Call another relative or friend, even if the caller says "keep this secret."
  • Agree a family code word now, before anyone needs it.

How does a copied voice scam work?

The United States Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is a government body that protects shoppers. It warns that scammers can use artificial intelligence (AI) to copy the voice of someone you know. The FTC says a short audio clip, which may come from something posted online, plus a voice-copying program can be enough to make a call sound like your relative.

We will not explain how the programs work, and you do not need to know. What matters is the plan behind the call. A scammer wants you to feel fear and to act before you think.

The caller may say they are your child, a cousin, a friend, or even a lawyer, a police officer or a doctor. The story is always urgent: an accident, an arrest, a hospital bill, a fee at school. You are told you are the only one who can help. You are asked to tell nobody.

This is an old trick with a new tool. Tanzania's communications regulator, the Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority (TCRA), has long told people to be careful with messages such as "your son is sick at school" and not to cooperate with them. A copied voice only makes the same story feel more real.

Why this matters in East Africa

In August 2026, INTERPOL published its African Cyberthreat Assessment Report. INTERPOL says artificial intelligence is enabling 55 per cent of the cybercrimes reported across Africa. It also says AI-made fake media, often called deepfakes, often support sextortion and online harassment. The report names East Africa as a hub for mobile money fraud.

We want to be careful here. That report does not say how many cases in Tanzania involve a copied voice, and we did not find a verified local case to describe, so we will not invent one. What it does show is that criminals are using these tools and that mobile money is a target. If you use M-Pesa, Tigo Pesa or Airtel Money, the habits below are for you.

Signs that a call or video is a trap

Your ears and eyes are not a reliable test. Look at what the caller asks for and how they ask. The FTC lists these warning signs, and they match what TCRA tells the public.

A video call is not proof either. Fake video exists too. Do not trust lip movement, blinking or picture quality as a test. Nobody can promise that such signs show up, and tools that claim to "detect" fakes are not something you should depend on. Use your own checks instead.

Five checks that work even when the voice sounds real

These checks do not depend on spotting a fake. They work because the scammer cannot easily pass them.

  1. Pause and hang up. Say "I will call you right back" and end the call. A real person in real trouble will understand. A scammer will push you to stay on the line.
  2. Call back on a number you already know. Use the contact saved in your phone, or call another number you trust, such as a school office or an employer's main line. Do not press "call back" on the missed call and do not use a number the caller gives you.
  3. Ask something only the real person knows. Choose a question that cannot be found online: the name of a childhood pet, what you ate last time you met. Do not ask something that is on their social media.
  4. Use a family code word. This is our suggestion, not an official rule. Agree one word or short phrase with the people close to you, and say it only in a real emergency. Pick something people cannot guess, and do not post it or send it in a group chat. If a caller cannot say it, stop.
  5. Contact someone else. Phone another relative, a friend or a neighbour who might know where the person is. The FTC advises this even when you were told to keep the matter secret.

If the call claims to be from your boss or a business partner, add one more rule to the list: no payment is made on a voice or a message alone. Send money only through the normal approval your workplace already uses, and confirm any change of account number in person or by calling a number you already hold.

Say you are the manager of a small shop in Mwanza. A voice that sounds like your supplier asks you to send TSh 800,000 to a new number "because the old one is blocked." That is an example, not a real case. The safe answer is: "I will call you back on the number I have." Then you call the number saved in your phone. If the supplier knows nothing about it, you have saved TSh 800,000.

What if the caller threatens or begs?

Stay kind to yourself. Panic is normal. The scam is designed to cause it. You do not need to argue, explain or be polite for long. You can say "I will call you back" and put the phone down.

If you are afraid for someone, turn that fear into action that is safe. Ring the person on the saved number. Ring the school, the hospital or the police station yourself, using a number you find on your own. If the real person answers and is fine, you can relax. If they cannot be reached, ask another relative to help you look for them before any money moves.

What can you do before anyone calls?

A little preparation makes you harder to trick.

  • Talk to older relatives. Parents and grandparents are often the target. Show them this page and practise the words "I will call you back."
  • Choose your family code word.
  • Think about what you post. Short clips of your voice on public pages can be copied. You do not have to stop sharing, but you can set accounts to friends only if that suits you.
  • Save the numbers of close relatives, your children's school and your bank or mobile money agent in your phone.
  • Ask your mobile money provider what protections it offers for your account, and use them.

If you already sent money

Act quickly. The sooner you report, the better the chance of help.

  1. Contact your mobile money provider or bank straight away and tell them what happened. TCRA suggests dialling 100 to reach your service provider.
  2. Report to the police. TCRA's frequently asked questions page says fraud is a police matter: go to a police station to report it and get an RB number (a police report number).
  3. Report the scammer's number to TCRA by SMS to 15040. TCRA pages describe the text slightly differently, so follow the automatic reply you receive. It will tell you what to send. You can also call TCRA free on 0800008272 for more information.
  4. Tell your family, so nobody else is caught by the same number.
  5. Keep screenshots, call logs and payment messages. Do not delete them.

We cannot promise that money will come back. Being quick helps, and so does having clear records.

If someone uses a fake video or voice to threaten you or share private pictures, do not pay and do not reply. Keep the evidence and report it to the police. It is not your fault.

Common questions

Can I tell a fake voice by listening carefully?

No, not reliably. Do not depend on your ear. The checks above work even when the voice is perfect.

Is a video call proof that the person is real?

No. Fake video exists, and you should not trust small visual signs. Use the same checks: call back on a saved number, ask a private question and never send money on a call alone.

Should I use an AI detection tool?

We cannot say that any such tool is dependable, and we did not find an official recommendation for one. Rely on your own habits instead.

What if the person really is in trouble?

Then checking will take only a few minutes, and the real person will not mind. Call them, call someone near them, and help in a way you can confirm, such as paying a hospital directly after you speak to the hospital on a number you found yourself.

Where do I report a scam call in Tanzania?

Go to a police station for an RB number. You can also report the number to TCRA by SMS to 15040 and follow the automatic reply. Details can change, so check the TCRA website for the current wording.

What to do next

  1. Choose a family code word today and tell the people you love.
  2. Save key numbers in your phone and teach one older relative to say "I will call you back."
  3. Read more about common job-offer scams in Tanzania, which use the same pressure tricks.
  4. Before you use any chatbot, learn how to write a good AI prompt and how AI chatbots work in plain words.

A scammer borrows a voice, but they cannot borrow your habits. A calm pause protects the people you love, and you can learn it in five minutes.
