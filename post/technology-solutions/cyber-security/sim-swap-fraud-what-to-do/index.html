SIM Swap Fraud: What It Is and What to Do Fast

Your phone loses network for no reason and codes stop arriving. Learn what SIM swap fraud is, the signs, and a first-hour plan to protect your accounts.

Your phone has no network bars. It was fine an hour ago. Most of the time this is only a network problem, and it passes. But sometimes it is a sign that someone has taken over your number. This post explains what that means in plain words and what to do if you suspect it. Read it now, while you are calm, so you know the steps if you ever need them.

The short answer

SIM swap fraud happens when a criminal gets your phone number moved to a SIM card that they control. Your calls and text messages then go to them. They can use the codes sent to your number to get into accounts. If your phone loses network for no clear reason, and especially if you also get strange alerts, act fast.

  • Use another phone to contact your operator.
  • Check the numbers registered to your ID by dialling *106#.
  • Change passwords from a safe device.
  • Where an app allows it, move important accounts away from SMS codes.
  • Report the matter, and keep a record.

What SIM swap fraud is, in plain words

Your phone number is tied to a SIM card. Many services use your number to send you a code, or to let you reset a password. That makes the number valuable.

The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) explains it like this: a scammer contacts your mobile company, says your phone was lost or damaged, and asks it to activate a new SIM linked to your number on a device the scammer controls. Once that happens, your calls and texts go to the scammer. They can then catch verification codes and try to sign in to your email, bank or social media accounts. They may also change passwords to lock you out.

The FTC page is American, written in 2019. We use only its main idea here, because the way the process works in Tanzania can differ. We found no TCRA or operator page that describes the exact SIM replacement process in Tanzania, so we do not describe it. If you want to know how your operator protects against it, ask your operator what protections it offers.

Is it common in Tanzania?

Tanzanian press has reported that it is a growing worry. The Citizen reported on 1 October 2025 a notable rise in SIM swap fraud in Tanzania and Uganda, where criminals take over phone numbers to drain mobile money wallets. The same article cited the INTERPOL Africa Cyberthreat Assessment Report 2025. That report suggests tighter checks on SIM registration and regular audits of SIM card vendors.

The article also quoted a bank cyber-security expert who said the problem is partly human: criminals may only need someone at a shop who is willing to bend the rules. We say this in one sentence only, and we are not saying that your local agent is dishonest. Most agents are honest people. The point is that you should guard your personal details.

Signs that something is wrong

Many things can cause a lost signal: a storm, a mast under repair, a damaged SIM, a phone fault or a flat battery. Do not panic at the first sign. But treat these together as a warning:

  • Your phone suddenly has no network, and other phones nearby have signal.
  • You stop getting calls and texts.
  • You get messages saying a SIM was changed or a number was moved, which you did not ask for.
  • You are signed out of apps, or get password reset emails you did not ask for.
  • Friends say they got strange messages or calls from your number.
  • Your mobile money or bank shows activity you do not know.

If it is only one sign, restart the phone, check the SIM, and move to a place with better signal. If the problem stays, or you see more than one sign, move to the next section.

What to do in the first hour

Work through these steps in order. Use someone else's phone or a trusted computer where you can.

  1. Contact your operator at once. TCRA's campaign page says you can reach your service provider by dialling 100. Use another phone. Say that you think your SIM may have been swapped. Ask what they can do to protect your number and wallet.
  2. Check the lines registered to your ID. TCRA says to dial *106# and follow the steps, to see the numbers registered under your national ID and remove any you do not recognise. Follow what your screen shows, because the exact steps can change.
  3. Contact your mobile money provider and your bank. Ask them to watch the account or block it for now. Use the customer-care numbers on their official pages, not numbers from a message.
  4. Change your passwords from a safe device. Start with email, because it is often the key to everything else. Then change mobile money and banking apps (follow each provider's own advice), Facebook, WhatsApp and Google or Apple accounts.
  5. Log out other devices. Most accounts have a list of signed-in devices. Remove the ones you do not know.
  6. Report it. TCRA's FAQ advises reporting fraud at a police station to get an RB number. You can also report a scam number by SMS to 15040 and follow the automated reply, as TCRA's pages describe.
  7. Write down what happened. Note the time your signal went, any strange messages and any money that moved. This helps your operator and the police.

If you are locked out of WhatsApp, see our post on protecting your WhatsApp account.

If your operator is slow, TCRA says to complain to your provider first in writing, and then to TCRA if there is no answer within 30 days or the reply is not satisfactory. TCRA's toll-free line is 0800008272. In a case where money is leaving your wallet, do not wait for that timeline. Call your operator and provider first.

Make it harder for a thief next time

You cannot control everything, but you can make yourself a harder target. The FTC and Google give these ideas:

  • Do not answer unexpected requests for personal information. If a call or text claims to be from your operator or bank, hang up and call the number you already know is genuine.
  • Share less in public. Your full name, address, phone number and birth date can help a thief answer security questions.
  • Ask your operator about extra protection. The FTC advises setting a PIN or password on your phone account. We do not know whether Tanzanian operators offer this, so ask.
  • Do not rely on SMS codes alone. The FTC says text message verification may not stop a SIM swap and suggests an authentication app or a security key. Google says text and call codes can be vulnerable to phone number-based hacks, while passkeys and security keys protect against phishing. It also says that Google prompts can help protect against SIM swap.
  • Keep backup codes. Google says that if you lose your phone, a backup code lets you complete the second step. Keep them in a safe place and never give them to anyone.
  • Keep your ID details private. Do not photograph or send your national ID to people you do not know.

Settings and menus change often. The advice above is as checked in November 2026. Start with email, because if a thief gets your email, they can reset almost everything else. For good passwords, see our post on strong passwords and password managers.

Common questions

Does losing network always mean a SIM swap?

No. Most signal losses are ordinary. A SIM swap is more likely when the signal loss comes with strange alerts, locked accounts or unknown activity.

Can someone swap my SIM without my phone?

Yes. That is the point of the fraud. The scammer does not need your phone. They need your operator to move your number to another SIM. This is why guarding your personal details matters.

Should I tell my family?

Yes. Scammers may write to your contacts. Tell people to call you on another number before they send money.

Will I get my money back?

We cannot promise that. Report fast, because speed gives your operator and the police the best chance to act.

What to do next

  1. Save your operator's customer-care contact from its official page.
  2. Dial *106# today and check that every number listed under your ID is yours.
  3. Make a plan for your email account: a long, unique password and a second step that is not only SMS.
  4. Write down two numbers of people who can lend you a phone in an emergency.

A few minutes of preparation now can make a bad hour much easier later.
