Buying a Used Phone in Tanzania: What to Check Before You Pay

A calm, step-by-step check you can do at the counter before paying for a second-hand phone: IMEI, account locks, battery, screen, buttons and charging port.

A used phone can save you a lot of money. It can also leave you with a phone that locks, dies by noon or never works properly. The seller usually knows more about the phone than you do, so you need a plan before you stand at the counter.

This post gives you a fixed order of checks. Do them every time, calmly, and be ready to walk away politely if something is wrong.

The short answer

Before you pay for a second-hand phone, check that it works, belongs to the seller and is free of any other person's account. Do not rush. A seller who is honest will not mind you taking ten minutes.

  • Get the IMEI (the phone's 15-digit ID number) and check it on the TCRA page. Make sure the details match the phone in your hand.
  • Make sure the previous owner's account is gone, so the phone does not lock on you later.
  • Test the screen, buttons, cameras, speaker, microphone and charging port.
  • Watch how the battery behaves while you test.
  • Ask for the seller's details and a receipt, and keep the box and charger if there are any.

Step 1: Get the IMEI and check it on the TCRA page

Every phone has an IMEI, a 15-digit number that identifies that one device. To see it, open the phone's dial pad and type *#06#. The number appears on the screen.

On an iPhone you can also find it in Settings, then General, then About. You may need to scroll down. Apple says iPhone 14 models and later have no IMEI engraving on the body, so older engraved numbers only apply to older models (Apple Support, as checked in November 2026).

The Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority (TCRA) has a public page for this. As checked in November 2026, the page says to dial *#06#, type the 15-digit number into the box and press Verify IMEI. The page then shows the model, the maker and the frequency bands. You compare these with the phone you are holding. The page also warns people to avoid fake devices.

Here is what that means for you:

  • If the model, maker or bands shown do not match the phone in your hand, do not buy it.
  • If the number on the screen is different from the number printed on the box or the tray, ask why. If the answer is not clear, do not buy it.

We could not find any statement on the TCRA page that it shows whether a phone is stolen or blocked. Do not treat a result as proof that a phone is clean. It is one check among several. A match tells you the phone is the model it claims to be. It does not tell you who owns it.

You can do the check on your own phone while standing at the counter. It takes about one minute and needs a little data.

Step 2: Make sure the phone is not locked to someone else

Modern phones are tied to the account of the person who set them up. If the seller does not remove that account, the phone can become useless in your hands. This is the single most important check, and many buyers skip it.

If it is an iPhone

Apple's advice for buyers is clear. Turn the phone on and unlock it. If you see a passcode lock screen or a home screen, the seller has not erased it yet. Ask them to erase it first.

Then start the setup. If the phone asks for the previous owner's Apple Account details, it is still linked to that person. Stop there. If you see an "iPhone Locked to Owner" screen, Apple says not to take ownership of it. The phone is ready for you only when the Hello screen appears (Apple Support, as checked in November 2026).

If the seller is not there, they would have to remove the phone from their account on the internet. That is a good reason to do this check in front of the seller.

If it is an Android phone

Google says that after a factory reset, an Android phone with this protection needs the Google Account that was added to it. If you cannot give that during setup, Google warns that you will not be able to use the device at all (Google Pixel Help, as checked in November 2026).

So the seller must remove their Google Account from the phone before you pay. Ask them to do it in front of you. The exact menu name may differ by brand and Android version, but it is usually found under Settings, in the accounts or passwords section.

After the seller has removed the account and reset the phone, turn it on and go through setup. If the phone asks you to sign in with an account you do not own, do not pay.

A short rule you can remember: the phone should reach a clean welcome screen and let you set it up as your own.

Step 3: Test the battery, screen, buttons, cameras and charging port

Once you know the phone is not locked, check that it works. Ask for a charger and a few minutes. Work through this list in order.

  1. Screen. Look at it in good light. Check for cracks, dark patches, coloured lines and dead spots. Drag your finger slowly across the whole screen, edge to edge, and see if it responds everywhere. A white screen helps show marks and a dark screen shows bright pixels.
  2. Buttons. Press volume up, volume down and the power button. Each should click and work every time.
  3. Cameras. Open the camera and test the back and front cameras. Take a photo and a short video. Zoom in on the photo and check that it is sharp and has no spots.
  4. Speaker and microphone. Play a song or video. Then record a voice note and play it back. Make a short phone call if you can.
  5. Charging port. Plug the cable in. It should sit firmly and the phone should show that it is charging. If you must wiggle the cable to make it charge, that is a warning sign.
  6. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and mobile network. Put in your own SIM card. Check that you get a signal and can open a web page.
  7. Fingerprint or face unlock. Set one up and test it. A broken sensor is a common fault.
  8. Water damage. You cannot see it easily, so be careful. If the phone has been opened or the screen has been replaced, ask why.

How to judge the battery

The battery decides how useful the phone is every day. We found no official way to read battery health across all Android brands, because it differs by maker. So test behaviour instead:

  • Note the charge level when you start. Use the phone for 10 minutes with the screen on, playing a video. If the percentage drops fast, the battery is probably worn.
  • Feel the phone while it charges. A little warmth is normal. If it gets hot, be careful.
  • Ask the seller how long the battery lasts on a normal day, and listen for an honest answer.
  • If the battery looks swollen, or the back cover is lifting, do not buy the phone.

On an iPhone, you can look in Settings, then Battery, then Battery Health. Apple says the "maximum capacity" figure compares the battery with when it was new. Apple says iPhone 14 models and earlier are designed to keep 80 percent of original capacity at 500 full charge cycles under ideal conditions. iPhone 15 models are designed for 1,000 cycles. These figures are as checked in November 2026, and real results depend on how the phone was used and charged (Apple Support).

A low figure does not mean the phone is bad, but it does mean you should spend less. If the battery is worn, you may have to pay for a replacement soon. Apple also says a message about a degraded battery is not a safety issue, but you may notice poorer battery life and performance.

What to ask the seller, and what to keep

Some questions cost nothing and tell you a lot.

  • Is this your phone? Why are you selling it?
  • Have you removed your accounts and erased the phone?
  • Has the screen, battery or any other part been replaced?
  • Do you have the box, charger and receipt?

Then protect yourself:

  • Ask for the seller's full name and phone number, and write them down. Do this politely. A seller who refuses may have a reason.
  • Ask for a receipt, even a handwritten one, that names the phone, its IMEI, the price and the date. This is common sense and not legal advice. If you need advice on a dispute, ask a qualified person.
  • Keep the box, if any, and any other papers.
  • Pay in a way that leaves a record, such as mobile money, so there is proof of the payment. Do not send money before you have the phone in your hand.
  • Meet in a busy, public place during the day. Do not go alone to a place you do not know.

A hypothetical example: say you meet a seller who will not erase the phone and tells you to "do it at home." That is a reason to walk away, because the account is still on the phone and you cannot confirm that it will be free to use. You lose nothing by leaving. You can lose a lot by staying.

If the price seems too good, take extra care. A low price is sometimes the only clue that something is wrong.

Common questions

Does the TCRA IMEI page tell me if a phone is stolen?

We could not find any such statement on the page. It lists the model, maker and frequency bands for you to compare with your phone, and it warns about fake devices. Use it to confirm the phone matches what the seller says. Do not use it as proof that the phone is clean.

What if the seller says the account lock is "not a problem"?

Treat it as a problem. Both Apple and Google say the phone needs the previous owner's account details if it was not removed. If the seller is not there to remove it, you cannot fix it yourself.

Is a cheap phone with a worn battery still a good buy?

It can be, if the price is lower to match and you are ready to replace the battery. Ask a trusted repair person what a battery replacement costs for that model before you decide. We give no prices here because they change.

Should I buy from a shop or from a person?

Either can be fine. A shop is easier to find again if there is a problem, and may give a short written warranty. A person may offer a lower price. Run the same checks in both cases.

How long should the checks take?

About ten to fifteen minutes. If the seller hurries you, slow down.

What to do next

  1. Write the checks from this post in your notes app so you can follow them in the same order every time.
  2. Before you meet a seller, charge your own phone and bring your own SIM card and a charger.
  3. Do the IMEI check, the account check and the hands-on test. If any step fails, say thank you and leave.
  4. After you buy, set a new screen lock, add your own account and make a first backup.

Buying second-hand takes patience, and your patience is the best protection you have. Take your time and you will find a phone that serves you well.
