How Esports Works, and Where Africa and Tanzania Fit In

What esports is, how federations and organisers run tournaments, what is known about Africa and Tanzania, and why school and health come first.

If you love games, you may have asked yourself a quiet question: can this lead anywhere? Many young Tanzanians ask it, and many parents ask it too. The honest answer has hope in it, and it also has limits.

This post explains how esports is organised, who runs it, and what we could confirm about Africa and Tanzania. Where we could not find facts, we say so. Dated facts below were checked in November 2026.

The short answer

Esports means organised, competitive video games, played by teams or single players in set events with rules and referees. It works like other sports in one way: there are organisers, federations, qualifiers and champions. It is also very hard to reach the top, and no result can be promised.

  • Esports has governing bodies, such as the International Esports Federation (IESF).
  • Even players on big club programmes must qualify on their own merit for each event, according to the Esports World Cup Foundation.
  • The eSports Federation of Tanzania was announced as a new IESF member on 17 March 2025.
  • We found very little public detail about Tanzanian players, teams or events, and we do not invent any.
  • School, health and sleep come first. Many popular esports games carry teen or adult ratings.

What is esports, and what is it not?

Esports is competition. Players or teams meet in a game, under the same rules, and a winner is decided. Some events are played in an arena and watched by fans. Many are played online.

It is not the same as playing for fun at home. Casual play is a good hobby, and it does not need to become a career. Esports is also not a quick way to earn money. We do not give income figures, because we cannot verify them, and we will not suggest that anyone can "go pro" by playing more hours.

This post does not cover betting, and we do not link to betting sites.

How do players and teams reach a tournament?

There is no single path, because each game and each organiser sets its own rules. What the sources we opened do show is this:

  1. Qualifiers come first. The Esports World Cup Foundation says that all clubs and athletes must seek to qualify on their own merits for each title. That is true even for clubs in its support programme. A club programme does not guarantee a place.
  2. National federations can send teams. The IESF says it works to give nations chances to compete through championships and programmes. Its first world championship was in 2009 with eight nations.
  3. Rules are set by each organiser. Age limits, entry rules and games change from one event to another. Read the official rules of the event you care about, and be careful with any page that asks for money to "guarantee" entry.

Say you are a student who plays a team game every evening. A sensible plan is to learn which organisers run events for that game, read their rules, and join only free or low-cost open events that you can verify. This is an example, not a promise of any result.

Please be careful with offers that look too good. Do not pay a stranger for a place in a tournament, and do not share your passwords with a "coach" or a "team" you have not checked.

Who runs esports? Federations and organisers

Two examples show the kinds of groups involved.

The International Esports Federation (IESF). Its own page says it is the global governing body for esports. It was founded in 2008 in Seoul, South Korea, and now lists its headquarters in Busan. It says it has more than 150 member nations, up from 50 in 2013 and 100 in 2017. It states that it aims to promote esports as a legitimate sport, set standards, and protect fair play.

The Esports World Cup Foundation. It runs the Esports World Cup and has a Club Support Program. Its news page says the programme helps multi-game esports clubs build lasting infrastructure, and that accepted clubs can receive large funding. The page also repeats that club members must still qualify on merit.

Besides these, game makers and private companies run their own leagues. We did not open pages for them, so we do not describe them.

What does the Africa scene look like?

Here our sources are narrower, and we want to be clear about what they cover.

The 2025 Africa Games Industry Report was published by KPMG with Maliyo Games. A report in ThisDay says Africa earned less than 1% of the 187 billion US dollars that the world games industry made in 2024. It also says the report counted 66 active studios and named barriers such as weak government support, limited funding and technical infrastructure, poor power supply and limited access to training.

Two points need care. First, this is about the games industry, meaning the businesses that make games. It is not a measure of esports. Second, we did not open the report itself, so we use only the figures from the news article, and we do not quote any esports or mobile share figures.

What does that mean for a young person? It suggests that the industry is young, and that power, internet and training are real obstacles. It also suggests there is room to grow. We cannot say more than that.

Tanzania: what is known and what is not

One fact is sourced. The news site Esports Advocate reported on 17 March 2025 that the IESF added five new member federations, and that the eSports Federation of Tanzania was one of them. That brought the total to 151 members. The report said the new members would take part in the IESF 2025 Season.

That is where our verified information ends. We found the IESF member page for Tanzania, but it has no description. We did not find a source about a Tanzanian national team, a national tournament, prize money or named players, so we say nothing about them.

If you want to know more, ask the federation or the IESF directly, and check any claim you read on social media against an official page. If you know of an event in your town, look at who runs it and what the rules say before you join.

Which games are involved, and are they right for you?

Many popular esports titles have teen or adult ratings. Before you or your child joins an event, check the game's age label on its official page. Our guide to game age ratings and parental controls explains how the labels work. We do not name particular games here, because we have not checked their ratings.

Also watch spending. Some games sell random items, and our guide to loot boxes and in-game purchases shows how to set limits on Google Play.

Common questions

Can I make a living from esports in Tanzania?

We cannot promise that. We found no verified figures for Tanzania, and reaching the top of any competitive field is difficult. Treat esports as something to enjoy and learn from, while you keep studying.

Is there a national esports federation in Tanzania?

The IESF added the eSports Federation of Tanzania as a member, as reported on 17 March 2025. We could not find a description of its work, so ask it directly for details.

Do I need an expensive computer?

That depends on the game. We did not check hardware needs, so look at the game's official page for its requirements. If you play on a basic phone, our guide to free games for low-end Android phones shows how to pick safe ones.

Does joining a club guarantee a place at a big event?

No. The Esports World Cup Foundation says all clubs and athletes must qualify on their own merits for each title.

What to do next

  1. Choose one game and read the official rules of its events.
  2. Check the game's age rating on its official page.
  3. Look up the IESF site and read its pages for members.
  4. Keep up your school work, sleep and meals. A good routine helps you in games and in life.

Wanting to be good at something is a fine goal. Take it one step at a time, and keep your feet on solid ground.
