Starting a Water Vending Business in Tanzania: Costs, Permits, Risks

What it takes to sell water in a Tanzanian city: business models, permits to ask about, costs to plan for, and the risks that can sink a small vendor.

Many people in Tanzanian cities buy water because the taps at home run dry or the supply is unreliable. That demand is real, and some people build small businesses around it. But a water business is not a quick way to get rich. It needs a safe water source, the right permits, equipment, and steady work every day.

This post explains the main models, what to check before you spend money, how to work out your own numbers, and what can go wrong. We have not invented any profit figures. Prices and rules differ by city and change often, so use this as a checklist and confirm details locally.

The short answer

  • There are four common models: a kiosk selling by the bucket, a delivery business (handcart, bodaboda or truck), a bowser (water tanker), and packaged drinking water.
  • Each model is regulated differently. The water authority in your city, EWURA and TBS are the main names to know.
  • Packaged water is the most heavily regulated. Selling water that is not tested and certified can harm customers and get you shut down.
  • Your profit depends on the price you pay for water, the price you can charge, and how many days the water actually flows. You must calculate it for your own location.
  • Start small, keep records, and do not borrow heavily before you have tested demand.

The main ways to sell water

1. Water kiosk or standpipe point

You connect to the public supply (or have your own approved source) and sell water by the bucket or jerrycan. Setup is cheap, but you depend on the public supply running. When the mains are dry, so is your business.

2. Delivery by handcart, motorbike or small truck

You collect water from a source and deliver to homes, shops or building sites. This works in areas where people do not want to queue. Your main costs are the vehicle, fuel or labour, and containers. Tanks and drums must be clean and used only for drinking water.

3. Water bowser (tanker)

A bowser is a larger tanker, usually for delivering to homes, institutions or construction. It costs far more to start and is the most formally regulated of the delivery models. See the permits section below.

4. Packaged drinking water

This means sachets, bottles or 20-litre refills sold to shops and homes. It needs treatment, hygiene controls and product certification, so it is the hardest model for a beginner. We do not recommend it as a first business unless you have the money, technical help and time to meet the standards.

Permits and rules to ask about

We could not find one single "water vendor licence" that covers every case. The rules depend on what you sell and how. Here is what we could confirm, and where to check the rest.

Water supply services and EWURA. Under the Water Supply and Sanitation Services (Licensing and Quality of Service) Rules, 2020, a person or water authority may not provide water supply services without a licence from EWURA. The rules are aimed at water providers such as utilities. Whether a small kiosk or an individual delivery vendor needs its own EWURA licence depends on how you operate, so ask your local water authority first, and ask EWURA directly if you are unsure.

Bowsers. The Water Supply and Sanitation (Water Bowser Services) Regulations, 2018 cover bowser registration through the local Water Authority. Ask your Water Authority how often registration must be renewed, what the fee is, and what an operator must display, such as the registration certificate and the maximum price per cubic metre. In Dar es Salaam, tankers are registered separately with DAWASCO. The regulations and the authority's own notices have the current requirements and fees.

Packaged drinking water. The Tanzania Bureau of Standards (TBS) lists a certification scheme for packaged and bottled drinking water under the standard TZS 574:2019 (also EAS 153:2018). Selling bottled or sachet water without meeting the standard is risky for customers and for you. Ask TBS for the current steps and fees.

Business basics. You will also need the usual things for any small business: a TIN from the Tanzania Revenue Authority (TRA), a local business licence from your municipal council, and a place to operate that your landlord or local government allows. The exact list varies by council, so confirm with them.

Water quality matters more than price

Customers cannot see germs. If your water makes someone sick, you can lose your customers and face legal trouble. Some basic habits:

  • Know where your water comes from, and use only an approved, safe source.
  • Clean tanks, drums and jerrycans regularly, and use containers made for drinking water.
  • Do not reuse containers that held fuel, chemicals or paint.
  • Keep taps, hoses and buckets off the ground.
  • If you sell for drinking, ask the water authority or a laboratory about testing.

Work out your own numbers

We will not give you a "typical profit" because we have no reliable data for it. Instead, here is a simple method with a made-up example so you can see the arithmetic. These numbers are not real prices.

Say you buy water at TSh 5 per litre and sell it at TSh 10 per litre (hypothetical). You sell 500 litres a day. Your daily gross margin is 500 x (10 - 5) = TSh 2,500. If you also pay TSh 1,000 a day for labour, fuel or containers, your daily profit is TSh 1,500. Over 25 working days, that is TSh 37,500.

Now change one thing. If the mains are dry for 10 of those 25 days and you cannot buy water, you only earn on 15 days, so about TSh 22,500. Small changes in price, volume or supply change the result a lot.

Before you start, write down:

  1. What you pay for water per litre or per cubic metre, and your delivery cost.
  2. What nearby vendors charge. Check the price cap if one applies to you.
  3. How many litres you can realistically sell on a normal day and on a slow day.
  4. Fixed costs: rent, licence fees, tank, containers, pump, wages.
  5. How many days a month the water supply fails.

If the sum only works on your best-case numbers, it is a risky business.

Main risks

  • Supply interruptions. Water cuts stop your sales and can leave you with costs.
  • Price caps and enforcement. Authorities can set maximum prices and cancel registrations for breaking rules.
  • Competition. Water is easy to sell, so neighbours may copy you.
  • Customers who pay late. Selling on credit ties up your cash.
  • Quality complaints. One sick customer can damage your reputation.
  • Seasonal change. Demand and your source can change between rainy and dry seasons.

Common questions

How much money do I need?

It depends on the model. A small kiosk or handcart delivery costs far less than a bowser or a packaged-water plant. Get written quotes for tanks, pumps, containers and vehicles in your town, and add permits and a cash reserve.

Do I need a licence to sell water?

Probably yes, in some form, but which one depends on your model and your council. Start with the water authority in your city, then your municipal council and TRA.

Can I sell water from a borehole or well?

Drilling and using groundwater usually requires approval from the basin water board. We did not verify the current process, so ask your regional basin water office before you invest.

Can I make a living from this?

Some people do, but we cannot promise it. Your income depends on supply, location, price and competition. Test with a small setup before putting in your savings.

What to do next

  1. Visit your local water authority (for example DAWASA or DAWASCO in Dar es Salaam) and ask which registration your model needs.
  2. Walk the area where you want to sell. Count customers, note prices and see how often water runs.
  3. Fill in the numbers list above with real quotes and prices.
  4. If you want to sell packaged water, contact TBS before buying any equipment.

For other low-cost ideas to compare, see our post on how to start a poultry business in Tanzania.

Sources and further reading

  • Product Certification Services, Tanzania Bureau of Standards (TBS)
