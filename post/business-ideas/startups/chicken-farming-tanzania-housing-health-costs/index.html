Starting a Chicken Farm in Tanzania: Housing, Health, Costs and Risks

A plain guide to starting a small chicken farm in Tanzania: broilers or layers, housing, feed, vaccination basics, a worked cost example, and the risks.

Chickens are one of the most common small livestock businesses in Tanzania. Eggs and chicken meat sell every day in towns and villages, and you can start with a small flock. But chicken farming is not easy money. Feed is expensive, disease can kill a flock in days, and prices go up and down.

This guide covers the basics: the main types of poultry farming, housing, feed, health and vaccination, and how to check whether a plan makes financial sense. It is general advice. Before you buy birds, talk to a livestock officer or a vet in your district.

The short answer

  • Decide first: broilers (meat birds), layers (egg birds) or improved indigenous chickens. Each has a different cost, timeline and market.
  • Feed is usually the biggest running cost, so work out your feed bill before anything else.
  • Vaccinating on time and keeping the house clean are the cheapest ways to protect your money.
  • Start small. Learn on a few hundred birds before you borrow money for a large house.
  • We do not give profit figures. Your result depends on your costs, your market and your skills.

Choose your type of chicken business

Broilers

Broilers are chicks bred to grow fast for meat. A batch is raised together and sold when they reach market weight, so your money comes back in a few weeks. The risk is that you must sell on time. If you cannot find buyers, feed costs keep rising each day.

Layers

Layers are hens kept for eggs. You buy day-old chicks or young hens, feed and care for them for months before eggs start, then sell eggs daily. The wait before income is longer, and you need steady cash for feed. Egg sellers also need a clean way to store and move eggs.

Improved indigenous or dual-purpose chickens

These are local-type chickens, improved for better growth or egg output. They are usually hardier and sell at a higher price per bird in some markets, but they grow more slowly. Ask your livestock office which breeds are available and healthy in your area.

Housing basics

The general rules of good housing are simple. They come from standard small-scale poultry guidance, such as the FAO manual, and from common sense.

  • Good airflow. Poor ventilation lets ammonia from droppings build up and causes breathing problems.
  • Do not overcrowd. Crowded birds spread disease faster. The right number of birds per square metre depends on the type of bird and the house. Ask your livestock officer or hatchery for the recommended space.
  • Dry floor. Use dry litter such as wood shavings or rice husks, and change it when it gets wet.
  • Keep it clean. Keep housing away from dung heaps and latrines, and limit contact with wild birds.
  • Separate ages. Keep chicks and older birds in different pens where you can.
  • Protection. The house must keep out rain, predators and thieves.

Basic equipment includes feeders, drinkers, a heat source for chicks (if the weather is cold), and a clean place to store feed.

Feed and water

Chickens need balanced feed, and the right feed changes as the bird grows. Chick starter, grower and layer feeds are different, so ask the seller which one fits your birds. Poor feed causes slow growth and low egg output. Mouldy feed can make birds sick, so store it dry and off the ground.

Birds also need clean water all day. Check drinkers daily, because water stops quickly in hot weather.

You can buy ready-made feed or mix your own. Mixing can cost less, but it requires correct recipes and good ingredients. Do not guess. Ask an extension officer or a nutrition expert.

Health and vaccination

Viral poultry diseases cannot be treated once they spread, so prevention matters. The FAO small-scale poultry manual says vaccination plus good hygiene is the most effective approach to Newcastle disease, and lists Newcastle disease, fowl pox and fowl cholera as priority vaccines for family flocks.

In Tanzania, a 2023 study of 200 commercial layer farms in Dar es Salaam, Morogoro and Unguja found that most farms vaccinated against Newcastle disease, infectious bursal disease (Gumboro) and fowl pox. But 47.5% had not completed the schedule, and 93% gave at least one vaccine outside the recommended time. Late or missed vaccines are a real problem.

We could not find one official national schedule to publish here, so we will not give exact dates. Ask your vet, hatchery or livestock officer for the schedule that fits your breed and area. Also ask how to store vaccines. Live vaccines are fragile and need a cold chain, and poor storage and transport are common reasons vaccination fails.

Biosecurity: simple habits

Biosecurity means keeping disease out of your farm. Practical steps from the FAO guidance:

  • Isolate new birds for at least 15 days before mixing them with your flock.
  • Separate sick birds quickly, and bury or dispose of dead birds properly.
  • Clean feeders and drinkers often, and change wet litter.
  • Disinfect empty housing between batches.
  • Limit visitors, and keep wild birds and other animals out of the house.
  • Do not share equipment with other farms without cleaning it.

If many birds die suddenly, call a vet or the livestock officer. Do not sell sick birds.

A simple cost example

The numbers below are made up to show how the arithmetic works. They are not real prices, so replace them with quotes from your area.

Say you raise 100 broilers (hypothetical):

ItemCost (TSh)
100 chicks at 2,000 each200,000
Feed for the batch600,000
Vaccines, medicine, litter, water100,000
Fuel, labour, other costs100,000
Total cost1,000,000

If 95 birds survive and you sell each for TSh 12,000, your income is 95 x 12,000 = TSh 1,140,000. Profit for the batch is 1,140,000 - 1,000,000 = TSh 140,000, before the cost of the house and equipment.

Now suppose 10 birds die, so 90 survive, and the price drops to TSh 10,000. Income is 900,000, and you lose TSh 100,000. A small change in deaths or price changes everything. Work out your own break-even price before you buy chicks.

Steps to start

  1. Visit the district livestock office and ask about permits, local rules, and advice for your area.
  2. Choose your type of chicken and market. Talk to buyers before you buy birds.
  3. Build or prepare a clean, dry, ventilated house.
  4. Buy healthy chicks from a trusted hatchery, and get vaccination advice there.
  5. Buy feed, drinkers and feeders, and arrange a clean water supply.
  6. Keep daily records: deaths, feed used, eggs or weights, and money in and out.

Common questions

How much money do I need?

It depends on the number of birds, whether you build a new house, and your feed costs. Get written quotes for the house, chicks, feed and vaccines. Keep a cash reserve for at least one batch of feed.

Which is better, layers or broilers?

Neither is always better. Broilers return money faster but need a quick sale. Layers give regular income but need longer waiting and steady feed money.

Can I farm chickens in town?

Many councils have rules about animals and smell. Ask your local government office before you build.

What is the biggest risk?

Disease and feed costs. A flock that is not vaccinated or kept clean can be lost quickly, and feed price rises can remove your margin.

What to do next

  1. Visit a livestock officer or vet and ask for the vaccination schedule for your birds.
  2. Visit two or three nearby farms and ask what costs and problems they have.
