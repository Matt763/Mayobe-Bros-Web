How to Write a One-Page Business Plan for a Small Business

Answer seven plain questions on one page, work out your costs and break-even with a made-up example, and test your idea with real customers before you spend.

You have an idea, a little money and a lot of hope. That is a real start. A business plan sounds like a big document for big companies, but it does not have to be. A plan is simply your thinking written down so you can check it before you spend your savings. This post gives you one page of seven questions, plus a worked example with made-up numbers so you can see how the sums work.

The short answer

A one-page plan is seven honest answers: who will buy, what you sell, what you charge, what it costs to start, what it costs each month, how many sales cover those costs, and how you will test the idea first.

  • Write it for yourself first. Showing it to a lender, family member or partner comes second.
  • Use real prices from your own market, not guesses from the internet.
  • Work out your break-even: the number of sales you need before you earn anything.
  • A plan can say "do not start yet". That is a good result, not a failure.
  • The structure here is a simple tool we made. It is not an official form.

Why a plan helps even if no one asks for it

Money spent on the wrong stock or the wrong place is hard to get back. Writing the plan makes you slow down and look at the numbers while changing your mind is still free.

A plan also helps you explain your idea. A bank, a family member or a business partner may ask what you will sell, to whom and what it will cost. If you can answer in a page, they can see you have thought it through. We have not seen any single rule that makes a plan a must for every small business, so ask each lender what they want to see.

The International Labour Organization (ILO) runs a training programme called Start and Improve Your Business. Its "Start Your Business" package, for people with a concrete idea, helps them assess whether they are ready, prepare a business plan and check that the business can work. The programme treats a plan as part of deciding, not as paperwork.

The one-page plan: seven questions

Take one sheet of paper or one page in a notebook. Write the question, then your answer in two or three lines.

  1. Who is my customer? Be specific. "Students at the school gate between 12 and 2 pm" is better than "everyone".
  2. What exactly do I sell? List three to five products or services. Say why a customer would buy from you and not from the next seller.
  3. What will I charge? Write the price of each item. Check what others near you charge.
  4. What does it cost to start? List everything you must buy once: equipment, first stock, a table or shelf, registration costs.
  5. What does it cost every month? Rent, transport, airtime, electricity, packaging. Add a small wage for yourself.
  6. How many sales do I need? This is the break-even number. The next sections show how.
  7. How will I test it? Decide what small trial you will do before you spend your full budget.

If you need help picking a name, colours or a logo, our post on branding basics is a good next read. For how to find your first customers, see the one-page marketing plan.

Start-up costs and monthly costs: a worked example

Imagine a hypothetical snack stand near a school. All numbers are made up for learning. Your own prices will be different.

Start-up costs (paid once):

ItemTSh
Stove and cooking pot150,000
Table and umbrella120,000
First stock of ingredients100,000
Cups, bags and napkins30,000
Total400,000

Monthly costs:

ItemTSh
Space or a small fee for the spot60,000
Transport40,000
Airtime and charcoal or gas10,000
Total without your own wage110,000
Your own wage (made-up)150,000
Total with your wage260,000

Many new owners leave out their own wage. Then the business looks better than it is. Putting it in tells you whether the stand can really pay you.

Break-even: how many sales do you need?

Now look at one item. Say you sell a snack pack for TSh 1,000, and the ingredients for that pack cost TSh 600. What is left from each pack is TSh 1,000 minus TSh 600, which is TSh 400. This is what pays your monthly costs.

  • Without your wage: TSh 110,000 divided by TSh 400 is 275 packs a month.
  • With your wage: TSh 260,000 divided by TSh 400 is 650 packs a month.

Say you trade 26 days a month. Then 650 packs is 25 packs a day. Without a wage, 275 packs is about 11 packs a day.

You also paid TSh 400,000 to start. To get that back, you need another TSh 400,000 divided by TSh 400, which is 1,000 extra packs, after you have covered the monthly costs.

Now ask yourself honestly: can I sell 25 packs on an ordinary day, not on the best day? If the answer is "I am not sure", that is exactly what the next step is for. These numbers are an example only. They do not say a snack stand will earn money.

Test the plan with real customers before you spend

Before you buy the full set of equipment, find out if people will really pay your price.

  • Ask ten possible customers what they would pay. Listen to the reasons, not only the number.
  • Sell for a few days with borrowed or minimal equipment, and write down every sale.
  • Count how many people walked past, how many stopped and how many bought.
  • Check your supplier's price again. Prices change, and your break-even changes with them.
  • Change one thing at a time, such as the price, the spot or the product.

If the test shows far fewer sales than your break-even, you have saved your savings. You can change the idea, the place or the price, and test again. Our post on knowing your customers has simple ways to ask them.

Where plans are mentioned in official lists

Two official pages mention plans, though not as a general rule. TRA's VAT page says an "intending trader" can show evidence of intent to start a business, such as contracts, tender awards, building plans, business plans or bank financing. That matters for early VAT registration, which most small traders do not need.

BRELA's licence page says a licence gives a business recognition by banks, including when opening an account, and lists access to loans among the benefits. It does not say a plan is needed for a small shop. Ask the council or lender about their own requirements.

Common questions

Does a business plan have to be long?

No. For a small business, one clear page that you understand beats twenty pages you copied. If a lender asks for more detail, you can build on your one page.

Should I pay someone to write my plan?

Not at the start. You know your market and customers best. If you pay for help later, ask for the person's work to match your own numbers and keep a receipt.

What if my numbers say the idea will not work?

That is useful. You learned it for free. Change the price, lower the costs, choose a better spot, or pick another idea. Our list of low-cost online business ideas may give you a new one.

Is there an official template?

We did not find an official one-page template from a Tanzanian government body. The seven questions here are our own simple tool. If a lender gives you a form, use theirs.

What to do next

  1. Take a sheet of paper and write the seven questions with your own answers.
  2. Visit two or three sellers near your planned spot and write down their real prices.
  3. Calculate your break-even as in the example, with and without your wage.
  4. Run a small test for a week before you spend the full amount.

A page of honest numbers is a strong start. Every owner starts with an idea and a question, and you have both.
