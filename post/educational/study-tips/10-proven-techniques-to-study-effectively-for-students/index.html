10 Study Techniques That Help You Remember More

Ten study methods you can start this week, from active recall to spaced review, with simple steps for school, college or university students in Tanzania.

Many students spend hours reading the same pages again and again and still forget most of it by exam day. The problem is often the method, not the effort. This post gives you ten practical techniques for secondary school, college or university. Each one has simple steps you can try today with only a pen, paper and your notes.

A note on evidence. A well-known review by psychologists (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013) looked at many popular study techniques. It rated practice testing and spreading study over time as high utility, and rereading and underlining as low utility. We link to it at the end. For the other techniques below, we describe how they work and why many students find them useful, but we do not claim they are proven by a specific study.

The short answer

  • Test yourself instead of only rereading. Close the book and write what you remember.
  • Spread revision over several days. Short, repeated sessions beat one long night before the exam.
  • Explain topics in your own words, out loud, as if teaching a friend.
  • Plan your week, protect your sleep and keep your phone away while you study.
  • Review your mistakes after each test or assignment.

Technique 1: Active recall

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory instead of just looking at it again. This is the method the Dunlosky review calls practice testing, and it was rated high utility.

How to do it:

  1. Read a section once.
  2. Close the book.
  3. Write down everything you remember, or answer questions about it.
  4. Open the book and check. Mark what you missed.
  5. Come back to the missed parts.

Past NECTA papers, your lecturer's past exams and end-of-chapter questions are good practice material. If you have no questions, turn each heading in your notes into a question.

Technique 2: Spaced repetition

Spaced repetition means reviewing material at growing gaps: today, tomorrow, in three days, in a week. The review calls this distributed practice and rated it high utility too.

A simple plan without any app:

  • Day 1: study the topic.
  • Day 2: quick test on it (ten minutes).
  • Day 4: test again.
  • Day 8: test again, then once more before the exam.

Paper flashcards work well. If you use an app such as Anki, remember that it needs data only when you sync.

Technique 3: The Feynman technique

Named after the physicist Richard Feynman, this method means explaining an idea in plain words. If you get stuck, you have found the part you do not yet understand.

  1. Pick one topic, for example "demand and supply" or "photosynthesis".
  2. Explain it out loud as if you were teaching a younger student.
  3. Mark where you hesitated or used words you cannot explain.
  4. Go back to your notes for those parts and try again.

Technique 4: The Pomodoro method

This is a time method. You study in short blocks with short breaks. The common version is 25 minutes of focus and a 5-minute break, and a longer break after four blocks. You can change the lengths. Some people prefer 40 minutes and 10 minutes.

How to use it:

  • Set a timer on your phone or a cheap kitchen timer.
  • Work on one task only. Write down distracting thoughts and deal with them later.
  • Stand up and drink water during breaks. Do not open social media, because five minutes easily becomes thirty.

Technique 5: A study space with few distractions

You do not need a perfect room. You need a spot where your phone, TV and noise do not keep pulling you away.

  • Study at the campus or community library if home is noisy or has frequent power cuts.
  • Put your phone on silent and out of sight. Even a small delay in checking messages helps.
  • Keep only what you need on the desk.
  • If you share a room, agree on study hours with the others.

Technique 6: Mind maps

A mind map is a diagram with the main topic in the middle and branches for sub-topics. It helps when a chapter has many linked ideas, such as the causes of a historical event or the parts of the digestive system.

Draw it from memory first, then check your notes and fill the gaps. Drawing it from memory also gives you recall practice. Use plain paper and two pen colours if you have them.

Technique 7: Study groups

A group can help you find gaps and keep going on hard topics. It works only if it is organised.

  • Keep it to three to six people.
  • Choose a topic beforehand and come prepared.
  • Take turns explaining and asking questions.
  • Do not turn it into a chat session. End on time.

Never use a group to copy assignments. Answers you did not write do not help you in the exam room.

Technique 8: Clear weekly goals

"Study maths" is too vague. A goal such as "Finish exercises 3 to 7 on quadratic equations by Thursday and mark them" is clearer.

Try this on Sunday evening:

  1. List your exams, tests and assignments with dates.
  2. For each, write two or three specific tasks.
  3. Put tasks on certain days and times, and allow for daladala delays or work shifts.
  4. At the end of the week, tick off what you finished and move the rest.

Technique 9: Sleep, food and movement

Tiredness makes studying slow. Most people remember better when they sleep enough, and staying up all night before an exam often backfires. We cannot give medical advice, and the right amount of sleep varies by person and age. As a rule, try to keep regular sleep times and avoid heavy study late at night right before an exam.

Eat regular meals and drink water. A short walk between study blocks can help you reset. If you have long-lasting sleep problems or constant exhaustion, speak to a doctor or nurse.

Technique 10: Review your mistakes

After every test, quiz or assignment, spend 15 to 30 minutes on what you got wrong. Many students look at the mark and never look again.

Make a small "error list":

  • What was the question?
  • What did I answer?
  • What is the right answer, and why?
  • What will I do differently next time? (For example: read the question twice, practise that formula, learn that term.)

Review the list before the next test.

A simple weekly plan

Say you have exams in three weeks and five subjects. Here is one way to use these techniques:

TimeTask
Weekday evening, 2 blocks of 25 minutesActive recall on one subject, using past papers
Weekday evening, 1 block of 25 minutesQuick review of last week's topics (spaced repetition)
Saturday morningStudy group on the hardest topic
Sunday eveningPlan the next week and review mistakes

This is only an example. Adjust it for your timetable, work and family duties.

Common questions

Is highlighting bad?

Highlighting is not harmful, but by itself it is a weak way to learn. The Dunlosky review rated underlining as low utility. Use it to mark things to test yourself on later, not as your main method.

How long should I study each day?

There is no single right number. Short, focused sessions with testing are better than long sessions of rereading. Start with two or three blocks a day and adjust.

What if I only have one night before the exam?

Do active recall on the most important topics, sleep, and go to the exam. Next time, start earlier and spread it out.

Do I need an app?

No. Paper, a pen and a phone timer are enough. Apps can help if you have data and enjoy them, but they are optional.

What to do next

  1. Pick one subject and do a 25-minute active recall session today.
  2. Write down your exam dates and plan spaced reviews for the topics.
  3. Start a small study group with two or three serious classmates.
  4. If money is a worry, read our guide on applying for a HESLB student loan, and our post on overcoming common barriers as a university student.

Sources and further reading

  • Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques, Dunlosky et al. (2013), Association for Psychological Science
