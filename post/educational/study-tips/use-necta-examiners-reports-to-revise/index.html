Use NECTA Examiners Reports to Revise Smarter for Your Exam

NECTA publishes free reports on how candidates answered each question. Learn what is inside them and how to turn one into a simple revision plan.

Most students revise by reading their notes again and again. There is a free resource that shows you what examiners actually saw in real scripts, and many students have never opened it. This post explains what it is and how to use it, one subject at a time.

The short answer

NECTA, the National Examinations Council of Tanzania, publishes Candidates' Item Response Analysis reports, called CIRA reports. For a subject and a year, each report goes through the exam questions and explains what the question asked, how candidates answered, and where they lost marks. You can use one to see the mistakes that cost others marks and to avoid repeating them.

  • The reports are free to read on NECTA's website.
  • They are written for students, teachers and other education stakeholders.
  • Each question is explained one by one, so you can study it in small pieces.
  • Use the report together with your syllabus and your past papers, not instead of them.

What is a CIRA report?

A CIRA report is NECTA's feedback after an exam. We opened the report for Information and Computer Studies in the 2022 Certificate of Secondary Education Examination (CSEE) to see how these reports are built. We read it ourselves, and what follows describes that report. Other subjects and years may be arranged a little differently.

The foreword says the report is meant to give feedback on what shaped performance in the subject. It says the report helps students find their strengths and weaknesses before they sit the exam, and helps teachers find the hard areas. That is the purpose in NECTA's own words.

Inside, you will find:

  • A short description of the exam: how many papers there were and how the sections were set.
  • An analysis of every question, with the topics it tested.
  • What the question demanded, what candidates wrote, and the mistakes they made.
  • Reasons why candidates did well or badly on that question.
  • Extracts from real answer scripts, used to show the points.
  • A conclusion and recommendations, and a table of how candidates did in each topic.

The report also explains how it labels performance. A question is called good, average or weak depending on the share of candidates who scored 30 per cent or more of the marks for that question. The report uses green, yellow and red colours in its charts for good, average and weak. So a red question is one where most candidates struggled to reach even 30 per cent of the marks.

Do not worry about the exact numbers. The useful idea is simple: red questions show where a lot of candidates lost marks, so they deserve your attention.

Where can I find the reports for my subject?

Start at the NECTA website, www.necta.go.tz. The top menu has a Publications section. When we opened that page in November 2026, it showed us no list of CIRA reports, so we cannot tell you exactly which menu leads to them. NECTA may also change its layout. If one route does not work, try again later or look for the Publications and Results menus from the home page.

We can tell you what the report files look like. The Information and Computer Studies 2022 report is a PDF on a NECTA address that has the exam level (csee), the year (2022) and the subject name in its web address. A 2023 report for Literature in English sits under a similar address on NECTA's main site. That shows the reports are organised by exam, year and subject.

Some practical points:

  • Do not assume that every year and every subject has a report. Check what is listed for your subject.
  • Reports are large PDF files. If you use a phone, download the file on Wi-Fi or a cheap bundle and keep it for offline reading. Our guide on saving mobile data when studying online can help.
  • Use only NECTA's own pages. Never download "leaked" papers or "guaranteed answers" from social media pages. That is cheating, it can get you into serious trouble, and it does not teach you anything.

How do I read one question analysis?

Pick one question and read its section slowly. A good way is to use five short steps.

  1. Read the question first, and try to answer it yourself on paper without looking at the report.
  2. Read what the question demanded. This tells you what the examiner wanted, such as "list", "explain" or "compare".
  3. Read what candidates wrote and the mistakes they made. Ask yourself: would I have made the same mistake?
  4. Read the reasons given for good or weak performance. These are often about not reading the question carefully, not knowing a topic, or not giving enough detail.
  5. Compare your own answer. Fix it using what you learned, and write the corrected version in your notebook.

In the Information and Computer Studies report, the first question is a set of multiple choice items. For each item, the report states the topic, the correct answer, and why candidates who chose other options were wrong. Reading that kind of explanation teaches you the idea behind the item, not only the right letter.

That is the real benefit. You learn how questions are built and how marks are lost, and you can use that on questions you have never seen.

How do I turn the mistakes into a revision plan?

Say you are in Form Four and you want to improve your Information and Computer Studies. This is an example only, to show the method.

  1. Download the latest report for your subject that is listed on the NECTA site.
  2. Open the page near the end that shows performance by topic. Write down the topics where candidates did worst.
  3. Match each weak topic to your syllabus and your class notes.
  4. Make a list with the headings Topic, What went wrong for candidates, What I will do.
  5. Spend your revision time on the topics at the top of the list first.

A simple weekly plan could look like this.

DayTaskTime
MondayRead one question analysis and answer it yourself first45 minutes
WednesdayRewrite the mistakes in your own words30 minutes
FridayDo a past paper question on the same topic45 minutes
SundayCheck your answers against the report and your teacher30 minutes

Change the days and times to fit your school week. What matters is that you do a little, often, and that you always check your answer afterwards.

You can also work with a friend. Each of you takes a different question, explains the mistakes to the other, and then swaps. Teaching a point aloud is a strong way to remember it. Our post on study techniques that help you remember more has more ideas you can mix in.

Do students really use these reports?

Not many do. A 2024 study by Geofrey Shahanga and Momole Kasambala, published by the Open University of Tanzania's journals site, looked at examiners' feedback reports and mathematics performance in Tanzanian secondary schools. It found that most secondary school students did not know about the reports. Because they did not use them, the reports had little effect on mathematics performance. The authors recommended that teacher training bodies include the use of these reports in their training.

This is one study on one subject, so we should not stretch it too far. But it points to a simple chance. If you read the report for your subject, you are using something many classmates never open.

Common questions

Are CIRA reports the same as past papers?

No. A past paper gives you the questions. A CIRA report explains how candidates answered them and where marks were lost. Use both. Try the paper, then read the report to learn from it.

Do the reports show the exact answers I should write?

Not always in a model-answer style. They explain what the question demanded and the common mistakes, and some include extracts of candidate answers. Treat them as guidance. Confirm answers with your teacher and your textbook.

Will the next exam have the same questions?

Do not expect that. Exams change from year to year. The value of the report is in learning how questions are set, what examiners look for and which mistakes to avoid.

What if the report for my subject is missing?

Check other years of the same subject, because the lessons often carry over. You can also ask your subject teacher whether the school holds copies, and look again on the NECTA site later.

What to do next

  1. Choose one subject you find hard and look for its latest report on the NECTA website.
  2. Read the foreword and introduction first, so you understand how the report labels weak and good questions.
  3. Work through one weak question using the five steps above, and write your corrected answer.
  4. Share the idea with a classmate, so that you can revise together.

You do not need to read a whole report in one sitting. One careful question a day is enough to start, and you are already ahead of where you were this morning.
