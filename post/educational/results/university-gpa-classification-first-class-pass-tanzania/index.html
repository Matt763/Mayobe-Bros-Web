University GPA in Tanzania: First Class, Upper Second and Pass

See how a university degree GPA is worked out, step by step, with a simple example, and which class each GPA band gives at the University of Dar es Salaam.

Every semester, your marks turn into letters, and your letters turn into a number that follows you for years. Understanding that number takes away a lot of fear. You can see where you stand, and you can plan to improve it. This post uses the rules of the University of Dar es Salaam (UDSM) as a clear example.

The short answer

At UDSM, each letter grade has points. You multiply the points by the course credits, add them up, and divide by the total credits. The result is cut down to one decimal place.

  • Points: A is 5, B+ is 4, B is 3, C is 2, D is 1 and E is 0.
  • GPA is total score divided by total credits, then truncated (cut, not rounded) to one decimal.
  • First Class is 4.4 to 5.0. Upper Second is 3.5 to 4.3. Lower Second is 2.7 to 3.4. Pass is 2.0 to 2.6.
  • These are UDSM rules. Other universities can use different scales, so read your own regulations.

How do grade points work?

The UDSM Undergraduate Prospectus for 2024/2025 (regulation 12) uses a five-point system. Letter grades get points like this:

GradePoints
A5
B+4
B3
C2
D1
E0

Each course also has a weight in credits. A bigger course counts for more. That is why a grade in a 10-credit course changes your GPA more than the same grade in a 3-credit course.

How do I work out a GPA step by step?

Follow these steps.

  1. For each course, multiply the points by the credits. This gives the course score.
  2. Add all the course scores. This is the total score.
  3. Add all the credits. This is the total weight.
  4. Divide the total score by the total credits.
  5. Cut the answer down to one decimal. Do not round up.

Here is a made-up example. The courses and credits are invented for practice, and real programmes have different credits. Say a student has these five courses:

CourseCreditsGradePointsScore
Course 16A530
Course 28A540
Course 37B+428
Course 46B+424
Course 53B39
Total30131

The GPA is 131 divided by 30, which is 4.3666. Truncated to one decimal, it is 4.3. That falls in the Upper Second band (4.3 to 3.5).

Notice what truncation does. If you rounded 4.3666 to one decimal, you would get 4.4, which is First Class. The UDSM rule says to cut it down, so the answer is 4.3. This is one reason not to trust a calculator or website that rounds.

Do not use a third-party GPA converter for anything official. Your university's own regulations decide.

What are the degree classes and their GPA bands?

The UDSM regulation (12.7) sets the final classification like this:

ClassGPA band
First Class4.4 to 5.0
Upper Second Class3.5 to 4.3
Lower Second Class2.7 to 3.4
Pass2.0 to 2.6

The degree GPA counts the courses that make up the minimum credits for the degree (regulation 12.5). As checked in November 2026, these are the 2024/2025 regulations. A newer prospectus may change something, so always look at the one for your year.

What about honours, supplementary exams and carry-overs?

Three more rules affect many students.

Honours. A degree with honours is given to a candidate with a First or Upper Second Class who passed all examinations at the first sitting, without supplementary exams or carry-overs in any academic year (regulation 12.8).

Moving to the next year. Full-time students must pass at least 120 course credits in the year and reach an overall GPA of at least 2.0 before moving on (regulation 11.1).

Supplementary exams. A student can re-sit failed courses in supplementary examinations if the overall GPA in the first sitting was 1.8 or above. The highest grade possible in a supplementary exam is C (regulation 11.2).

Carry-overs also have a limit. A carried-over course is capped at C, and carried-over courses must be cleared within the maximum registration period, which is five years for a three-year programme and six years for a four-year programme (regulation 11.4).

If a result has gone wrong, speak to your department early. Rules for appeals and special cases are in your own university regulations.

Do other universities use the same scale?

Not always. We only opened the UDSM regulations. Some universities use a different scale or different bands. We have not read their regulations, so we do not quote any other scale. Look for the "Examination Regulations" or "Undergraduate Prospectus" of your own university, and use that.

This also matters when you apply for further study or a job. They may ask for your class, and they will read it using the rules of the university that gave the degree.

A second example: what one grade can change

Using the same five courses, imagine Course 3 (7 credits) had been a B instead of a B+. Its score would be 21 instead of 28, so the total score would be 124. Then 124 divided by 30 is 4.1333, which truncates to 4.1. The class stays Upper Second, but the number moves by 0.2.

This shows why big-credit courses deserve more study time. A weaker grade in a 7-credit course costs more than the same grade in a 3-credit course. It is also a reminder that one bad result does not decide a degree, since many courses add up.

Common questions

Is the GPA rounded or truncated?

At UDSM it is truncated down to one decimal.

What GPA do I need to pass a year?

Under regulation 11.1, an overall GPA of at least 2.0 and 120 credits passed.

Can I get honours with a supplementary?

Not under UDSM regulation 12.8. Honours need passes at the first sitting.

Which class is a GPA of 3.4?

Lower Second at UDSM, since the band is 2.7 to 3.4.

What to do next

  1. Find your university's prospectus or examination regulations and read the grading section.
  2. Write your own courses, credits and grades in a table and calculate your GPA with the steps above.
  3. If your GPA is below your goal, talk to a lecturer or adviser about which courses to focus on. Our tips for studying effectively can help.
  4. Keep your results slips safe, and see how to avoid plagiarism in university assignments so a mark is never put at risk.

A GPA is a number, and numbers can move. Every course you take from now on is a chance to move yours.
