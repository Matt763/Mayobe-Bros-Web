Zanzibar's Slave Trade: How It Worked and How It Ended

How Zanzibar became a main slave-trading port in East Africa, what the 1873 treaty changed, why slavery lasted until 1897, and what Stone Town remembers.

This is a hard part of Tanzanian history. Men, women and children were taken from their homes, marched or shipped to Zanzibar, and sold. This post tells how that trade worked and how it slowly ended, using the sources we could open, and it leaves out graphic detail. The people at the centre of it were not numbers. They were people with families and lives of their own.

The short answer

Zanzibar was one of the main slave-trading ports in East Africa in the 1800s. Slaves from the mainland were sold in its market and worked on its clove and coconut plantations. A treaty signed on 5 June 1873 ordered the public slave markets closed, but it did not end slavery. The Sultan's decree of 6 April 1897 abolished it in Zanzibar, and even then freedom was slow and hard to claim.

  • UNESCO calls Zanzibar one of the main slave-trading ports in East Africa and says Stone Town is the place where the trade was finally ended.
  • Cloves, grown on plantations from the 1830s, increased the demand for forced labour.
  • The 1873 treaty closed public markets for buying and selling imported slaves, but people already enslaved stayed enslaved.
  • Britain took formal control of Zanzibar's administration in 1890.
  • The decree of 1897 ended slavery by law. Only about 10% of enslaved people had completed the paperwork in the first year, according to one summary.

Why Zanzibar? Cloves, cargo and the Indian Ocean

Zanzibar had traded across the Indian Ocean for centuries. Its main exports from the mainland, brought by caravans from the interior, were ivory and slaves, according to the EBSCO research summary on the abolition of slavery.

In the 1800s the island's rulers came from Oman. Sultan Said ibn Sultan moved his seat of government from Muscat to Zanzibar in 1840. The EBSCO biography says he brought clove growing to the island, where soil and weather suited it, and that cloves were in demand in Europe and North America. Plantations of cloves and coconuts spread over Unguja and Pemba, and they needed many workers.

The same biography says Said signed treaties with Britain in 1822 and 1845 aimed at limiting the slave trade, but that the 1845 treaty left loopholes. The article's judgement is sharp: it calls him "essentially a merchant prince" who "prolonged the slave trade to serve his commercial needs". That is the article's reading, and other historians may weigh it differently.

How the trade worked, from the mainland to the island market

The trade began far from the coast. According to the EBSCO summary, caravans carried ivory and enslaved people from the interior to the coast, and the plantations on Zanzibar and Pemba relied on enslaved workers bought from the mainland.

We deliberately do not quote any figure for how many people were taken each year. The numbers in popular articles and travel pages vary widely, and none of the pages we opened gave a count that we could trace to a solid study. We can say that it was large enough to shape the island's economy and its population.

One estimate is worth giving with a warning. EBSCO reports that in 1895 a British official estimated that three-quarters of Zanzibar's 200,000 inhabitants were slaves. It was an official's estimate, not a census, and it shows how much of island life rested on forced labour.

We do not blame any one group of people. What the sources show is that the system involved rulers, merchants and plantation owners, and that it was ended step by step, through treaties, a protectorate and a decree.

1873: the treaty that closed the open market

In 1873 Britain pressed Sultan Barghash to sign a treaty. The text was printed in the United States government's Foreign Relations of the United States series, which gives the date as 5 June 1873. It was signed by John Kirk, the British Political Agent in Zanzibar, and by Nasir bin Said on behalf of the Sultan. The Sultan later ratified it with his own signature.

What did it say? According to the text:

  1. Article I: the export of slaves from the Sultan's mainland coast, whether to other parts of his lands or abroad, "shall entirely cease".
  2. Article II: public markets in the Sultan's dominions for buying and selling imported slaves "shall be entirely closed".
  3. Article III: the Sultan would protect freed slaves and punish anyone who harmed them or tried to enslave them again.
  4. Article IV: Britain would stop people from Indian states under British protection from owning slaves or getting new ones.

What it did not say matters just as much. It did not abolish slavery. The EBSCO summary says that slaves already on the island and their children stayed legally enslaved, that enforcement was hard, and that the number of enslaved people in Zanzibar kept growing afterwards. Sources do not agree on how hard the Sultan was pressed to sign, so we do not describe that part.

After the market closed, the Universities' Mission to Central Africa took over the site. An Anglican cathedral was built there in 1876, according to EBSCO. UNESCO says Christ Church Anglican Cathedral stands on the site of the last slave market and commemorates the work of David Livingstone in ending the slave trade. A church on that ground is a memorial. It is not the whole story, and we do not treat any one European as the hero of it.

Abolition was slow: 1890, 1897 and after

Britain's grip grew. Encyclopedia.com says that British consuls-general held an informal protectorate over Zanzibar, which became formal in 1890 when Britain took responsibility for administering the islands on the Sultan's behalf.

The law that finally abolished slavery came in 1897. EBSCO says that Sultan Hamoud ibn Muhammad issued the decree on 6 April 1897. Britain had installed him after British warships bombarded the palace in 1896 and removed a rival who had proclaimed himself Sultan. The Sultan's own wishes are hard to know. British officials were divided, and anti-slavery campaigners in Britain, including members of the Society of Friends, pressed for action.

The decree gave compensation to the owners of legally held slaves. To become free, a person had to apply and prove they had a home and a way to support themselves. Vagrancy laws limited the freedom of those who were released. According to the EBSCO summary, only about 10% of enslaved people had completed the paperwork in the first year, and many others stayed on plantations on negotiated terms.

The decree did not cover the British-controlled coastal mainland, where abolition efforts continued for roughly another decade. After abolition, cloves replaced the slave trade as the main source of the island's income, as Encyclopedia.com puts it.

Remembering today: Christ Church and Stone Town

Stone Town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In its list of what makes the town special, UNESCO names the Christ Church cathedral as built on the site of the last slave market, and also names the residence of Tippu Tip, a slave trader. Both buildings, side by side in the same town, are part of why UNESCO says Zanzibar has "great symbolic importance in the suppression of slavery".

If you visit, go to learn, not just to take photographs.

Why it still matters

It matters because Zanzibar is a place where this history is still visible, and UNESCO counts it among the reasons Stone Town is a World Heritage Site. The way abolition happened also shows how slowly rights can arrive: a treaty in 1873, a protectorate in 1890, a decree in 1897, and about a decade more on the mainland coast. Each step was real, and none was enough alone.

For readers who study history at school, this topic ties together trade, empire, economy and human rights in one place. It also reminds us to be careful with numbers and with blame. Where the sources do not agree, we say so. Where they are silent, so are we.

Common questions

Did the 1873 treaty end slavery in Zanzibar?

No. It ended the open public markets for imported slaves and ordered an end to exports from the mainland coast. People already enslaved remained enslaved. Slavery was abolished by law in 1897.

Who was Sultan Barghash?

He was the Sultan of Zanzibar in 1873. His representative signed the treaty and he ratified it later. Beyond that, the sources we opened say little about him, so we do not add details we could not confirm.

How many people were sold through Zanzibar?

We could not find a reliable figure we could trace to a solid study, so we do not give one. Be careful with round numbers in popular articles.

Where was the slave market?

UNESCO says Christ Church Anglican Cathedral in Stone Town stands on the site of the last slave market.

What to do next

  1. Read the UNESCO entry for Stone Town to see how the site is described and protected.
  2. If you plan to visit Stone Town, ask your guide about the history of the cathedral site and listen carefully.
  3. Read about another turning point in the region in our post on the Tanganyika and Zanzibar union, and about the Swahili coast's older trade in Kilwa Kisiwani.

Remembering the whole story, including the painful parts, is a way of respecting the people who lived it.
