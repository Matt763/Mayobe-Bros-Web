Who Built Great Zimbabwe? The Myths and the Facts

Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona between about 1100 and 1450. See what the evidence shows and why the Sheba, Ophir and Phoenician stories fail.

Great Zimbabwe is a large stone city in the south of Zimbabwe, near the town of Masvingo. For more than a century people argued about who built it. The answer is now clear, and it is worth knowing, because the wrong answers were used to serve political ends.

The short answer

Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona people, a Bantu-speaking people, over several centuries. UNESCO says the property was built between 1100 and 1450 AD. The stories that credited the Queen of Sheba, the biblical land of Ophir or Phoenician traders have no support in the archaeology.

  • The site was founded in the 11th century, according to UNESCO, and was a major trading city by the 14th.
  • Radiocarbon dating and excavation point to African builders in the Middle Ages.
  • Gold was the most valuable trade good. Beads, porcelain and coins from far away were found there.
  • Sources differ on exact dates and on how many people lived there, so we give ranges.
  • The myths grew in the 1800s and early 1900s, when many Europeans did not believe Africans could have built such a place.

What is Great Zimbabwe?

It is a group of stone ruins about 30 km from Masvingo, at roughly 1,100 m above sea level. UNESCO divides it into three parts: the Hill Ruins, the Great Enclosure and the Valley Ruins.

The Hill Ruins sit on a granite hill and were lived in from the 11th to the 15th centuries. UNESCO says the east enclosure there is thought to have had a ritual purpose, because six upright soapstone posts topped with birds were found in it. The Great Enclosure is oval, dates to the 14th century and was built of cut granite blocks laid in regular rows. It is the huge curved wall you see in most photos. The Valley Ruins are separate groups of houses, which UNESCO dates to the 19th century.

UNESCO describes the walls as dry-stone masonry, meaning the stones are fitted together without mortar.

Who built it, and when? What the evidence says

UNESCO says "scientific research has proved" that Great Zimbabwe was founded in the 11th century by a Bantu population of the Iron Age, the Shona. Encyclopedia.com says the site's earliest structures date to around the year 1000. It names the Gokomere, Bantu-speaking farmers and cattle keepers, as the ancestors of the Shona.

None of this means one source is wrong. Archaeologists work from fragments, and numbers about old populations are estimates. The safest answer is "between about 1000 and 1500, with the main building from the 11th to the 15th century".

How was the age worked out?

The WorldHistory.org article explains a key step. In 1958 Keith Robinson began radiocarbon dating wood poles from an excavation. The wood came from a tree cut down between 915 and 1215 CE. That pointed to a medieval date, not an ancient one. Later scholars' radiocarbon samples suggested that most of the buildings date to between 1300 and 1450 CE. A dated piece of wood shows when a tree died, not exactly when a wall went up, so archaeologists use many samples.

A trade city linked to the coast

Great Zimbabwe was the centre of a state that spread over what UNESCO calls the gold-rich plateaux. Gold was its most valuable trade good, says Encyclopedia.com, and the state also mined copper and traded ivory.

Digs have found glass beads, porcelain from China and Persia, and gold and Arab coins from Kilwa. UNESCO says these show long trade with the outside world. Kilwa was on the coast of what is now Tanzania. You can read about it in our post on Kilwa Kisiwani and the Swahili trade. Coins from one are found in the other. That is a real, documented link between the coast of present-day Tanzania and Zimbabwe.

We do not claim more than that. The finds show trade. They do not show that the coastal cities designed or built the stone walls.

The myths: Sheba, Ophir and the Phoenicians

The WorldHistory.org article names the main European claims.

  • Karl Mauch was a German explorer who reached the ruins in 1871 while looking for gold. He said the ruins were Ophir, the palace of the Queen of Sheba, and suggested that cedar beams there came from Lebanon.
  • Theodore Bent led an expedition in 1891. He decided the Phoenicians built it, partly because the soapstone birds looked to him like Near Eastern carvings.
  • Richard Hall was hired by Cecil Rhodes in 1902 to look after the site. He described the builders as "more civilized races". The article says his clearing removed deposits up to two metres deep and destroyed much evidence of African origin.

Encyclopedia.com adds that the "Solomon's mines" story about the gold was probably used to attract European settlers. Solomon, it notes, lived about a thousand years before the site was first settled. It also says that, after Rhodes's company arrived in the 1890s, writers proposed that Phoenicians or coastal Arab traders were the builders, and links this to a Eurocentric view that Africans could not have built an organised state.

You may also read that UNESCO still says the ruins are the capital of the Queen of Sheba. UNESCO's short description does mention it, but as a legend: "according to an age-old legend". Its fuller text says the city struck the imagination of travellers and "persistent legends" gave it a biblical origin. UNESCO does not claim it is true.

How archaeologists corrected the story

Early 20th-century archaeologists began to challenge the myths. According to WorldAtlas, Randall-MacIver excavated in 1905 and 1906 and found objects he said were of Bantu origin, although not everyone accepted his claims at the time. Gertrude Caton-Thompson ran a 12-day excavation in 1929 and confirmed many of his findings, including that Bantu people built the city.

The WorldHistory.org article says she used stratigraphy (reading the layers of soil) and an aircraft survey, and concluded in her book Zimbabwe Culture that the site was medieval and African, and that foreign objects there showed trade. Robinson's radiocarbon dates in 1958 then supported her medieval dating.

Most researchers today think the Gokomere were the builders, as ancestors of today's Shona, according to WorldAtlas. In plain words, the work of decades settled it.

Why was it abandoned?

UNESCO says that about 1450 the capital was left because the surrounding land could no longer feed an overpopulated city and because of deforestation. Encyclopedia.com lists overpopulation, drought and the loss of gold in riverbeds as suggested causes. The people did not vanish. UNESCO says the migration benefited Khami, which became the most influential city of the region.

Why it matters today

The WorldHistory.org article notes that Southern Rhodesia became Rhodesia, that it broke from British rule in 1965 under Ian Smith, and that Zimbabwe adopted its present name in 1980. It also says that in the Smith years tourist guides presented the site as the work of white "visionaries". UNESCO says the Zimbabwean nation identifies with the site and has adopted the soapstone bird as its emblem. That bird may have been a royal totem.

For a student the lesson is simple. A claim is not true because it is told with confidence, or because it is printed in a book. Evidence, dated and checked by different people, is what finally decided this case.

Common questions

Who built Great Zimbabwe?

The ancestors of the Shona, Bantu-speaking Africans, according to UNESCO and the other sources we read.

When was it built?

UNESCO says between 1100 and 1450 AD. Encyclopedia.com puts the earliest structures around 1000. Use "about 1000 to 1450" if you need a short range.

Was Great Zimbabwe built by the Queen of Sheba?

No. That was a 19th-century European guess with no evidence. Even the date does not fit: the ruins were built long after the time of the biblical Sheba.

Is there a link between Great Zimbabwe and Tanzania?

Yes, one proven link: gold and Arab coins from Kilwa were found at the site, according to UNESCO.

What to do next

  1. Look at UNESCO's page for Great Zimbabwe and read the three sections on the Hill Ruins, the Great Enclosure and the Valley Ruins.
  2. Read our post on Kilwa Kisiwani to see the other end of the trade.
  3. When you meet a "lost civilisation" story online, ask who dated it, how, and whether independent teams agree.

Good history asks questions, and it is willing to change its answer when the evidence does.
