What Caused World War I? The Causes and the Tanzania Link

The long-term causes of World War I, the shot in Sarajevo, the weeks that followed, and how the war reached German East Africa and changed Tanganyika.

Many students learn the causes of World War I as a list to memorise. This post explains them in plain words, one step at a time. It also answers a question that school books often skip: what did this European war mean for the land that is now Tanzania?

The short answer

World War I did not start from one cause. Rivalry between the big powers had been building for years. Then a killing in Sarajevo, on 28 June 1914, set off a chain of decisions that pulled one country after another into war.

  • Europe was split into two alliance blocs, so a fight between two countries could spread.
  • The big powers were building larger armies and navies.
  • They also competed for trade and colonies, including in Africa.
  • Nationalism was strong, especially in the Balkans.
  • Leaders made choices in July 1914 that turned a crisis into a war. Historians still argue about how much blame each country carries.

What were the long-term causes?

Think of these as dry wood piled up for years. None of them made war certain. Together they made it easier for a spark to cause a fire.

Alliances. The big powers had agreements to support each other. By 1914 the main groups were the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary and, later, the Ottoman Empire) and the Allies, also called the Entente (France, Russia and Great Britain). The Canadian War Museum describes how this meant that if one power went to war, others were likely to follow.

Arms races. Major powers built bigger armies and navies. The Canadian War Museum notes that many people at the time described Europe as a powder keg.

Rivalry over trade and land. The museum also points to growing disputes over trade and territory, and unhappiness with the balance of power in Europe. Many of these disputes reached beyond Europe, into Africa and Asia. Our post on the Berlin Conference explains how European powers competed for African land in the decades before the war.

Nationalism in the Balkans. In south-eastern Europe, some groups wanted more power for their own people. History.com says tensions in Bosnia, Serbia and Herzegovina were a danger to the old alliances. Some Serbian nationalists wanted to break up Austria-Hungary.

A careful reader should note one thing. These causes are the usual textbook list, and historians weigh them differently. Some say the alliance system made war more likely. Others say it was less binding than people think, and that decisions made by leaders in 1914 mattered more.

What happened in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914?

Archduke Franz Ferdinand was the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. On 28 June 1914 he visited Sarajevo, in Bosnia, with his wife, Sophie. A young Serbian nationalist, Gavrilo Princip, shot and killed them both, according to History.com.

Austria-Hungary traced the attack to Serbian extremists. Its government believed that people in Serbia had helped. This became the centre of the crisis.

The killing of two people did not make a world war by itself. What mattered was what the governments decided to do next.

How did one killing become a world war?

Historians call the weeks after Sarajevo the July Crisis. The main steps, in order, were these.

  1. 5 July 1914. Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany secretly promised to support Austria-Hungary if it took action. People often call this the "blank cheque".
  2. Late July. Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum, a list of demands with a deadline. History.com describes its terms as nearly impossible to accept.
  3. 28 July. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.
  4. End of July. Russia, which backed Serbia, mobilised its army.
  5. Early August. Germany moved against France. German troops entered neutral Belgium on 3 or 4 August 1914. Our sources give both dates, so we give both. The Canadian War Museum says Belgium had been neutral for nearly a century.
  6. 4 August. Britain declared war on Germany.

Within about a week of the Austrian declaration, Russia, Belgium, France, Great Britain and Serbia stood against Austria-Hungary and Germany. Italy, first allied with the Central Powers, joined the Allies in May 1915.

Notice how many moments there were when leaders could have chosen differently. That is why many historians say the war was not unavoidable.

Why did a European war reach Africa?

Britain, France, Germany and Belgium all ruled colonies in Africa. When they went to war, the war followed them there. German East Africa, which covered most of today's mainland Tanzania, plus Rwanda and Burundi, was one of the places where fighting lasted the whole war.

The colonies mattered for ports, railways, resources and as a way to pressure the enemy. WorldHistory.org says that in August 1914 Allied warships bombarded the ports of Dar es Salaam and Tanga, which the Allies wanted to deny to German warships.

What happened in German East Africa?

In November 1914 a British-Indian force of about 8,000 men tried to take Tanga. A German colonial army led by Lieutenant Colonel Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck defeated it, according to WorldHistory.org.

After that, Lettow-Vorbeck avoided big battles. He used guerrilla tactics and kept many Allied soldiers busy for the rest of the war. His force moved through a wide area, even into Northern Rhodesia.

He did not give up when the war ended in Europe. He surrendered after the armistice of November 1918. Other accounts we read place this in late November at Abercorn, in what is now Zambia.

Most of the soldiers on both sides were Africans. WorldHistory.org says the German force never had more than about 15,000 men, at least 11,000 of them African. It says the Allied force was about 100,000, and that about 250,000 porters and labourers were taken from neighbouring Congo. Numbers like these come from one source, so treat them as rough.

What did the war cost the people of the region?

The cost fell heavily on ordinary Africans, many of whom had no say in the war.

Armies in East Africa depended on carriers, people who carried food, ammunition and equipment on foot. A study in the Journal of African History by G. W. T. Hodges estimates that Britain used more than one million African followers, mostly carriers, from several territories. He estimates that about 10 per cent of them died, and concludes that "over 100,000 must have died". WorldHistory.org gives a similar figure of about 100,000 African deaths from fighting, disease or hunger. These are estimates, and they cover different groups, so they should not be added together.

Hunger followed the armies. A scholarly article on the Mtunya famine in Dodoma, in central Tanzania, says that German and British demands for men, cattle and food left the dry region short of everything. The famine lasted from 1917 to 1920. The abstract says that about 30,000 of the region's roughly 150,000 people died.

We could not confirm a single reliable figure for all civilian deaths in East Africa, so we do not give one.

How did the war end, and what changed for Tanganyika?

Germany asked for an armistice on 11 November 1918. History.com says Austria-Hungary had agreed to its own armistice on 4 November, and the Ottoman Empire had signed with the Allies in late October.

Total deaths in the war are given in different ways. History.com says more than 9 million soldiers died, and that civilian deaths came close to 10 million. Its introduction says more than 16 million people died in all. Because estimates differ, treat the totals as ranges and not as exact counts.

For East Africa, the peace brought a new ruler. Under the 1919 Treaty of Versailles, WorldHistory.org says, German East Africa was placed under a British mandate and renamed Tanganyika Territory. Belgium received Ruanda-Urundi. The League of Nations told the new rulers to govern in a way that promoted the welfare of the people. How well they did that is a bigger story. You can follow it in how Tanganyika won independence.

For the German colonial period before the war, see the Maji Maji war.

Common questions

Who started World War I?

There is no agreed single answer. Austria-Hungary declared the first war, and Germany had promised it support. Russia, France, Britain and Serbia also made choices. Historians weigh these actions differently, so be careful with anyone who blames only one country as settled fact.

Was the assassination the real cause?

It was the spark. Without the long-term tensions, the killing might have stayed a local crisis. Without the killing, the war might have come later, or not at all. Historians cannot prove either point.

Did World War I really happen in Tanzania?

Yes. Fighting took place across German East Africa from 1914 until the German surrender in late 1918, and the region became British-ruled Tanganyika afterwards.

How many people died?

Nobody knows the exact figure. Estimates for the whole war range from about 9 million soldiers to more than 16 million people, soldiers and civilians together. For East Africa the estimates are also uncertain.

What to do next

  1. Write the six steps of the July Crisis in your own words, with dates, to learn them for an exam.
  2. Read the Hodges abstract and the Mtunya abstract in the sources below to see how historians work with uncertain numbers.
  3. Read about Tanganyika's path to independence to see what came after the mandate.

History asks us to remember the people behind the numbers, including the carriers who never came home. Learning their story carefully is one way to respect them.
