No Job Since Graduating? Skills and Volunteering That Count

Still waiting for work after graduation? Learn which skills employers look for, how to show them with proof, and how to use volunteering safely.

You finished your studies, you gave the exams everything you had, and now the phone does not ring. Friends ask what you are doing. Some days you do not want to answer. If that is where you are, this post is for you.

The short answer

While you wait, build proof that you can do the work. Employers read a CV to find out what you can do, not only what you studied. Evidence of that can come from a small project, a few hours of volunteering a week, or a skill you practise in public.

  • Pick two or three skills employers ask for and practise them on purpose.
  • Turn each skill into one short example: what you did, how you did it, what came out of it.
  • Volunteer only where you are never asked to pay and the hours are fair.
  • Follow a weekly plan so the waiting time adds up to something.
  • Protect your money and your mind. Both matter more than any single application.

Waiting is hard, and it is not a verdict on you

Waiting after graduation can feel like standing at a bus stop while every bus goes past full. You did the hard part. You studied, often with family support and sacrifice, and it is natural to feel ashamed or tired when the next step is slow.

Here is something plain and true: a gap in your CV does not mean you have no value. Employers know that finding a first job takes time. What helps you most is what you do with the weeks, not how fast the first offer comes.

Be kind to yourself, and also be practical. Both can be true on the same day.

Which skills do employers say they want?

No single list covers every job, but the same ideas come up again and again.

A 2025 study by three researchers at the University of Dodoma (Mkwizu, Tenge and Makalanga), reported by The Citizen, looked at the match between what graduates know and what the labour market needs. It found a significant gap, especially in soft skills such as communication, teamwork, adaptability and problem solving. It also said graduates with stronger people skills were more likely to get jobs. The report names internships and apprenticeships as useful ways to gain real workplace exposure. The article gives no figures, and neither do we.

Soft skills are the habits that shape how you work with other people. Outside Tanzania, the careers service Prospects (UK) lists these as common ones employers value: communication, resilience, adaptability, planning, and the ability to lead or manage yourself. The University of Washington careers office (US) names eight "career readiness" areas. Several of them match what you can practise at home:

  • Communication: speaking, writing and listening clearly.
  • Critical thinking: looking at information and solving a problem.
  • Teamwork: listening, owning your part and handling disagreement fairly.
  • Professionalism: being dependable, prepared and careful with details.
  • Technology: using tools well and learning new ones quickly.

Those are UK and US sources, so treat them as general advice. The idea is the same in Dar es Salaam, Arusha or Mbeya: people want a colleague who turns up, learns fast and works well with others.

How to show a skill with proof

Saying "I am a hard worker" costs nothing, so employers do not believe it much. A short example does the job.

Use this pattern for each skill: what the task was, what you did, and what happened.

For example, say you helped run a school-leavers' meeting in your neighbourhood. You could write:

Organised a meeting for 30 young people in my ward: booked the hall, called each person and prepared a one-page programme. All 30 attended.

(That is an example of the shape, not a real claim. Use only what truly happened to you.)

Here are places to find your own examples:

  • A group project at university, and your part in it.
  • A club, church group, sports team or student society where you had a role.
  • Helping a relative's shop with stock, prices or customers.
  • Tutoring a younger student.
  • A final-year project, and what problem it solved.

Prospects suggests numbers where you honestly have them, because a result is stronger than a duty. "Wrote the posters" is fine. "Wrote posters that filled the hall" is better, if it is true. If you have no number, describe the outcome in plain words.

Make a small thing you can show

Personal projects show initiative, and they give an employer something to look at. Prospects mentions blogs, creative portfolios and research work. A few ideas for your own situation:

  • A neat spreadsheet that tracks a small shop's sales for a month, with your notes on what you learned.
  • A short written report on a local problem, such as how people in your area get clean water, with clear sources.
  • A set of designs, a simple website or a collection of translated texts, depending on your field.

Keep it modest and honest. One finished small project beats five unfinished big ones.

Volunteering: what it gives and what to check first

Volunteering can give you real tasks, people who can speak for you, and something true to write on your CV. Prospects points to non-profit organisations, which often need help and are willing to train volunteers. It also points to university societies, student unions and sports teams as places to build skills and confidence.

For a Tanzanian reader, this might be a school, a youth group, a clinic's community programme, a church or mosque project, or a local non-profit that you know and can visit in person.

Check these things before you say yes:

  1. You never pay. A real volunteer role does not ask for money for a "placement fee", "registration" or "certificate". If someone asks, walk away. Paying for a job or a place is a common scam.
  2. The hours are clear and fair. Ask how many hours a week and for how long. Volunteering should fit around your job search, not replace it.
  3. The tasks are clear. Ask what you will do. Be careful with a "volunteer" role that looks like a full-time job with no pay and no end date. That is unpaid labour, not a stepping stone.
  4. You can visit and meet real people. Go to the place. Ask who you report to.
  5. Your safety is covered. Tell someone at home where you are going. Do not travel alone to an unknown place for a first meeting.
  6. You will get a reference. Ask at the start whether the organisation can give you a short written or spoken reference if you do well.

If something feels wrong, trust that feeling and leave.

A simple weekly plan while you wait

You do not need to be busy every hour. A steady rhythm beats a rush. Here is one plan you can change to suit your life and your data bundle.

WhenWhatTime
MondaySend 3 to 5 tailored applications or enquiries2 hours
TuesdayPractise one skill (for example Excel, writing or a language)1 to 2 hours
WednesdayVolunteer or work on your small project3 to 4 hours
ThursdayAsk one person for advice by message or in person1 hour
FridayUpdate your CV with one new proof point1 hour
WeekendRest, family, exerciseFree

About asking for advice: Prospects says to request an informational chat instead of asking directly for a job. That makes people more comfortable. You could ask a former lecturer, a neighbour who works in your field, or someone from your course who is a few years ahead. Say you would value ten minutes of their advice. Many people like being asked.

Keep a simple notebook. Each week, write one thing you did, one thing you learned and one thing you will try next. After a month, you will have material for your CV and a clear sense that you are moving.

If an internship is your goal, read how to find a real internship and spot a fake one. For help with the bigger picture, our guide on building a career from zero in Tanzania covers choosing a direction and finding a first opportunity.

Look after your money and your mind

Job hunting costs money: transport, bundles, printing. Decide a small weekly limit and keep to it. Apply to roles you can reach and prepare for properly, rather than sending the same CV everywhere.

Never pay anyone for the promise of a job, a "guaranteed interview" or a place on a list. Honest employers do not charge applicants. If someone asks for a fee before you start, treat it as a warning sign.

Your mind needs care too. Sleep, eat, and talk to someone you trust. If sadness or worry feels heavy and does not lift, speak to a health worker, a counsellor or a faith leader you trust. You do not need to carry it alone, and asking for help is a strong thing to do.

Common questions

How long should I volunteer?

There is no fixed answer. A few months with steady weekly hours can give you real examples and a reference. Choose a length you can keep, and check that you are still learning. If you are doing the same easy task with no growth, ask for something new or move on.

Do employers care about volunteering?

Prospects says volunteering is a way to gain experience and show your abilities, and it counts most when you describe it with specific examples. Employers differ, so do not expect it to replace skills your target job needs. Use it alongside your studies and projects.

What if I have no money for courses?

You can build many skills for free: writing, speaking, spreadsheets, teamwork and reliability cost nothing but time. Our post on free online library resources shows one place to start reading. Pick one skill and stay with it for a month before you add another.

Should I take any job just to be working?

That depends on your money and your goals. A job outside your field can still show reliability and give you a reference, and you can keep building toward your target role in your spare time. Make sure the job is real, the pay is agreed and your terms are clear.

What to do next

  1. Pick two skills from this post that match the jobs you want. Write one honest example for each, using the "task, action, result" pattern.
  2. Choose one volunteering place you can visit in person. Ask the six questions in the volunteering section before you agree.
  3. Put the weekly plan into your phone calendar. Start this week with one small task.
  4. Tell one person you trust about your plan, so someone is cheering for you.

The waiting will end. The skills and habits you build now are yours, and they will still be with you at the first desk, shop or site where you are given your chance.
