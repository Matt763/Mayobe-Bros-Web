Rejected for a Job? What to Do Next and How to Keep Going

A calm plan for after a job rejection or a long silence: look after yourself, ask for feedback politely, review your application and try again.

You hoped, you prepared, you went to the interview, and then came the "no", or worse, no answer at all. If you are reading this with a heavy chest, you are in good company. This post will not pretend it does not hurt. It will give you a plan for the next few days and weeks.

The short answer

Give yourself a short time to feel disappointed, then take small steps. Look after your body and mind, thank the employer politely, ask for feedback if you wish, review your application and interview, and apply again. Do not expect that feedback will come, and do not take one "no" as a verdict on you.

  • Rest first. You do not need to be fine today.
  • Talk to one person you trust.
  • Send a short, polite message. Ask for feedback, but do not demand it.
  • Look again at your CV, your cover letter and how the interview went.
  • Keep a simple weekly routine of applying and practising.

It hurts, and that is normal

Say you were interviewed last week. You told your family. You imagined the first salary, the first day, the uniform or the office desk. Then the message came, or the days passed in silence. It is normal to feel sad, angry, embarrassed or numb. It is also normal to wonder if you are not good enough.

A rejection says that this employer chose someone else for this job at this time. It does not tell you your worth. Often many people apply for one post, and the choice can come down to small things you cannot see.

You may feel tempted to hide it from others. Try not to. Carrying it alone makes it heavier.

The first 48 hours: what to do and what not to do

The NHS Every Mind Matters page on stress offers simple tips that fit this moment: talk to someone, break big tasks into smaller parts, stay active, and pause to breathe. Here is how that can look.

Do:

  • Talk to a friend, a relative or a lecturer you trust. Say it plainly: "I did not get the job, and I feel low."
  • Walk, stretch or play sport. Movement helps burn off nervous energy.
  • Breathe slowly for a few minutes when your thoughts race.
  • Eat, drink water and sleep. These matter more than they seem.
  • Write down one thing you did well in the process, even if it is small, such as getting the interview.

Do not:

  • Send an angry message to the employer. It cannot help you and they may remember it.
  • Delete your CV or stop applying in a rush.
  • Compare yourself with classmates who got jobs. You do not know their whole story.
  • Spend money you do not have on "guaranteed jobs" or agents who ask for fees. See our post on fake job offers if someone offers a shortcut.

How to ask for feedback politely

Feedback can teach you a lot, but you cannot demand it. Some employers reply with a line or two, and some do not reply at all. Colorado College's career centre advises being gracious and patient in any follow-up, and keeping requests polite and measured.

Wait a few days after the decision, so you write calmly. Keep the message short. Here is an example you can adapt.

Subject: Thank you, and a small request

Dear [name],

Thank you for considering me for the [job title] position and for the time you gave me at the interview. I am disappointed, but I am grateful for the chance. If you have a moment, I would be thankful for any short feedback that could help me improve for the future. I understand if that is not possible.

Kind regards, Your name

Send it once. If you get no reply, let it go and do not send a second or third message. Whatever happens, you have shown good manners, and that is worth something.

Review your CV, letter and interview

Once you feel steadier, look at the whole process as a coach would, with no blame. Use these questions.

  1. The advert. Did you meet the main requirements? Did your CV show that clearly in the first half page?
  2. Your CV and letter. Is each page neat, free of spelling mistakes and focused on this job? Did you use the same words the advert used, where they are true for you?
  3. The interview. Write down the questions you remember and how you answered. Which ones felt weak?

Prospects, the UK graduate careers service, lists common interview mistakes and ways to fix them. They include being unprepared, dressing in a way that does not suit the workplace, talking too much or too little, criticising past employers, and not asking questions. Compare that list with your own interview, and choose one or two things to change.

For help with the next attempt, see our posts on job interview questions and answers and writing a CV.

Then practise. Ask a friend to ask you three questions and give honest comments. Fix one thing at a time.

What to do when you hear nothing

Silence is hard, because you do not even get the clarity of a "no". Colorado College's career centre says that employers may take longer than they first said, and that no news does not necessarily mean bad news.

Here is a calm way to handle it:

  • Check the date they gave you, if any, and wait until it has passed.
  • If you were given no date, many career advisers suggest waiting a week or two, though this is common practice and not a rule.
  • Send one short, polite message asking about the timeline. Restate your interest in the role.
  • After that, keep applying elsewhere. Do not stop your search while you wait.

If you still hear nothing, treat it as a no, so that your mind can rest, and move on. If they do reply later, that is a bonus.

Keep a simple weekly routine

Job hunting feels better when it has a shape. NHS advice on stress suggests breaking big tasks into small ones and giving yourself credit when you finish each. You could set a weekly plan like this:

DayOne small task
MondayFind and read two or three adverts that fit you
TuesdayTailor your CV for one of them
WednesdaySend one application or one direct message to an organisation
ThursdayPractise two interview answers out loud
FridayLearn one small skill, or do some volunteering
WeekendRest, walk, see people

If you have no job yet and want ideas on skills and volunteering that employers value, read no job since graduating.

Common questions

Should I ask why I was not chosen?

You can ask politely, once, but the employer does not have to answer. Do not argue with the decision.

How many rejections are normal?

There is no standard number. We do not have reliable figures for Tanzania, and we will not invent any. What matters is to keep improving and keep trying.

Should I stop applying while I wait?

No. Keep applying while you wait for a reply. It also takes some pressure off any one result.

When should I get help for how I feel?

If sadness, worry or stress is affecting your sleep, eating, studies or daily life for more than a short time, talk to someone you trust and to a health worker. The NHS page says the same: seek help if stress affects daily life. If you ever feel unable to cope or fear for your safety, ask for urgent help from a person near you or a health facility.

What to do next

  1. Today, tell one trusted person how you feel.
  2. This week, send the short thank-you and feedback message, if you wish.
  3. Write down two things to improve in your CV or interview, and fix them.
  4. Choose three new places to apply, and begin on Monday.

A rejection closes one door and tells you nothing about the rest. You are still learning how to be found, and that skill improves with every try.
