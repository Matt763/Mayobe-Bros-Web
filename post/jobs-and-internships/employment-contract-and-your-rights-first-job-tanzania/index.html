Your First Job Contract in Tanzania: What to Check Before You Sign

What your employer must put in writing, plus hours, leave, notice and pay deductions under Tanzania's employment law, with a note on the 2025 changes.

A job offer is exciting, and it is easy to sign the first paper you are given. Take a little time first. A contract is the written promise about your work, your pay and your rights, and you should understand it before you put your name on it.

This post is general information, not legal advice. It explains the main points of Tanzania's Employment and Labour Relations Act, 2004. That Act was amended in 2025, so some details may now be different. Always check the current text with the Ministry or a qualified person before you rely on any figure here.

The short answer

Your employer must give you the main terms of the job in writing when you start, and explain them if you do not understand. The Act also sets minimum standards for working hours, leave, notice and pay deductions. Read your contract against these points, and ask questions before you sign.

  • Check the job title, place of work, start date, hours and pay are written down.
  • Check the type and length of contract (permanent, fixed term or for one task).
  • Look at probation, notice and leave terms.
  • Ask about any deduction from your pay before you agree.
  • Keep a signed copy.

Unless we say otherwise, the figures below come from the original 2004 Act, as checked in November 2026. The amendments may have changed some points. Section numbers are from that original text. The Act does not apply to every kind of worker in the same way. For example, section 2 of the original text excludes some services and lets the Minister decide on categories of staff in them, and some parts of the Act do not apply to certain groups.

What your employer must give you in writing

Section 15 says that when you start work, your employer must give you a written statement of the main particulars. These include:

  • Your name, age, permanent address and sex.
  • The place where you were recruited.
  • A description of the job.
  • The date you start.
  • The form and length of the contract.
  • The place of work.
  • Your hours of work.
  • Your pay, how it is worked out, and any benefits or payments in kind.

If all of this is in a written contract that you were given, the contract can serve as the statement. If you do not understand the written particulars, your employer must make sure they are explained in a way you understand. If any of these items changes, your employer must talk to you, update the particulars and tell you in writing.

The employer must keep these records for five years after your employment ends. In any court case, if the employer cannot show the written contract or particulars, it is the employer who has the burden of proving what the terms were. The section does not apply to a person who works fewer than six days in a month for an employer.

A written contract helps you most of all. If a promise is only spoken, such as a salary, a transport allowance or a start date, ask for it in the paper.

Hours, overtime and leave

Hours. Section 19 sets the maximum ordinary hours: six days a week, 45 hours a week and nine hours a day. An employer may not require or permit you to work more than 12 hours in any day.

Overtime. You may only be asked to do overtime under an agreement, and not more than 50 overtime hours in a four-week cycle. Overtime must be paid at not less than one and a half times your basic wage. Basic wage does not include allowances.

Annual leave. Section 31 says you are entitled to at least 28 consecutive days of leave for each leave cycle. The leave includes any public holiday that falls inside it. A leave cycle for annual leave is 12 months of continuous employment.

Less than six months. Section 29 says that an employee with less than six months of service is not entitled to paid leave under this part of the Act. There are exceptions, for example for seasonal workers.

Sick leave. Section 32 gives at least 126 days of sick leave in a leave cycle: the first 63 days at full pay and the next 63 days at half pay. Your employer need not pay if you cannot produce a medical certificate, or if you are entitled to paid sick leave under another law, fund or collective agreement.

Other kinds of leave, such as maternity and paternity leave, are in the same Part of the Act. The 2025 amendments changed some of these rules, so check them on the Ministry website.

Probation and the six-month rule

Many first jobs begin with a probation period. A Tanzanian law firm article from March 2024 explains that, under the Code of Good Practice, probation should not be longer than 12 months. It also says that probation ending does not automatically make you a confirmed employee. A confirmation letter is generally needed under the contract.

The same article says employees with six months or less of service with the same employer are not covered by the unfair termination protections. It also says the way courts treat unconfirmed probationers is debated, and that a probationer may still bring an unfair labour practice complaint to the Commission for Mediation and Arbitration (CMA) in some cases.

The practical advice: ask what the probation length is, what you will be judged on, and who will confirm you. If probation ends and you hear nothing, ask your employer in writing.

Notice, severance and deductions from pay

Notice. Under section 41, if a contract can be ended with notice, the notice must be at least seven days in the first month of employment. After that it is at least 28 days for a monthly paid employee, and four days for a daily or weekly paid one. Notice must be in writing and state the reasons and the date it is given. A longer notice period can be agreed, but it must be the same for both sides.

Severance. Section 42 says that after 12 months of continuous service, an employee whose employment is ended by the employer is generally owed severance pay. It is at least seven days of basic wage for each completed year of service, up to a maximum of ten years. It does not apply to a fair termination for misconduct, and there are other exceptions.

Deductions. Section 28 says an employer may not deduct from your pay unless the law or an agreement allows it, or you agree in writing to a deduction for a debt. To recover loss or damage, among other conditions, the employer must show you in writing the cause, amount and calculation and give you a chance to challenge it. Such deductions may not total more than one quarter of your pay in money. An employer may not make you repay pay except for an earlier overpayment from a calculation error.

Equal treatment. Section 7 forbids an employer from discriminating against an employee on listed grounds, including tribe or place of origin, sex, pregnancy, marital status, disability, HIV/AIDS, age and religion. Harassment is treated as discrimination.

Fixed-term and graduate training contracts

The 2004 text lists three kinds of contract: for an unspecified period, for a specified period for professionals and the managerial cadre, and for a specific task.

A Tanzanian law firm summary dated 4 June 2025 says the 2025 amendments added more fixed-term categories, including fixed-term employment of a graduate to train or gain experience, with training not to exceed 24 months. That is a secondary source, and we did not read the amending Act itself. If you are offered a "graduate trainee" contract, ask exactly how long it lasts, what happens at the end and whether it can lead to a permanent job. Do not assume it will.

The same summary says the amendments changed how compensation for unfair termination is worked out. We do not give those figures here. Check the current Act.

Where to get help

  • Read the Act and related laws on the website of the Prime Minister's Office, Labour, Employment and Relations (kazi.go.tz).
  • Ask the labour office near you or the CMA about your situation.
  • Talk to a lawyer, a legal aid provider or a trade union if you belong to one.
  • Keep copies of your contract, payslips and any messages about pay and conditions.

We do not state a minimum wage here. Minimum pay can be set by sector, so ask the Ministry or your employer which rate applies to your job.

Common questions

Does my employer have to give me a written contract?

The Act requires written particulars when you start work, and a written contract that contains them can serve as that statement. A contract for work outside Tanzania must be in writing. Ask for yours in writing in every case.

Can I be dismissed without notice?

If your contract can be ended with notice, the Act sets minimum notice periods. It also says either side may end employment without notice for a cause recognised by law. If this happens to you, ask for the reason in writing and speak to the labour office or the CMA.

Is there one fixed probation length?

We found none. The law firm article says the Code of Good Practice allows up to 12 months, depending on the job. Check what your contract says.

Does the Act cover everyone?

Not always. Some services and categories are excluded or treated differently. Check whether your kind of work is covered.

What to do next

  1. Ask for the contract before your first day, and read it slowly, with someone you trust.
  2. Mark anything you do not understand and ask for it to be explained.
  3. Check the points above: hours, pay, leave, probation, notice and deductions.
  4. Sign only when you are comfortable, and keep a copy.

Asking questions about your contract is not rude. It is how a careful person starts a new job. Once you start, read our tips for your first week at a new job.
