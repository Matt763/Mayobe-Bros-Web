How to Build a LinkedIn Profile That Helps You Get Noticed

Set up a LinkedIn profile as a student or graduate with little experience: photo, headline, About, skills, Open to Work, and how to stay safe from scams.

A LinkedIn profile is like an online CV that people can find. If you are a student or recent graduate, you may think you have nothing to put on it. You have more than you think, and this post shows you how to build it step by step on a phone or a laptop.

LinkedIn is one tool, not a requirement. Many Tanzanian jobs are found through people, notice boards and direct applications. Use LinkedIn if it helps you, and keep your data costs and your time in balance.

The short answer

Fill in a clear photo, a headline that says what you do, a short About section, your education, any projects or volunteering, and a list of skills that match the jobs you want. Switch on "Open to Work" if you want recruiters to see it, and choose who can see it. Be careful with strangers: never pay for a job, and report posts you doubt.

  • Use a recent, clear photo of your face.
  • Write a headline that says more than "Student".
  • Use the About section to say who you are and what you want.
  • Add studies, projects, volunteering and skills with plain examples.
  • Check any recruiter or job before you reply.
  • Never pay anyone to get a job.

Why have a profile at all?

A profile lets employers and recruiters find you, and lets you check them. It also gives you a place to collect proof of your skills over time.

LinkedIn's own help page on job-search best practice advises adding a recent profile photo, using the Open to Work feature, and researching companies before you apply. The careers site Prospects (UK) also says a well-kept LinkedIn profile matters because many graduates receive job offers through it. We could not confirm figures for Tanzania, so we do not give any. Treat it as one useful option.

Think about cost. LinkedIn works on a phone, but photos and pages use data. Update your profile in one sitting on Wi-Fi if you can, and turn off auto-play of videos in your phone settings.

Photo, headline and About section

These three parts are what people see first.

Photo

Prospects advises a recent, high-quality headshot that suits your field, with a clean background, good light and your face in the middle. It says to avoid casual shots, low-quality or heavily edited images and group photos.

You do not need a studio. Ask a friend to take a photo of you in daylight, in front of a plain wall, in neat clothes. Check that your face is clear and not cut off.

Headline

The headline sits under your name. Prospects says it should say more than a job title: what you do and the value you bring, with words recruiters might search for.

Compare these:

  • Weak: "Student"
  • Better: "Final-year accounting student | Excel and bookkeeping | Seeking internship in Dar es Salaam"

(These are examples of the shape. Write only what is true for you.)

About

Use the About section to expand your headline. Prospects suggests covering who you are, what you do best, your main achievements, and what opportunities you want, ending with a call to action such as inviting people to connect or giving contact details.

Here is a simple pattern for a graduate:

  1. One sentence on what you studied and what field you want.
  2. Two or three sentences on skills you have shown, with an example.
  3. One sentence on what you are looking for.
  4. One sentence on how to reach you.

Write it in the first person, in plain words. Short and honest beats long and fancy.

Education, projects and skills

With little work history, your best material is what you have already done.

Education. Prospects says to list the institution, degree and graduation year, in order. Add a line on subjects or a final-year project if it relates to the job you want.

Projects and volunteering. Add any project, club role, volunteering or part-time help in a shop or family business. For each, write what you did and what came from it. If you have a number you can honestly stand behind, use it. If not, describe the result in words. Prospects suggests tailoring achievements to the audience you want to reach, and says that in visual fields you can add examples like presentations or short videos.

Skills. Prospects suggests looking at job adverts for roles you want, listing the skills they ask for, and choosing the ones you truly have. Include a mix of hard skills (such as software) and soft skills (such as communication and teamwork). Do not list a skill you could not show in an interview.

For help finding examples of skills, see our post on volunteering and skills while you wait for a job.

A custom profile link

A short, clean link looks better on a CV. LinkedIn Help says the custom part must be 3 to 100 characters, with no spaces, symbols or special characters, and it should not include the word "LinkedIn". Links are first come, first served, you can hold one at a time, and you can change it up to five times in six months. LinkedIn recommends using your name or professional brand. On a laptop, you open the Me menu, choose your profile, then edit the Public profile and URL section. Menus change, so follow the current LinkedIn Help page if the steps look different.

Open to Work: who sees it?

Open to Work tells recruiters you are looking. Prospects explains that you can set preferences such as job titles, locations, start date and job types (full-time, part-time, contract or freelance). You then choose the audience:

  • Recruiters only: your status is hidden from people at your current company, and recruiters can still find you through their filters.
  • All LinkedIn members: a green #OpenToWork banner appears on your photo, and everyone can see it.

If you are a student with no employer to hide from, the second option may be fine. If you work somewhere now and do not want your employer to know, choose recruiters only. Think about which suits you, and check the settings from time to time.

Connecting and messaging politely

Connecting is not a numbers game. A few relevant people are worth more than many strangers.

  • Connect with classmates, lecturers, supervisors, people you met at events and people you have worked with.
  • When you invite someone you do not know well, add a short note: who you are and why you would like to connect.
  • Ask for advice, not for a job. Prospects recommends requesting informational chats, which helps people feel comfortable.
  • Keep messages short, polite and specific. Say thank you.
  • Do not send the same copied message to a hundred people.

Example of a short note: "Hello Ms Asha, I am a final-year accounting student at [your university]. I read your post about audit work and would value ten minutes of advice on how to start. Thank you."

(Use a real name and real facts, of course.)

Staying safe from fake recruiters

Fake job offers exist on every platform. Protect your money, your documents and your accounts.

The US Federal Trade Commission, in its job scams guidance, says plainly: "Don't pay for the promise of a job." It lists warning signs including offers of big earnings for little effort, upfront fees for training, certificates or job lists, and unsolicited job offers. Honest employers do not charge applicants. The FTC advises searching the company or recruiter's name with words like "scam" or "complaint", and talking through the offer with someone you trust.

Practical rules:

  1. Never pay for a job, an interview, a visa, "training" or a certificate to get hired.
  2. Check the company outside LinkedIn. Look for an official website, a physical address and a phone number you can call.
  3. Be careful with a private chat. If a "recruiter" quickly moves you to WhatsApp or asks for your ID number, bank details or mobile money PIN, stop.
  4. Never share your passwords, one-time codes, mobile money PIN or copies of ID with someone you have not verified.
  5. Watch for pressure, such as "reply in one hour" or "only three places left".
  6. Report it. LinkedIn says that if a job posting worries you, you can report it. If you have already paid, contact your mobile money provider or bank straight away.

For internships, see how to spot a fake one. Our post on freelancing scams and fees has more on spotting offers that ask for money first.

We could not find reliable official information on how LinkedIn verifies recruiters, so we do not make claims about it. Check each offer yourself.

Common questions

Do I need a LinkedIn profile to get a job in Tanzania?

No. Many jobs are found through direct applications, notices and personal contacts. A profile can help you be found and can show your work, but it is optional.

What if I have no work experience?

Use education, projects, volunteering, clubs and skills. Describe what you did and the result, in plain words. Do not invent anything.

Should I use a photo?

Yes. LinkedIn's help page advises adding a recent profile photo. Pick one that is clear and suitable for work.

Is it safe to turn on Open to Work for everyone?

It is safe for most students, but it makes your status public. If you have a current employer you do not want to know, choose the recruiters-only option. Stay alert to messages from strangers either way.

What to do next

  1. Take a clear, daylight photo and add it to your profile.
  2. Write your headline and a four-sentence About section, and ask a friend to read them.
  3. List three projects or activities and the skills they show, then add ten honest skills.
  4. Learn the scam warning signs above and share them with a friend who is job-hunting.

A profile takes an hour or two to build, and it grows as you do. Start with what is true today, and add to it as you learn.
